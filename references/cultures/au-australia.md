# Australia

## In brief

Australian readers expect friendly, plain writing that treats them as equals: first names, a short opener, the point, and a clear next step. The relaxed tone sits on top of firm expectations about deadlines, accuracy and doing what you said. Anything that sounds superior, pushy or overhyped is discounted fast, so let facts carry the case and keep the voice modest. Australia is more informal and less coded than the UK, but it has its own red lines: respectful terms for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, strict consumer law on claims and prices, and consent rules for marketing email.

## Voice and tone

- Plain English, active voice, short sentences, "you" for the reader and "we" for the organisation. Contractions are fine in most business and government writing; drop them in legal text, reports and formal letters.
- Friendly and egalitarian. Stiff or ornate courtesy ("We humbly request", "Please be informed") creates distance rather than respect.
- Positive and practical. Readers dislike complaining ("whinging"); describe a problem with what you will do about it.
- Use Australian spelling: British-based, checked against an Australian dictionary (colour, organise, centre, licence as a noun, travelling), but "program" for all senses.
- Do not imitate Australian slang ("arvo", "brekkie", "no wuckers") or swear to sound local. Locals tone it down for clients; from an outsider it rings false.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, then surname. Use first names in most business email; mirror the form the other person signs with.
- Openers: "Hi Priya," or "Hello Priya," (standard), "Hi team," (groups), "Dear Ms Walker," (cold formal contact). A bare "Hey" or starting with the question is too casual. Avoid "Dear Sir/Madam"; find a name or use a role ("Dear Hiring Manager").
- Titles take no full stop (Mr, Ms, Dr). "Ms" is the default for women; use "Mx" only when the person asks. Academia and medicine keep "Dr" and "Professor" for first contact.
- Sign-offs: "Kind regards," (safe everywhere), "Thanks," (everyday), "Cheers," (friendly, with people you know).
- Use singular "they" when gender is unknown and neutral job titles (chair, salesperson, flight attendant).
- "Mate" is common among colleagues in speech, but out of place in client or formal writing.
- Many organisations add an Acknowledgement of Country to email signatures: a line recognising the Traditional Custodians of the land where the sender works and paying respect to Elders. Keep it if the source has one; do not invent one for a foreign sender.

## Structuring a message

1. A specific subject line ("Draft contract: two questions before Friday").
2. Greeting and, at most, one friendly line.
3. Purpose and request early, phrased politely ("Could you please confirm..."), with enough context to act.
4. Detail in short paragraphs or bullets; numbers and practical outcomes rather than background.
5. Next step with owner, date and time zone, then thanks and sign-off.

- Write full sentences: chat-style one-liners read as abrupt. Allow two or three working days for a non-urgent reply. After a meeting, a short recap with owners and deadlines is welcome.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Juniors may disagree with seniors, openly and politely. Keep it about the issue: "I'd come at this a bit differently, for two reasons."
- Instructions often come as questions ("Do you think you could have a look at this today?"); they are still instructions.
- Say no clearly and give a reason and an alternative: "I can't take this on this week, but I could start on Monday."
- Bad news goes in plain, active wording, not buried in passive phrasing.
- Admit a mistake quickly, fix it and move on; long apologies feel awkward.
- No hard bargaining, artificial deadlines or repeated chasing within a day. Readers ask whether a message passes the "pub test": would an ordinary person see it as fair?
- Decoding: "no worries" means "you're welcome" or "that's fine", not indifference. "Yeah, nah" in speech means no.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Evidence over adjectives: figures, named clients, guarantees. Avoid sounding superior; standing out by boasting draws criticism (the "tall poppy" reaction). Humour, the underdog and poking fun at pretension work well in consumer ads.
- Under the Australian Consumer Law every claim (price, quality, delivery time, "unlimited", "free", green claims) must be accurate and provable, intended or not. Obvious puffery passes; specific superiority claims and claims about the future need evidence. Comparative ads must be fair. Fake reviews are illegal.
- Prices must show the total, including GST (10%) and unavoidable fees. "Was/now" savings need a genuine earlier price. Advertising and influencer posts must be clearly marked as ads.
- The advertising code bans discrimination and vilification and trivialising disability or mental illness. Mild swearing may pass; strong swear words in full are likely to breach it.
- Marketing email and SMS need consent (the Spam Act), a clear sender and a working unsubscribe honoured within five working days. An email asking for consent is itself spam.
- Resume: two to three pages (more for senior roles), short profile, reverse chronological, quantified achievements, action verbs, keywords from the ad. No photo, date of birth, marital status, religion or nationality; state work rights if relevant. Referees are expected and actually called. Cover letter: one page, specific to the role and organisation.
- Job ads must not suggest discrimination on sex, age, marital status, pregnancy, race, disability, religion and other protected grounds; the employer, the agency and the publisher can all be liable. Avoid "young team", "max 30 years", "mature", "salesman", and celebrating long hours. Inviting First Nations applicants and people with disability is encouraged.

## Humor and emphasis

- Dry, deadpan and self-deprecating humour is everyday, and readers may not flag a joke. In writing for strangers keep it light or leave it out; irony does not travel to readers abroad.
- Teasing is a sign of acceptance among colleagues, but from a newcomer or in writing it can hurt.
- Emphasis comes from understatement. No capitals, "!!" or "????" in email.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **First Nations terms:** use the specific nation or community name where known, otherwise "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples" or "First Nations people". Capitalise Aboriginal, Elders and Country. Never "Aborigines", "ATSI", "half-caste" or "our Indigenous people". Do not call beliefs "myths", languages "extinct" ("sleeping") or joke about "walkabout".
- Do not use ancestral beings or sacred art as motifs without permission. Names and images of First Nations people who have died need care; content often carries a warning.
- **26 January** is a public holiday that many call Invasion Day or Survival Day; brand celebrations draw backlash from both sides. Prefer "colonisation" or "British arrival" to "discovery" or "settlement".
- **Anzac:** the word cannot be used commercially without government permission; Anzac Day (25 April) is solemn. The Coat of Arms is not for commercial use.
- Use "older people" or "older Australians", not "elderly"; do not show older people as helpless.
- Privacy law requires a privacy policy and collection notice when collecting personal data.
- Religion, salary and party politics stay out of early contact. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The Aboriginal Flag (black, red, yellow) and Torres Strait Islander Flag (green, blue) are official flags; use them respectfully, not as decoration.
- Seasons are reversed: Christmas falls in summer and the long holiday runs from mid-December to late January. Drop northern "winter sale in December" or "back to school in September" copy.
- Kangaroos, surfing and "G'day mate" clichés read as tourist copy. Show urban, multicultural Australia; most First Nations people live in cities.
- NAIDOC Week (usually July) and National Reconciliation Week (27 May - 3 June) are key dates for First Nations content.

## Variation within the culture

- **Multicultural readership:** about half of Australians were born overseas or have a parent who was. Many readers do not share Anglo-Australian slang or humour, so plain English serves everyone.
- **Time zones:** Queensland, the Northern Territory and Western Australia do not use daylight saving, so the gap between Brisbane and Sydney changes in summer. Always name the zone.
- **Sectors:** government and legal writing is more formal and slang-free; universities keep titles for first contact; startups, retail and social media are the most casual and playful.
- **States:** anti-discrimination law differs by state on top of federal law; HR copy may need a state check.
- **First Nations communities:** many nations with their own languages and preferences (Koori, Murri, Noongar and others). A community's own terms override general rules.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Australian spelling (colour, organise, centre, program) and day-first dates.
- [ ] Opens with a first name, or "Dear Title Surname" only for cold formal contact; titles without full stops.
- [ ] At most one friendly opener; request and next step appear early, with date and time zone.
- [ ] Requests are polite questions; nothing reads as demanding, chat-style or deferential.
- [ ] No hype words, superlatives about the sender or stacked exclamation marks; claims backed by figures.
- [ ] No slang, swearing or forced "Aussie" jokes and clichés.
- [ ] Refusals give a reason and an alternative; criticism targets the work.
- [ ] No pushy follow-ups, fake urgency or hard bargaining.
- [ ] Consumer prices include GST and fees; "free", "unlimited", green and superiority claims are provable.
- [ ] Marketing email has consent, sender details and a working unsubscribe.
- [ ] First Nations terms are current and capitalised; no "myths", "ATSI" or appropriated sacred imagery.
- [ ] No casual Australia Day celebration and no commercial use of "Anzac" or the Coat of Arms.
- [ ] Seasons and holidays fit the southern hemisphere.
- [ ] Resumes have no photo or personal data; job ads carry no age, gender or other protected-ground wording.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (formal, US-style sales email):

> Dear Sir/Madam, We are thrilled to introduce our industry-leading platform, trusted by the world's best companies! This exclusive offer expires in 24 hours, so act now. Please revert at your earliest convenience.

**After** (tailored for an Australian operations manager):

> Hi Rachel,
>
> I'm Tom from Brightline. We help logistics teams cut manual booking work; a Perth freight company using us has saved about six hours a week since March.
>
> Would a 20-minute call next Tuesday or Wednesday suit? I'm in Melbourne, so any time between 10 am and 3 pm AEDT works.
>
> Kind regards,
> Tom

**Why:** The first name and one-line introduction match the informal, equal footing readers expect. A single local, measurable result replaces the superlatives, which would trigger scepticism, and the fake deadline is gone. The ask is concrete, with a time zone, because the reader may be in another state.
