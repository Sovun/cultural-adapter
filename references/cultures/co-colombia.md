# Colombia

## In brief

Colombian professional readers expect courtesy first: a greeting, a line of goodwill, "usted" and the right title before any request. A plain "no" is rare, so refusals arrive wrapped and a vague answer should never be taken as agreement. Business writing is polite and fairly formal, with sober closings such as "Cordialmente"; the warmth lives in the opening lines, in calls and in chat. Trust grows through people and referrals, and important points are settled by phone or in person, then confirmed by email. Jokes or imagery about drugs, cartels or the armed conflict cause deep offence, and consumer law makes advertised promises binding.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous, respectful and warm, never brusque. A collaborative frame ("¿cómo podemos apoyarlos?") works better than an instruction.
- Plain wording is gaining ground: the state's own clear-language rules favour short sentences and everyday verbs ("usar", "hacer") over "utilizar" or "efectuar". Long legalistic formulas still appear in government and older corporate mail.
- Use Latin American Spanish with Colombian choices where possible: "computador", "celular", "ustedes" (never "vosotros"). Spain forms ("ordenador", "vale", "móvil") stand out.
- Diminutives ("un momentico", "un favorcito") soften speech and chat but look too casual in formal writing. Avoid "ahorita" in commitments: it can mean now or much later.
- Street endearments ("mi reina", "mamita") and crude language never belong in business or service text.
- Sales and support material should be in Spanish; consumer information legally must be.

## Addressing people

- Names: one or two given names, then paternal and maternal surnames (Andrés Felipe Rojas Mejía). Address by the first surname (señor Rojas), never the second alone. Do not assume a married woman changed her name.
- Titles: "Doctor/Doctora" is a broad courtesy title for lawyers, physicians, officials and senior professionals, with or without a doctorate. "Ingeniero/a" is common. When unsure, "señor/señora" plus surname is safe.
- Salutations end with a colon: "Respetado señor Rojas:" (formal), "Estimado Andrés:" (warmer), "Buenos días, Andrés:" (everyday). Find out the reader's gender; "Sr(a)." or "@" forms look careless.
- "Usted" is the safe default. In much of the Andean interior many people use "usted" even with friends and family, so it never sounds cold. "Tú" suits youth brands and close colleagues; "vos" is regional (Antioquia, Valle del Cauca) and out of place in national copy.
- Closings: "Cordialmente," is the standard; "Atentamente," for formal letters; "Saludos," between colleagues. Many writers add "Quedo atento/a a sus comentarios" (I look forward to your comments) before the sign-off.
- Sign with full name, position (cargo) and company. In a group message, greet the most senior person first and be courteous to assistants who manage access.

## Structuring a message

1. Short subject line with the key idea.
2. Salutation and a goodwill line ("Espero que se encuentre muy bien").
3. Context in a sentence or two, then the request, framed as joint work.
4. Next steps with owners and exact dates, and an offer to talk ("Con gusto lo conversamos por teléfono").
5. Pre-closing, "Cordialmente," and signature.

- Formal letters carry city and date ("Bogotá, D.C., 17 de octubre de 2026"), an address block with title, name, position and organisation, and an "Asunto:" line before the salutation.
- Settle sensitive issues by phone or in person, then send a recap. Plain-language guidance asks for the answer in the first paragraph and legal references at the end.
- Since 2022 employers must respect workers' right to disconnect; do not demand replies at night, at weekends or on holidays.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with a reason and an impersonal frame: "Lamentablemente, por ahora no es posible..." beats "No podemos". Formal mail often cushions news with "De manera atenta le informo que...".
- Still make the decision findable; a refusal buried under procedure frustrates readers.
- Decode soft answers: "vamos a ver", "voy a ver qué puedo hacer" and "haré lo posible" are not commitments. Follow up with a concrete question ("¿Le funciona el viernes 24 a las 3:00 p. m.?").
- Criticism is private. Never correct someone in a group thread or widen the CC list on a complaint. Describe the problem and the fix, not the person.
- When adapting outbound text written by Colombians for US or Northern European readers, turn polite intentions into explicit yes, no and dates.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with referrals, known clients, after-sales service, guarantees and payment in instalments. Help your mid-level contact champion the proposal internally.
- Consumer law (Ley 1480 de 2011): ads must be truthful and complete; concrete promises ("entrega en 24 horas") bind the advertiser; promotions must state conditions, dates and stock limits or they stay valid until publicly withdrawn. Prices in pesos with all taxes included; extra charges shown separately. Online and distance sales carry a 5-business-day withdrawal right.
- Data protection (Ley 1581 de 2012): forms need prior, informed authorisation, a stated purpose and the controller's contact; sensitive questions must be marked optional; children's data is restricted.
- Commercial calls, SMS, app messages and emails only Monday to Friday 7:00 a. m. to 7:00 p. m. and Saturday 8:00 a. m. to 3:00 p. m., never Sundays or holidays, with an easy opt-out (Ley 2300 de 2023). Tobacco advertising is banned.
- CV ("hoja de vida"): one to two pages (three for senior roles), a short first-person profile, reverse-chronological experience with quantified achievements, "formación complementaria" for courses, CEFR language levels. A formal photo is common in traditional sectors and often dropped in tech or international applications; the cédula number often appears in local CVs.
- Job ads: no exclusion by race, sex, religion, orientation, disability, nationality or ideology (criminal law covers discrimination); do not require the military service card; avoid age limits and photo requirements. Quote monthly pay in COP. Legal items are pointers, not advice.

## Humor and emphasis

- Light, friendly humour helps in relationship messages and is common in ads; keep teasing and double meanings out of business text.
- Emphasise with bold or italics, not capitals. Opening "¿" and "¡" are mandatory; missing accents look careless.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Drug trade and conflict stereotypes:** no references to cocaine, cartels, Escobar, "Narcos", guerrillas or violence in marketing, small talk or humour, even as a wink. Readers find them deeply offensive.
- **Armed conflict and peace process:** decades of conflict left millions of victims and displaced people, and the 2016 peace agreement remains politically divisive. Take no side and do not use the conflict as a backdrop.
- **Politics, poverty and class:** polarised and personal; avoid comments on inequality, security or negative comparisons with other countries.
- **Hate speech:** discriminatory content is a criminal offence, aggravated through mass media.
- **Ethnic diversity:** Afro-Colombian and Indigenous communities should appear as people, not costume; ethnic languages are co-official in their territories.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Positive pride points: biodiversity, landscapes, coffee regions, music and the national football team. Use them authentically, not as cliché.
- Lilies and marigolds are associated with funerals in gift imagery.
- Public-facing imagery is expected to show women and men and a range of skin tones and regions.

## Variation within the culture

- **Bogotá and the Andean interior:** the most formal in dress and register; "usted" dominates and its speech is the usual national standard. Use it as the default for national copy.
- **Medellín and Antioquia (paisa):** strong tech and startup scene with business-casual norms; "vos" and "usted" in speech, and a reputation for entrepreneurial warmth. Write standard Spanish unless the campaign is deliberately local.
- **Caribbean coast (Barranquilla, Cartagena):** more relaxed and open in tone; "tú" is more common. Avoid caricaturing the accent or "costeño" style.
- **Cali and the Pacific:** strong voseo, large Afro-Colombian population; San Andrés also has an English-based creole.
- **Sectors and age:** banking, insurance, government and healthcare keep formal templates; younger professionals working with US clients write shorter messages with firmer deadlines.
- Regional stereotypes (rolo, paisa, costeño) in copy can offend; keep regional humour to local creatives.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (brisk US-style):

> Hi Camila, the pilot results don't justify a full rollout. We're pausing the project. Let me know if you have questions.

**After** (for a Colombian client contact, in Spanish with gloss):

> Respetada Doctora Restrepo:
>
> Espero que se encuentre muy bien. Gracias a usted y a su equipo por el apoyo durante el piloto. Revisamos los resultados con cuidado y, por ahora, no alcanzan las metas que acordamos para pasar a la implementación completa, así que proponemos hacer una pausa y ajustar el plan juntos. Me gustaría explicarle los detalles por teléfono: ¿le funciona el jueves 22 a las 10:00 a. m.?
>
> Cordialmente,

*(Gloss: "Dear Dr Restrepo, I hope you are very well. Thank you and your team for your support during the pilot. We reviewed the results carefully and, for now, they do not reach the targets we agreed for moving to full rollout, so we propose pausing and adjusting the plan together. I would like to explain the details by phone: does Thursday the 22nd at 10:00 a.m. work for you? Kind regards,")*

**Why:** The decision stays clear, but it is cushioned with thanks, a shared goal and a reason ("las metas que acordamos"), and the pause becomes joint work. The courtesy title, "usted" and a dated call protect the relationship; a Spanish reader would accept a shorter version with "tú".
