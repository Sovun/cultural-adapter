# Denmark

## In brief

Danish readers expect short, plain, friendly text that gets to the point. The address system is among the most informal anywhere: "du" for everyone, first names from the first email, no titles. That informality does not license sloppiness, though: work mail still avoids emoji, slang and strings of exclamation marks. Hype, urgency and self-praise trigger distrust, and marketing claims must be documentable by law. Dry irony runs through Danish working life and is easy to misread in both directions, so outsiders should keep their own irony light and read Danish deadpan as friendly by default.

## Voice and tone

- Clear, concise, close to everyday speech, yet professional. Short words beat long formal ones.
- In Danish copy, swap stiff or archaic words for plain ones: "hvordan" not "hvorledes", "men" not "imidlertid", "alle" not "samtlige", "vores" not "vort/vore".
- Active voice by default. Passive is fine where it avoids blaming the reader ("Filen blev ikke gemt" rather than "Du gemte ikke filen").
- Rewrite English for intent rather than translating sentence by sentence; word-for-word Danish sounds wooden.
- Admit a problem, delay or limitation early and plainly. Honesty builds trust; spin damages it.
- Most professionals read English very well, and B2B work often runs in English. Consumer content still belongs in Danish.

## Addressing people

- "Hej Mette" is the safe default for nearly all business mail, including to people you have never met. Many readers over 30 find a bare "Hej" without a name too casual for first contact.
- "Kære Mette" (dear) splits readers: some find it neutral and polite, others dated or oddly intimate. It is more common among older writers; use it mainly when replying to someone who used it.
- Use "du" (lowercase). The formal "De" (capitalized) survives for royalty and a few service brands; to a business contact it sounds stiff.
- No titles or "herr/fru": "Dear Dr. Jensen" style reads outdated or patronising. Unknown reader: address the team ("Hej kundeservice") or use "Til rette vedkommende" (to whom it may concern), which is a little stiff.
- Name order is given name, surname. Many Danes carry a middle name that looks like a surname (Anne Holm Larsen); only the last one is the legal surname, so copy the full name as the person writes it.
- Sign-offs: "Med venlig hilsen" (standard), "Venlig hilsen", "Mange hilsener" or "Bedste hilsner" (warmer), "Hilsen" (light). Write the closing in full in a first email; "Mvh" strikes some readers as curt. "Kærlig hilsen" is for family.

## Structuring a message

1. A subject line that names the matter.
2. "Hej + name" and at most one friendly line ("Tak for din mail", thanks for your email; "Tak for sidst", thanks for the last meeting).
3. Purpose or request in the first sentences, then reasons, facts and figures in short paragraphs.
4. Who does what by when. In flat organisations decision lines are not visible, so name the decision-maker if it matters.
5. Short close and sign-off.

- Meetings are booked ahead, confirmed in writing and come with an agenda stating the purpose.
- A friendly "sounds good" is not a final decision; Danish teams often consult internally first. Leave time and ask for the next step.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- A clear "nej" with one reason is normal and not rude. Hints are rarely decoded; if something is wrong, say so.
- Disagreement targets the work, not the person, and is expected from juniors too. Frame it as input to a discussion ("Jeg ser det anderledes, fordi...", I see it differently because...).
- Feedback nobody asked for, or a sarcastic line that ends a debate, can land as aggressive even with Danes. Offer it ("Må jeg komme med en kommentar?") and keep criticism of individuals one-to-one.
- Decoding: a Dane's short email usually means exactly what it says. Danish-written English lacks the softening particles of spoken Danish (such as "jo") and can sound harder than intended.
- Adapting Danish source text for higher-context readers (Japan, the Gulf, Latin America) needs added warmth, cushioning and context.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with facts, practical benefits, transparent prices, reliability and sustainability backed by evidence. Hard selling, countdown urgency and emotional pressure breed scepticism.
- "Janteloven" (the informal norm against putting yourself above others) is the word readers reach for when copy sounds boastful. Younger and urban audiences care less, but superlatives about yourself rarely help.
- Law: every factual claim must be documentable when published; misleading claims, omitted key information and aggressive practices are banned, and the consumer regulator fines actively. Green words such as "bæredygtig", "grøn" and "klimaneutral" need solid documentation (new recommendations apply from 27 September 2026).
- Advertising must be recognisable: influencer and social posts need a clear label such as "reklame" or "annonce"; a brand tag is not enough. Stricter standards apply to content aimed at children.
- Email and SMS marketing needs prior consent, including to business addresses; the main exception is similar products to existing customers, with an easy opt-out.
- CV: one to two pages, reverse chronological, a short profile at the top saying what you can do for the employer, results with numbers, teamwork shown. A friendly, smiling photo is common and many Danish employers expect one; international firms often do not. Cover letter: one page, motivation and personality.
- Job ads may not express a preference by age, sex, race, colour, religion, political view, sexual orientation, origin or disability. "Ung", "yngre", "young dynamic team" and gendered titles like "værtinde" (hostess) have been found unlawful; "nyuddannet" (recent graduate) is safer if experienced applicants are also invited. Selling points that work: autonomy, flexible hours, flat structure, work-life balance.
- Pay transparency: an EU-based law requiring a pay range for applicants and banning pay-history questions is expected from 1 January 2027; check its final status.

## Humor and emphasis

- Office humour is dry, ironic and self-deprecating, often delivered deadpan with no signal that it is a joke. Being teased lightly usually signals acceptance. A colleague who made a mistake may bring a "kvajebajer" or "kvajekage" (oops beer or cake).
- In writing, the missing tone of voice makes irony risky. Foreign writers should prefer mild, self-directed understatement and skip sarcasm aimed at the reader.
- Danish advertising often uses irony and self-mockery, so earnest imported hype can stand out badly.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Greenland and the Faroe Islands:** self-governing parts of the Kingdom of Denmark with their own languages and identities. Since 2025 US claims on Greenland have made the topic tense. Do not call them Danish regions or treat them as available, and do not joke about a sale.
- **Religion and caricature:** the 2005 cartoon crisis remains a reference point; a 2023 law bans improper treatment of religious texts. Keep religious imagery and satire out of business content.
- Displays of wealth, luxury framing and lavish gifts sit badly; modest gifts or none.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The red-and-white Dannebrog appears at birthdays, on Christmas trees and at sports; it reads as festive rather than nationalist. The swallow-tailed version belongs to the state and royal house.
- Equality, bikes, nature, practical design and relaxed everyday settings fit; status and luxury imagery fits poorly. Heavy "hygge" branding by outsiders reads as a cliché.
- Profile and CV photos: relaxed and smiling, smart-casual rather than a stiff suit.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | dd.MM.yyyy (17.10.2026). Long: 17. oktober 2026; full: lørdag den 17. oktober 2026. Months and days lowercase. |
| Time | 24-hour. Standard form 14.30; 14:30 is also common. Pick one per text; no AM/PM. |
| Numbers | 1.234.567,89; always a leading zero (0,5). Ordinals take a period (3. maj). Numbers up to ten usually spelled out in running text. |
| Currency | 1.234,50 kr. (amount, space, "kr."); DKK in B2B and international texts. |
| Percent | 25 % (space before the sign). |
| Week starts | Monday; ISO week numbers widely used for planning ("uge 42"). |
| Phone | +45 12 34 56 78: eight digits, usually in pairs, no area codes. |
| Address | Nørregade 7, 2. tv. (street, number, floor, door left/right) / 1165 København K. Four-digit postcode before the town. |
| Punctuation | No serial comma before "og"; spaced en dash rather than em dash; hyphen in ranges (10-20). |
| Other | Metric. 37-hour work week; many offices empty after about 16:00. Five weeks' holiday; July is slow. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Age:** greeting choice is age-marked. Under-30s use "Hej" with or without a name; older readers use and expect "Kære" more and may find a nameless "Hej" impolite. Mirror the other person's opener.
- **Sectors:** banking, law and public bodies keep a slightly more formal tone and dress; startups and tech are the most casual and often work in English. A few hotels and luxury services address adult customers with "De".
- **Region:** Southern Jutland has a German-speaking minority. German town names on signs have been contested there, so use the Danish place names.
- **Greenland and the Faroes:** separate languages (Greenlandic, Faroese) and their own norms; do not reuse Danish consumer copy unchanged.
- **Hidden hierarchy:** despite flat manners, informal power exists; openly overruling or going around a manager can meet a cool response.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Opens with "Hej + first name" (or mirrors the reader's opener); no titles, "herr/fru" or "Dear Sir/Madam".
- [ ] Danish text uses "du" throughout; "De" only for royalty or a brand that deliberately uses it.
- [ ] The purpose or ask appears after at most one friendly line.
- [ ] Refusals and problems are stated plainly with one reason; no stack of cushions.
- [ ] Owner, next step and exact date are explicit; delays are announced early.
- [ ] No superlatives, urgency tricks or emotional pressure; every factual claim can be backed by documentation.
- [ ] Green, health and "best/cheapest" claims are documented or removed.
- [ ] Sponsored social content is labelled "reklame" or "annonce".
- [ ] Email/SMS marketing goes only to people who consented (or existing customers with opt-out).
- [ ] No emoji, slang or "!!" in work email; sign-off written in full in a first message; no "Kærlig hilsen".
- [ ] Irony or sarcasm aimed at the reader is removed; any humour is mild and self-directed.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (deferential, hype-heavy outreach):

> Dear Mr. Nielsen, I hope this finds you well! I would be truly honoured if you could spare a few moments of your valuable time to consider our revolutionary, industry-leading platform. Please do not hesitate to contact me at your earliest convenience!

**After** (for a Danish operations manager, in Danish with gloss):

> Hej Lars
>
> Vi hjælper lagervirksomheder med at planlægge vagter. Hos to kunder i Jylland har det halveret tiden til vagtplanen. Har du 20 minutter i uge 44, så viser jeg, hvordan det kunne se ud hos jer? Hvis det ikke er relevant, så sig bare til.
>
> Med venlig hilsen
> Sofie

*(Gloss: "Hi Lars, we help warehouse businesses plan shifts. At two customers in Jutland it has halved the time spent on the rota. Do you have 20 minutes in week 44 so I can show you how it might look for you? If it isn't relevant, just say so. Kind regards, Sofie")*

**Why:** First name and "du" replace the title and the deference. One checkable result replaces "revolutionary, industry-leading", which a Danish reader would discount and which would need documentation in an ad. The ask is concrete (20 minutes, week 44), and the easy way out respects the reader's time without sounding needy.
