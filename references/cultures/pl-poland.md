# Poland

## In brief

Polish readers combine frankness with a strong sense of courtesy and dignity. Business writing is formal at the start, with "Pan/Pani" forms of address, and becomes warmer as the relationship grows. Facts and fairness persuade; arrogance, condescension and boastfulness do not. Twentieth-century history (WWII, the Holocaust, Soviet domination) is deeply felt, and some historical phrasings are not just offensive but legally contentious.

## Voice and tone

- Polite, respectful and factual. Courtesy formulas are expected in Polish-language business writing.
- Warmth grows over time; early informality from a foreign partner can seem presumptuous.
- Humor is ironic and self-deprecating among Poles, but outsiders should be careful: jokes about Poland or Poles from foreigners can sting.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, surname. Many surnames have gendered forms (Kowalski for a man, Kowalska for a woman). Do not "correct" a woman's -ska ending to -ski.
- Polish uses the **vocative case** with names and titles in direct address: "Panie Tomaszu" (Tomasz), "Pani Anno" (Anna). Automated templates that insert a nominative name ("Dzień dobry, Pani Anna") look clumsy.
- Formal email opener: "Szanowny Panie," / "Szanowna Pani," (optionally with surname or title: "Szanowna Pani Profesor"). Semi-formal: "Dzień dobry," is widely used and gender-neutral.
- Polite address in Polish is in the third person ("Czy mógłby Pan...", "Czy zechciałaby Pani...").
- Professional titles are used as forms of address ("Pani Doktor", "Panie Prezesie", "Panie Mecenasie" for lawyers).
- Sign-offs: "Z poważaniem" (formal), "Z wyrazami szacunku" (very formal), "Pozdrawiam" or "Pozdrawiam serdecznie" (neutral to warm).
- In English, "Dear Mr Nowak" is safer than first names in first contact.
- Preserve Polish diacritics in names (ł, ś, ż, ź, ć, ń, ó, ą, ę). "Łódź" written as "Lodz" in a formal Polish context looks careless.

## Structuring a message

- Polite greeting, short courteous opening line, then the matter.
- Present facts, figures and reasoning. Make commitments and conditions explicit.
- End with a polite closing and an offer of further contact.
- Phone calls and personal meetings often accompany important written exchanges.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement is expressed fairly openly and debate is acceptable.
- Criticism should be fair and specific; Poles may respond strongly if they feel unfairly criticized or their competence or honor is questioned.
- Bottom-up feedback to management is relatively new in many companies; frame it respectfully.
- Refusals can be direct, often with a reason.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Concrete benefits, price-quality ratio, reliability and guarantees work well. Exaggerated claims and boasting reduce trust.
- Quality and European standards are reassuring; "Made in Poland" and local brands enjoy growing pride.
- The Polish Language Act requires Polish for consumer-facing information (product descriptions, warranties, instructions, advertising aimed at Polish consumers) and for employment documents.
- CVs: one to two pages, often with a photo. Traditionally include a GDPR (RODO) consent clause at the bottom allowing the employer to process personal data; many recruiters still expect it.
- Job ads should be gender-neutral; job titles often add "(K/M)" (woman/man).

## Humor and emphasis

- Irony and sarcasm are frequent among Poles, but avoid them in formal business writing to outsiders.
- Avoid stereotypes about Polish people (alcohol, plumbers, "Polish brides"); they are deeply offensive.
- Exclamation marks are common in Polish greetings and letters ("Szanowni Państwo!" can open a formal letter), but limited in business body text.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Never write "Polish death camps" or "Polish concentration camps".** The camps were built and run by Nazi Germany in occupied Poland. Use "German Nazi death camps in occupied Poland". This is both deeply offensive and politically and legally contentious.
- Promotion of Nazi, Communist or other totalitarian symbols and ideologies is illegal (Penal Code art. 256). Holocaust denial is illegal.
- WWII, the Katyn massacre, the Communist period, the Smolensk air disaster, Volhynia, relations with Russia, Germany, Ukraine and Belarus are all sensitive.
- Do not praise Russia or Soviet history uncritically; distrust of Russia is widespread.
- Refer to Poland as Central Europe (or Central and Eastern Europe), not just "Eastern Europe".
- Use Polish place names (Wrocław, Gdańsk, Szczecin), not historical German names (Breslau, Danzig, Stettin).
- Religion: Catholicism is culturally important; avoid mocking faith or religious institutions. Abortion, LGBT rights and other moral-political topics are polarized.
- Domestic politics (the main parties' rivalry, courts, media) is heated.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- White and red are national colors.
- Even-numbered bouquets are for funerals; flowers given as gifts come in odd numbers. Chrysanthemums are associated with cemeteries and All Saints' Day (1 November).
- The "figa" gesture (thumb between index and middle finger) is a rude "you get nothing".
- Hammer and sickle, swastika and other totalitarian symbols must not appear even ironically.

## Variation within the culture

- Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław, Poznań and Gdańsk are cosmopolitan and English-friendly; rural and eastern regions are more traditional and religious.
- Younger professionals in IT, startups and international companies are informal and use first names (often in English).
- Large Ukrainian community since 2014 and especially since 2022; Ukrainian-language versions may be useful for some consumer and HR content.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (casual US style):

> Hey Anna, quick one: can you send me the contract by tomorrow? Thx!

**After** (tailored, in Polish, first contact):

> Szanowna Pani,
>
> uprzejmie proszę o przesłanie podpisanej umowy do jutra, 8 marca, do godz. 12:00. Jest ona potrzebna do rejestracji zamówienia w systemie.
>
> Z poważaniem,

*(English gloss: "Dear Madam, I kindly ask you to send the signed contract by tomorrow, 8 March, 12:00. It is needed to register the order. Yours sincerely,")*

**Why:** Early in a Polish business relationship, a formal salutation, polite request form, precise deadline and a reason are expected. Abbreviations and casual tone read as disrespectful.
