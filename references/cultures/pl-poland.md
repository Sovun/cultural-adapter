# Poland

## In brief

Polish readers combine frankness about content with a strong sense of courtesy and dignity. Business writing starts formal, with "Pan/Pani" address, a title or the vocative in the salutation and a set closing formula, and warms up as the relationship grows. Facts, realistic numbers and fairness persuade; arrogance, condescension and boasting do not. Polish law is strict on everyday marketing and HR text: consent for marketing email, the 30-day price rule for discounts, labelled advertising and gender-neutral job ads with pay ranges. Twentieth-century history (WWII, the Holocaust, Soviet domination) is deeply felt, and "Polish death camps" is a phrase never to use.

## Voice and tone

- Polite, respectful and factual. Courtesy formulas are expected in Polish business writing; a formal email does not signal coldness.
- Early informality from a foreign partner can seem presumptuous. Initial reserve is a formality, not lack of interest.
- Poles writing in English may use fewer hedges and more imperatives than natives; decode incoming mail with that in mind.
- UI and consumer copy should sound natural, not bureaucratic ("wybrać" not "dokonać wyboru", "można" not "istnieje możliwość"). Word-for-word translation from English reads stiff.
- Humour is often ironic and self-deprecating, but jokes about Poland, its history or religion from outsiders sting.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, surname. Many surnames have gendered forms (Kowalski / Kowalska); never "correct" -ska to -ski.
- **Vocative** in direct address: "Panie Tomaszu", "Pani Anno". Templates that insert a nominative name ("Dzień dobry, Pani Anna", "Cześć Anna") look clumsy.
- Formal opener: "Szanowny Panie," / "Szanowna Pani," / "Szanowni Państwo," (unknown reader, shared inbox, groups). **No surname after it**: "Szanowny Panie Kowalski" is a calque. Add a title or function in the vocative instead: "Szanowna Pani Profesor", "Szanowny Panie Dyrektorze", "Szanowna Pani Prezes".
- The salutation ends with a comma and the next line starts lowercase. An exclamation mark ("Szanowni Państwo!") is now dated.
- "Dzień dobry," is a widely used, gender-neutral opener for routine and semi-formal email. Avoid "Witam" in first contact: many readers find it condescending.
- Capitalise Pan, Pani, Państwo and their forms (Panu, Pana, Państwa) when addressing the reader.
- Polite requests use the third person: "Czy mógłby Pan...", "Czy zechciałaby Pani...".
- Titles are forms of address: "Pani Doktor", "Panie Prezesie", "Panie Mecenasie" (lawyer).
- After some contact, "Pani Magdo / Panie Marcinie" is a middle register. The move to "ty" is proposed by the higher-ranked person; a junior should not start it.
- Sign-offs, no comma after them: "Z poważaniem" (formal), "Z wyrazami szacunku" (formal, warmer), "Pozdrawiam" / "Pozdrawiam serdecznie" (semi-formal, for established contacts, not a first formal letter).
- In English, "Dear Mr Nowak" is safer than first names in first contact. Keep Polish diacritics (ł, ś, ż, ź, ć, ń, ó, ą, ę): "Lodz" for Łódź looks careless.

## Structuring a message

- Specific subject line; never send an attachment without a covering text.
- Salutation, a short courteous line, then the reason for writing ("Zwracam się z prośbą o...", "W odpowiedzi na Pana wiadomość..."), then the facts and justification.
- Make commitments, owners, dates and conditions explicit; confirm verbal agreements in writing.
- End with a polite closing and an offer of further contact. If a written thread stays unclear, suggest a short call.
- Official letters carry place and date top right: "Warszawa, 19 października 2026 r.".

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Debate is acceptable and not taken as a personal judgement; problems should be raised openly and early.
- The "blunt Poles" image is overstated. Requests and criticism are mostly phrased conventionally ("Czy mógłby Pan...?", "Wydaje mi się, że..."), yet Poles state criticism more often and more directly than English speakers and rarely stay silent.
- Feedback often names the problem first; keep it specific, fair and private. Public criticism is a serious mistake, and an unfair attack on competence or honour provokes a strong defence.
- In traditional firms, upward feedback is newer; frame it respectfully. Multinational and IT teams are more open.
- Refusals are clear in content but wrapped in the formal frame, usually with a reason.
- Decode "Jakoś to będzie" (it'll work out somehow) as possibly downplaying a problem.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Concrete benefits, price-quality ratio, reliability and guarantees work. Exaggeration and pushy sales language reduce trust.
- Foreign sellers need extra trust signals: PLN prices, local payment methods, clear delivery and returns. Local brands enjoy growing pride.
- Register: consumer tech brands write "ty" with capital "Ty/Twój"; banks, insurers, public services and B2B use Pan/Pani or Państwo. Avoid gendered verb forms (zapomniałeś/aś): use present tense, imperative or impersonal forms ("Nie pamiętasz hasła?", "Hasło jest nieprawidłowe").
- **Discounts:** any price reduction must show the lowest price from the 30 days before it, and the percentage must be computed from that price (price information act, UOKiK guidance).
- **Advertising labels** in Polish: "#reklama", "Płatna współpraca z marką X". Displayed reviews must say whether and how they are verified; fake reviews draw heavy fines. Vague green claims ("eko", "neutralne dla klimatu") are pursued by UOKiK; EU rules tightened from September 2026, check current status.
- Comparative ads must rely on objective, verifiable features and not denigrate rivals.
- The Polish Language Act requires Polish for consumer offers, product information, warranties and advertising; an English slogan is fine only if the offer content also exists in Polish.
- CVs: reverse chronological, often with a professional photo (optional). Employers may require only basic data (Labour Code art. 22¹); a RODO consent clause at the end is customary and expected by many recruiters, though not legally required for basic data. Use the wording the ad asks for.
- **Job ads** (Labour Code, since 24 Dec 2025): titles and ads must be gender-neutral or give both forms ("asystent/asystentka"); "(K/M)" is common practice. Candidates must get the pay or pay range, and employers may not ask about past pay. No age or sex preferences ("do 35 lat"). Breaches lead to compensation claims rather than inspectorate fines for wording; a bill to drop the neutrality duty was pending at last check.

## Humor and emphasis

- Irony and sarcasm are frequent among Poles; keep them out of formal business writing to outsiders.
- Avoid stereotypes about Poles (alcohol, plumbers, "Polish brides"); they are deeply offensive.
- Exclamation marks are rare in business text.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Never write "Polish death camps" or "Polish concentration camps".** Nazi Germany built and ran them in occupied Poland: write "German Nazi death camps in occupied Poland". The 2018 remembrance-law amendment dropped criminal penalties, but civil claims to protect Poland's good name remain possible; check current status.
- Promoting Nazi, Communist or other totalitarian systems is illegal (Penal Code art. 256); Holocaust denial is illegal. Ads offending religious feelings can trigger prosecution (art. 196).
- **Marketing email, SMS and calls** need prior, specific consent, for business addresses too (Electronic Communications Law art. 398, since November 2024). A cold B2B sales email is legally risky.
- WWII, Katyn, the communist period, Smolensk, Volhynia and relations with Russia, Germany, Ukraine and Belarus are sensitive. Do not praise Russia or Soviet history.
- Poland is Central Europe, not "Eastern Europe", and not a generic "nearshore" location. Use Polish place names (Wrocław, Gdańsk), not German ones (Breslau, Danzig).
- Catholicism is culturally important; do not mock faith. Abortion, LGBT rights and domestic politics are polarised.
- Disability: "osoba z niepełnosprawnością", not "kaleka" or "cierpiący na".

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- White and red are national colours; the eagle and flag are protected.
- Odd-numbered bouquets as gifts; even numbers are for funerals. Chrysanthemums mean cemeteries and All Saints' Day (1 November), a solemn day for upbeat sales.
- Christmas Eve (Wigilia) and name days (imieniny) are important occasions.
- The "figa" gesture is a rude "you get nothing". No totalitarian symbols, even ironically.

## Variation within the culture

- Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław, Poznań and Gdańsk are cosmopolitan and English-friendly; rural and eastern regions are more traditional and religious.
- Corporations, IT and startups use first names and "ty", even with bosses. Public administration, law, medicine, academia and many SMEs keep Pan/Pani and titles. Some older readers resent "ty" from younger writers.
- Large Ukrainian community since 2014 and especially 2022; Ukrainian versions may help in consumer and HR content.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (casual US style):

> Hey Anna, quick one: can you send me the contract by tomorrow? Thx!

**After** (tailored, in Polish, first contact):

> Szanowna Pani,
>
> uprzejmie proszę o przesłanie podpisanej umowy do jutra, 8 marca, do godz. 12:00. Jest ona potrzebna do rejestracji zamówienia w systemie.
>
> Z poważaniem

*(English gloss: "Dear Madam, I kindly ask you to send the signed contract by tomorrow, 8 March, 12:00. It is needed to register the order. Yours sincerely")*

**Why:** Early in a Polish business relationship, a formal salutation without the surname, a lowercase start after the comma, a polite request form, a precise deadline and a reason are expected. Abbreviations and a casual tone read as disrespectful.
