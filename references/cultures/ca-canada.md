# Canada

## In brief

Canadian professional readers expect North American efficiency with a softer edge: the point early, first names, clear next steps, but more courtesy, less hype and more room for other people's views than a typical US text gives. Claims are trusted when they are modest and backed by evidence. Canada is bilingual by law at the federal level, and anything sold, advertised or posted to Quebec must be available in French that is at least as prominent as any other language. Writing for Canada as if it were the US (spelling, prices, holidays, "domestic") is a common and, lately, touchy mistake.

## Voice and tone

- Plain, courteous and even-tempered. Short sentences, active voice, "you" for the reader and "we" for the organization. Common contractions (we'll, don't) are fine even in official text.
- Requests carry "please" and a softener: "Would you be able to send the draft by Friday?" A curt but correct message can read as rude.
- A light apology ("Sorry for the slow reply") is ordinary courtesy and does not admit fault.
- Strip US-style hype. "We're thrilled to unveil a game-changing solution!" becomes a plain statement with one fact.
- Canadian French copy is clear and close to everyday speech but without slang. Use French terms where France often borrows English: "courriel" (email), "fin de semaine" (weekend), "application" not "app".

## Addressing people

- English: "Hi Priya," for most business email; "Dear Ms. Tremblay," for a first formal contact, with a period after the title. Titles go with surnames only, never "Ms. Priya". Mirror the name the other person signs with.
- Unknown recipient: use the full name without a title, or a role ("Dear Hiring Manager"). Avoid "Dear Sir or Madam" and "Hi guys"; "Hi everyone" or "Hello all" works for groups.
- Sign-offs: "Thanks," "Best regards," "Kind regards," in email; "Sincerely," in cover letters and formal letters.
- French (Quebec): the scale runs from "Madame," (official) to "Bonjour Madame Tremblay," (polite) to "Bonjour Céline," (colleagues). "Chère Céline," only for someone you know well; in Quebec it reads more intimate than in France. Write "Madame" in full in a salutation, not "Mme". "Bonjour," alone is acceptable when you are unsure of the title.
- French closings: "Salutations," "Bonne journée," "Merci de votre collaboration" (neutral); "Cordialement" (slightly warmer); "Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, mes salutations distinguées" only in formal letters.
- "Vous" with customers, older people and superiors until they offer "tu". "On peut se tutoyer?" (shall we say tu?) is common and friendly; declining it can seem distant.
- Gender-inclusive wording is standard in both languages: "everyone", "chairperson"; in French, doublets or epicene forms ("le personnel", "les spécialistes", "toutes et tous").

## Structuring a message

1. Clear subject line naming topic and, if needed, action and date.
2. Greeting and one courtesy line ("Hope your week is going well").
3. Purpose in the first paragraph, then reasons in short paragraphs or a short list.
4. An explicit invitation for input where others are affected ("Let me know if this works for your team").
5. Next steps with owner and date, then thanks and sign-off.

- Spell out abbreviations at first use; prefer "for example" to "e.g.". Link text should say where it goes, not "click here".
- Silence is not agreement. Ask for a confirmation when you need one.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagree with the idea, not the person, and acknowledge the other view first: "I see why that option appeals. My concern is the timeline."
- Feedback usually comes balanced with positives. Make sure the actual request survives the cushioning; newcomers often miss a criticism buried between compliments.
- Hints such as "It might be worth another look" are often real objections. When decoding Canadian source text for blunter readers, make them explicit.
- Refuse with a reason and, if possible, an alternative. Hard bargaining, ultimatums and early escalation (copying a boss on a first complaint) read as aggressive; proposals that leave room for discussion work better.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with evidence, references and fair framing. Understated confidence, warmth and humour beat hard sell; many high-performing US ads do worse in Canada. Local relevance matters, but token maple leaves without substance are noticed.
- Advertising rules: a claim must not mislead through what it says, what it leaves out or the picture it creates; have the proof in hand before publishing; footnotes cannot contradict the headline. Comparative ads are allowed without unfair disparagement. Testimonials must be genuine and current. Environmental claims need proper substantiation under competition law.
- Prices: a bare "$" means Canadian dollars. Mark US prices "US$" or "USD". Advertised prices usually exclude sales tax, which varies by province.
- Commercial email and SMS need prior consent, sender identification and a working unsubscribe link (anti-spam law).
- Quebec bans commercial advertising aimed at children under 13. Quebec campaigns are best created for Quebec, not translated from English Canada or adapted from France.
- Résumés: one or two pages, reverse chronological, a short summary, achievements with numbers, pronoun-less bullets ("Managed a team of 8"). No photo, date of birth, marital status, religion, ethnicity or Social Insurance Number. References on request, not on the page. Volunteer work is valued. Quebec employers posting in French expect a French CV.
- Job ads must not express a preference based on age, sex, origin, religion, disability, family status or other protected grounds; avoid "young team", "recent graduate", "native English speaker". In Ontario, larger employers must now state a pay range, disclose AI screening and not require "Canadian experience". Other provinces have their own rules.

## Humor and emphasis

- Dry, self-deprecating and situational humour works; humour at a group's expense does not. Jokes about Canadian politeness or winter sound tired from an outsider.
- Quebec humour is its own tradition; English-Canadian and France-French jokes rarely transfer.
- Emphasis: bold sparingly, no all caps, at most one exclamation mark in friendly text. In French copy, replace English exclamation marks with stronger words.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **French in Quebec (Charter of the French Language, amended by Bill 96):** consumers have the right to be informed and served in French, including by email, chat and phone. Labels, manuals, warranties, catalogues, websites, social posts and invoices aimed at Quebec must exist in French, and another language may not be more available. Standard-form contracts must be offered in French first. Since June 2025, outdoor signs with a non-French trademark need French wording with markedly predominant visual weight, and generic words in a trademark on a product must appear in French. Firms with 25 or more employees in Quebec must register and work mainly in French. The regulator is the OQLF.
- **Job postings in Quebec** must be in French; other-language versions appear at the same time and with no greater prominence, and any requirement for a language other than French must be justified.
- **Federal bilingualism:** federal institutions publish equivalent content in English and French. Mandatory information on prepackaged consumer products must be in both languages across Canada. Machine-translated or token French in national material reads as disrespect.
- **Quebec identity and sovereignty:** do not take sides, and do not treat Quebec as just another province or French as a courtesy language.
- **Indigenous peoples:** First Nations, Inuit and Métis are three distinct peoples; Métis are not a First Nation. Name the specific nation where possible; "Indigenous" (capitalized) is the usual umbrella term; "Aboriginal" survives mainly in legal text; "Indian" only in legal or historical contexts; "Eskimo" is offensive. Avoid possessives like "our Indigenous peoples" and slang such as "pow wow" for a meeting. 30 September is the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, linked to residential schools.
- **Land acknowledgements** open many events, and public bodies and universities often add them to email signatures. If used, name the specific nations and treaty, use the present tense, and mean it; a generic pasted line can read as performative.
- **The US:** framing Canada as part of the US market, or US politics as "ours", irritates readers, more so since the 2025 trade dispute.
- Politics, religion, health and personal finances stay out of early correspondence. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- No strong number or colour taboos. The maple leaf and red and white signal Canada; use them with substance, not as decoration.
- Red poppies are worn from late October to Remembrance Day (11 November) and are a serious symbol.
- Imagery should reflect a diverse, multicultural population; conspicuous luxury works less well than relatable everyday scenes. Do not convey meaning by colour alone.

## Variation within the culture

- **Quebec (fr-CA):** French is the default public and working language; most people understand English but expect French. Tone is warmer and more animated, relationship building counts a bit more, and hierarchy is slightly more visible ("vous" to superiors), while "tu" spreads faster than in France. Quebec French differs from France French in vocabulary, salutations, typography and formats; France-French copy used unchanged is spotted at once. Treat Quebec as a separate market with its own creative.
- **Francophones outside Quebec:** New Brunswick is officially bilingual; Acadian and Franco-Ontarian communities also expect French where services are offered in it.
- **English Canada:** Toronto business is often described as corporate and fast-paced, Western Canada as more informal. Tech and creative sectors are the most casual; government, law and finance more careful.
- **Immigrant population:** nearly a quarter of residents were born abroad, so readers bring many norms. Plain, idiom-free English serves everyone.
- **Indigenous communities:** use each nation's own name and spelling; in Nunavut, Inuktitut is an official language.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Canadian spelling (colour, centre, cheque) and no US-only references (states, US holidays, "domestic" meaning US).
- [ ] Prices in CAD with a plain "$", or US prices marked "US$"; sales tax handling clear.
- [ ] Dates use the month name or YYYY-MM-DD, never ambiguous numeric forms.
- [ ] Purpose appears after at most one courtesy line; next steps have an owner and a date.
- [ ] Criticism and refusals are clear but cushioned, with a reason; input is invited where others are affected.
- [ ] No hype, superlative strings or multiple exclamation marks; claims carry evidence.
- [ ] Content reaching Quebec has French at least as prominent as English, published at the same time.
- [ ] French text is fr-CA, not France French: OQLF salutations, no space before ; ! ?, "14 h 30", "1 234,56 $".
- [ ] French uses "vous" with customers and strangers; register is consistent.
- [ ] National or federal material is professionally translated into both official languages.
- [ ] Indigenous references use First Nations, Inuit or Métis correctly, specific nation names, no possessives or appropriated slang.
- [ ] Any land acknowledgement names the specific nations and is not boilerplate.
- [ ] Résumés have no photo, birth date, marital status or SIN; references are not listed.
- [ ] Job ads avoid age, origin and "native speaker" wording; Ontario ads state pay range and AI use.
- [ ] Marketing email has consent, sender identity and an unsubscribe link.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style sales email):

> We're the #1 analytics platform in North America and we'll transform your reporting overnight! Sign the attached agreement by Friday to lock in this exclusive price. Pricing is $40,000/year.

**After** (for a Canadian operations manager):

> Hi Marc,
>
> Thanks for your time on Tuesday. As promised, here is our proposal for the reporting project. Two Canadian retailers using the platform cut their month-end reporting from five days to two, and we've included their contact details if you'd like to hear it from them. The price is CA$40,000 a year before tax, and we can hold it until November 14 to give your team time to review.
>
> Would a call next week with you and your finance lead be useful? Happy to adjust the scope based on their input.
>
> Best regards,

**Why:** The claim moves from a superlative to checkable proof and references, the price names the currency and tax, and the pressured deadline becomes a reasonable hold date. Inviting the finance lead respects consensus decisions. For a Quebec client, the whole message would be in French, starting with "Bonjour Marc," and using "vous" until Marc suggests "tu".
