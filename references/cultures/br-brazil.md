# Brazil

## In brief

Brazilian readers respond to warmth, personal connection and optimism. Business runs on trust in people, so a cold, purely transactional message rarely works. Communication is expressive but avoids confrontation: a flat "no" is uncommon and criticism is cushioned. Brazilian Portuguese is not European Portuguese, Brazilians are not Hispanic, and both mix-ups irritate. Consumer and advertising law is strict and actively enforced: claims must be provable, paid content labelled and job ads free of discriminatory requirements; racism is a serious crime.

## Voice and tone

- Warm, friendly, optimistic, personal. Even B2B email is more cordial than in Northern Europe or the US.
- Brand, app and UI copy is conversational: "você", short everyday words, fragments like "Pronto para começar". An institutional register reads bureaucratic.
- Formal and official email has no emoji or displays of emotion; chat (WhatsApp, Teams) is looser, and readers expect warmth to be mirrored.
- No internet abbreviations ("vc", "pq") in professional email. ALL CAPS reads as shouting; use bold.
- Use Brazilian Portuguese (pt-BR). European forms look foreign: celular not telemóvel, usuário not utilizador, senha not palavra-passe, time or equipe not equipa, ônibus not autocarro, "estamos fazendo" not "estamos a fazer". Text translated for Portugal needs full adaptation. Never Spanish.
- Light humor is welcome; sarcasm is risky in writing. Coarse language is banned in ads.
- English proficiency is low outside multinationals; send important material in Portuguese.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s), then usually mother's and father's surnames (João Carlos Pereira Silva). The last surname is the short form, but some go by another, and married women often add the husband's surname. Mirror the signature, including nicknames.
- "Você" is the national default. "O senhor / a senhora" (third-person verb) shows respect to older people, senior clients and in formal B2B. "Tu" is regional (South, parts of the North and Northeast) and not for national copy.
- Title + first name is common ("Dr. Paulo", "Dona Maria", "Sr. Ricardo"); title + surname also occurs in formal settings. "Doutor/Doutora" is standard for lawyers and physicians; as a general courtesy it sounds dated, and "Senhor/Senhora" is enough.
- Openers: "Prezado Sr. Ricardo," / "Prezada Fernanda," (formal; "Prezado(a)" if gender unknown, "Prezados" for a group), "Caro(a)" (slightly warmer), "Bom dia, Ricardo," or "Olá, Fernanda," once in contact, "Oi!" in chat.
- Closings: "Atenciosamente," (safe default, often "Att."), "Respeitosamente," (superiors, authorities, courts), "Abraços," / "Abs" (known colleagues), "Beijos" only between close contacts. Opener and closer must match: no "Abraços" to a judge or senior stranger.
- Government and judiciary: "Vossa Excelência" for ministers, members of Congress, senior judges; "Vossa Senhoria" for other authorities. "Ilustríssimo" and "Digníssimo" are obsolete.

## Structuring a message

- Specific subject line ("Agendamento de reunião sobre X", not "Reunião").
- Greeting plus a short personal touch ("Espero que esteja tudo bem com você").
- Then state the purpose; the warmth is a short layer, not a long preamble. Short paragraphs and bullets.
- Close by restating the request: "Aguardo seu retorno" (request), "Fico à disposição" (information). Full signature with title and phone.
- WhatsApp is the preferred business channel, even with partners. Delicate matters go to a call.
- Confirm verbal agreements in a warm recap email; it does not read as distrust.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Avoid blunt negatives. Use softeners: "Talvez seja difícil...", "Vamos ver o que dá para fazer", or point to organisational constraints so the refusal is not personal.
- Decoding: "vou ver", "vamos ver", "pode ser" or a friendly "sim, claro" may be polite non-commitment. Look for a concrete next step before counting it as agreement.
- Employees avoid contradicting superiors. Ask explicit, open questions to check status.
- Criticism privately, positives first, changes framed as shared improvement. Never in group emails or CC'd threads.
- When adapting Brazilian text for direct cultures: make commitments explicit and trim affectionate phrasing.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Emotional, aspirational, family- and community-oriented messaging works. Campaigns tied to shared rituals (Carnaval, football, festa junina, family gatherings, year-end) beat imported concepts; build ads on Brazilian settings.
- Influencers, social commerce and WhatsApp are central.
- Prices in reais, with installment options ("em até 10x sem juros") next to the cash price. Credit or installment ads must show the cash price, number and value of installments and total; no "easy credit" pressure.
- Claims (CDC and CONAR code): the advertiser must prove every factual claim; surveys need a named source; comparisons must be objective; "grátis" only if nothing is charged; a discount needs the earlier price. A precise offer or price binds the advertiser.
- Paid, gifted or affiliate influencer posts must be labelled (platform tool, "#publi" or "#publicidade"), visible without tapping "mais".
- Consumer product information, terms and labels must be in Portuguese. Advertising aimed at children is heavily restricted.
- Represent Brazil's ethnic diversity; the ad code bars any discrimination.
- CV (currículo): one to two pages, reverse chronological, short summary with measurable results. Header: name, city/state, phone with WhatsApp, email, LinkedIn. Leave out CPF, RG and full address. Photo: current default is none (it belongs on LinkedIn); traditional firms and some customer-facing roles may still expect one. Age and marital status are fading.
- Job ads (CLT art. 373-A, Law 9.029/1995): no requirement on sex, age, colour, marital or family status unless the job obviously needs it. "Boa aparência", "até 30 anos", "moça", "sem filhos" are unlawful. Affirmative vacancies for disadvantaged groups are accepted.

## Humor and emphasis

- Warm, light humor is welcome in consumer copy and among colleagues who know each other.
- Exclamation marks and emoji are fine in business chat; keep them out of formal email.
- Avoid jokes on race, religion, sexuality, regions or class. The number 24 joke is a homophobic trope; using 24 is fine.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Racism is a crime** (Law 7.716/1989; imprescriptible and not bailable under the Constitution). Racial insult counts as racism, and a "joke" context raises the penalty (Law 14.532/2023).
- **Religious discrimination** is a crime; do not mock any religion, including Candomblé and Umbanda. Avoid "macumba" as a put-down.
- Brazilians are not Hispanic and do not speak Spanish.
- Politics is highly polarized, especially around general elections (held every four years in October: 2026, 2030). Check current context before any political or patriotic reference; party propaganda falls under electoral law.
- The military dictatorship (1964 to 1985), Amazon deforestation and indigenous rights are charged; avoid exoticizing favelas and poverty.
- LGPD (regulator ANPD): consent specific to a purpose, highlighted and easy to withdraw; race, religion, health and political opinion are sensitive data; children's data needs parental consent; publish a data protection contact.
- E-commerce must show company name, CNPJ and address, explain the 7-day withdrawal right and answer within 5 days (Decree 7.962/2013).

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The **OK hand gesture** is obscene. Use thumbs up instead.
- Green and yellow (flag, national jersey) became politically charged in recent elections; heavy flag-colour branding can read as partisan, though the colours are broadly shared during World Cups. Red with political symbols reads as left. Check current context.
- The national flag must not be altered or printed on packaging or labels (Law 5.700/1971); hint at national colours instead.
- Carnival imagery fits festive campaigns but is reductive for corporate content; disrespectful use of folk festivities is condemned in ads.
- Purple is sometimes linked to mourning (low evidence); avoid purple flowers as gifts to be safe.

## Variation within the culture

- Register varies by sector: consumer, digital and startups are informal and warm; law, finance, academia and government stay formal ("Prezado(a)", "Senhor", "Atenciosamente").
- São Paulo is usually described as faster and more task-focused; regional accents, references and the use of "tu" differ across states, so national campaigns should avoid one region's codes.
- Gender-neutral forms ("todes") are divisive; prefer neutral recasting ("a equipe", "pessoa", "você").
- Evangelical Christianity is growing and influences attitudes in some consumer segments.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (terse Northern European style):

> Ana, the report is late. Send it by Friday.

**After** (tailored for a Brazilian reader, in English):

> Hi Ana, I hope everything is going well with you! I wanted to check in on the quarterly report: do you think we could have it by Friday? It would really help us prepare for Monday's meeting with the client. If anything is getting in the way, just let me know and we'll find a solution together. Abraços,

**Why:** Brazilian readers expect warmth and a relationship-oriented tone. The request and deadline remain clear, but the problem is framed collaboratively instead of as a reprimand.
