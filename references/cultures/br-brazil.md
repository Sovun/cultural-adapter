# Brazil

## In brief

Brazilian readers respond to warmth, personal connection and optimism. Business is built on relationships and trust; a cold, purely transactional message rarely succeeds. Communication is expressive but conflict-avoidant: a direct "no" is uncommon, and criticism is softened. Brazilian Portuguese differs from European Portuguese, and Brazilians are not Hispanic; both mix-ups irritate readers. Racism is a serious crime and religious intolerance is punishable by law.

## Voice and tone

- Warm, friendly, optimistic, personal. Even B2B communication tends to be more cordial than in Northern Europe or the US.
- Brazilian Portuguese (pt-BR) must be used; European Portuguese (pt-PT) vocabulary and grammar ("tu" forms, "autocarro", "telemóvel", "equipa") look foreign. Do not use Spanish.
- Light humor and positivity are welcome; sarcasm and irony can be misread.
- Avoid swearing and blasphemy.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s), then mother's family name, then father's family name (João Carlos Pereira Silva). The **last** surname is usually the main one in formal contexts, but many people are known by first name even in business.
- Common business address: title + first name ("Sr. Ricardo", "Sra. Fernanda", "Dr. Paulo"). "Doutor/Doutora" is used for lawyers and physicians and sometimes as general respect for senior professionals.
- Email openings: "Prezado Sr. Ricardo," / "Prezada Sra. Fernanda," (formal), "Olá, Fernanda," or "Oi, Fernanda!" (informal), "Bom dia, Ricardo," (very common).
- Sign-offs: "Atenciosamente," (formal), "Att.," (abbreviated), "Abraços," or "Abs," (warm, common between business contacts once there is rapport), "Um abraço,".

## Structuring a message

- Greeting plus a personal touch ("Espero que esteja tudo bem com você e sua família").
- Context and relationship, then the request.
- Keep a friendly closing and an invitation to talk (phone or WhatsApp; WhatsApp is the dominant business channel).
- Follow up on commitments; enthusiasm at the start does not guarantee follow-through.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Avoid blunt negatives. Use softeners: "Talvez seja difícil...", "Vamos ver o que dá para fazer".
- Employees may avoid contradicting superiors and may say a problem is solved to please. Ask open questions to check status.
- Criticism privately and gently, starting with positives.
- When adapting Brazilian source text for direct cultures: make commitments explicit and remove excessive affectionate phrasing.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Emotional, aspirational, family- and community-oriented messaging works well. Football (futebol), music, celebration, nature and humor are strong cultural codes.
- Social commerce, influencers and WhatsApp marketing are central.
- CONAR (self-regulatory advertising code) and the Consumer Defense Code (CDC) prohibit misleading claims; product information for consumers must be in Portuguese. LGPD governs personal data.
- Advertising to children is heavily restricted.
- Represent Brazil's ethnic diversity authentically (large Afro-Brazilian population); all-white imagery is increasingly criticized.
- CV (currículo): one to two pages; photo optional and declining; age and marital status were traditionally included but are increasingly omitted. LinkedIn is widely used.

## Humor and emphasis

- Light-hearted, warm humor is welcome; self-deprecation and gentle teasing are common among friends.
- Exclamation marks and emoji are acceptable even in business chats.
- Avoid humor about race, religion, regions (especially Northeastern stereotypes) or poverty.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Racism is a crime** in Brazil (imprescriptible and not subject to bail under the Constitution); racial slurs and "jokes" can lead to prosecution.
- **Religious intolerance** is punishable; do not mock any religion, including Afro-Brazilian religions (Candomblé, Umbanda). Avoid the pejorative term "macumba".
- Brazilians are not Hispanic and do not speak Spanish; "Latino" is acceptable to some, not all.
- Politics is polarized; the 1964 to 1985 military dictatorship is divisive. Foreign criticism of Brazil is resented.
- Favelas and poverty: avoid exoticizing or using them as backdrops.
- Amazon deforestation and indigenous rights are politically charged.
- Argentina is a friendly rival; avoid praising Argentina over Brazil (especially in football).

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The **OK hand gesture** is obscene in Brazil. Use thumbs up instead.
- Green and yellow are national colors and became politically charged in recent elections; check context.
- Purple is associated with mourning in some contexts; avoid purple flowers as gifts.
- Carnival imagery is fine for festive campaigns but reductive for corporate content.

## Variation within the culture

- São Paulo is fast, business-focused and more punctual; Rio de Janeiro more relaxed; the South has strong European (German, Italian) influence; the Northeast has strong Afro-Brazilian and regional cultures and faces stereotyping.
- Large corporations and multinationals are more formal and structured than family businesses.
- Evangelical Christianity is growing and influences consumer attitudes in some segments.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (terse Northern European style):

> Ana, the report is late. Send it by Friday.

**After** (tailored for a Brazilian reader, in English):

> Hi Ana, I hope everything is going well with you! I wanted to check in on the quarterly report: do you think we could have it by Friday? It would really help us prepare for Monday's meeting with the client. If anything is getting in the way, just let me know and we'll find a solution together. Abraços,

**Why:** Brazilian readers expect warmth and a relationship-oriented tone. The request and deadline remain clear, but the problem is framed collaboratively instead of as a reprimand.
