# Indonesia

## In brief

Indonesia is the world's largest Muslim-majority country and an archipelago of hundreds of ethnic groups; Javanese and Islamic norms shape the mainstream, but Bali (Hindu), Christian regions and Chinese-Indonesian communities differ. Communication is gentle, indirect and harmony-seeking: people avoid saying no, avoid open conflict and may give the answer they believe you hope for. Respect for age and seniority is expressed through forms of address (Bapak, Ibu). Relationships come before business, and high-pressure or emotional tactics backfire.

## Voice and tone

- Gentle, polite, respectful, warm. Calm language without pressure.
- Use Bahasa Indonesia (call it "Indonesian" or "Bahasa Indonesia", not just "Bahasa", which means "language"). It differs from Malay in vocabulary; Malaysian copy should not be reused unchanged.
- Formal written Indonesian (bahasa baku) for official and B2B documents; casual, slang-rich Indonesian (bahasa gaul) for youth consumer marketing on Instagram, TikTok and WhatsApp.
- English proficiency is moderate overall; in English, keep sentences simple and avoid idioms.

## Addressing people

- Many Indonesians have a single name (Suparman) or several names that are all part of the given name; there is often no family surname. Do not force "first name / last name" fields or infer a surname.
- Address with honorifics + name: "Bapak Budi" / "Pak Budi" (Mr), "Ibu Sari" / "Bu Sari" (Mrs/Ms). Used for anyone older or senior, and with clients regardless of age.
- Formal letters/emails: "Yth. Bapak Budi Santoso" (Yth. = Yang terhormat, "the respected"), "Dengan hormat," as opening. Closing: "Hormat kami," (respectfully, on behalf of a company), "Salam," or "Terima kasih,".
- Titles and initials (H. for Haji, Hj. for Hajjah, academic degrees such as S.E., M.M., Dr.) may be included in names and signatures; preserve them.
- In English: "Dear Mr Budi" (using the given name is normal because many lack surnames), or "Dear Pak Budi".

## Structuring a message

- Polite greeting, often with Islamic salutation in Muslim contexts ("Assalamualaikum") where appropriate, and a wish for wellbeing.
- Background and relationship context before the request.
- Phrase requests as invitations or suggestions ("Kami mohon bantuan Bapak untuk...", "We kindly request your help to...").
- Leave room for consensus; avoid ultimatums.
- Confirm details later in writing; meetings can end with some points still vague.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Never shame anyone in front of others. Criticism is private, indirect, and wrapped in appreciation.
- "Yes" (ya, iya) often means "I hear you". "Insya Allah" can mean uncertainty.
- Avoid raising your voice, sarcasm or laughing at mistakes.
- If something is wrong, ask for help solving a problem rather than assigning blame.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Family, community, religion, togetherness (kebersamaan), national pride and festive seasons (Ramadan, Lebaran/Eid al-Fitr, Christmas in Christian areas, Chinese New Year, Nyepi in Bali) are central themes.
- **Halal**: Indonesia's Halal Product Assurance Law makes halal certification mandatory for many product categories (food, beverages, cosmetics, some consumer goods), phased in over the 2020s. Halal status is a strong purchase driver; non-halal products must be labelled.
- **Language Law (Law 24/2009)**: Indonesian must be used in agreements involving Indonesian parties, product information, public signage and many official contexts; bilingual contracts are common.
- Indonesian advertising ethics (Etika Pariwara Indonesia) prohibit misleading claims and content offensive to religion, ethnicity, race and inter-group relations (SARA).
- Modest imagery: avoid revealing clothing, alcohol (illegal in Aceh, restricted elsewhere), pork, gambling, romance and LGBT themes for mainstream audiences.
- Social commerce, influencers and WhatsApp are dominant channels.
- CV: often includes photo, date of birth, religion and marital status; international firms follow global norms.

## Humor and emphasis

- Light, friendly humor is welcome once rapport exists; avoid humor at others' expense.
- Avoid jokes about religion, ethnicity, race or groups (SARA): such content can trigger legal action under blasphemy and electronic information laws.
- Emoji and exclamation marks are common in chat; formal letters are restrained.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Religion**: Indonesia has blasphemy laws; content perceived as insulting Islam or any of the six recognized religions can lead to prosecution and mass protests. Never use religious texts, symbols or figures casually.
- **Communism**: communist ideology and symbols (hammer and sickle) are banned; promoting them is a crime. The 1965 to 1966 killings remain sensitive.
- **Israel**: strong public support for Palestine; avoid Israel-related content in Indonesian marketing.
- **SARA** (ethnicity, religion, race, inter-group relations) is a recognized category of sensitive content.
- Papua independence and the Suharto era are divisive.
- Criminal Code (2022): sex outside marriage and cohabitation can be prosecuted on complaint by family members; Aceh enforces Sharia law (alcohol, gambling, same-sex relations punished).
- Avoid portraying Indonesia only through Bali; do not equate Indonesians with Malaysians.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Left hand is considered unclean: show giving, receiving, eating and pointing with the right hand (or thumb).
- The head is sacred; avoid images of touching children's heads.
- Showing the soles of feet and hands on hips look rude.
- Red and white are national colors (Merah Putih).
- Hindu and Buddhist swastika-like symbols appear on temples in Bali and elsewhere and are religious, not Nazi.

## Variation within the culture

- Java (Javanese and Sundanese) sets the dominant indirect, refined style; Bataks of North Sumatra are known for more direct speech; Bali is Hindu with distinct customs; Aceh applies Sharia law; Eastern Indonesia (Papua, Maluku, Nusa Tenggara) has many Christian communities.
- Jakarta's business elite is cosmopolitan and English-speaking; rural audiences are more traditional and religious.
- Chinese Indonesians have distinct traditions and a history of discrimination; be careful with stereotypes.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No direct criticism or blunt refusal; feedback is private and wrapped in appreciation.
- [ ] Bapak/Ibu (or Pak/Bu) honorifics are used; single-name and no-surname cases are handled.
- [ ] Text is in Bahasa Indonesia (not Malaysian Malay) and calls the language correctly.
- [ ] No content touching SARA, blasphemy, communism or Israel.
- [ ] Imagery is modest; no alcohol, pork or gambling for mainstream audiences; halal status is clear for relevant products.
- [ ] Gestures and imagery use the right hand; no soles of feet.
- [ ] Time zones (WIB/WITA/WIT) are specified; deadlines allow some flexibility and are followed up.
- [ ] Dates, numbers and currency (Rp, juta/miliar) follow Indonesian conventions.
- [ ] Campaign timing considers Ramadan, Lebaran and Friday prayers.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (direct Western feedback):

> Your proposal has several mistakes. Fix the pricing table and resend it today.

**After** (tailored for an Indonesian reader, in English):

> Dear Pak Andi,
>
> Thank you very much for the proposal and the effort your team put into it. We noticed a few points in the pricing table that we would like to align together, especially the delivery costs. Would it be possible to review them with us this week? We would be happy to have a short call at a time convenient for you.
>
> Warm regards,

**Why:** Saying "mistakes" and giving an order threatens face. Appreciation, "align together" and a collaborative offer keep harmony while still making the correction clear.
