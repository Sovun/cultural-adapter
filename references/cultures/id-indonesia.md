# Indonesia

## In brief

Indonesia is the world's largest Muslim-majority country and an archipelago of hundreds of ethnic groups; most etiquette advice describes Javanese norms, while Bali (Hindu), Christian communities, Bataks and Chinese Indonesians differ. Text should be gentle and harmony-seeking: people rarely say no outright, avoid open conflict and may tell a senior what they hope to hear. Rank and age show in every message through Bapak/Ibu and through who is addressed first. Advertising, job ads, prices and product labels are tightly regulated, so a direct translation of Western copy often breaks a rule as well as a norm.

## Voice and tone

- Gentle, polite and warm, without pressure, urgency words or "!!". Harmony (rukun) counts for more than winning the point.
- Call the language "Indonesian" or "Bahasa Indonesia", not just "Bahasa" ("language"). It differs from Malaysian Malay in vocabulary and spelling; do not reuse Malaysian copy.
- Formal Indonesian for letters, contracts and B2B. Consumer and app copy is friendly and short ("perlu", not "memerlukan"); slang only where the brand truly speaks that way.
- Many readers use English as a second language and keep their indirect style in it: short sentences, no idioms.
- Prefer neutral job nouns ("pegawai", not "karyawan/karyawati") and "penyandang disabilitas".

## Addressing people

- Most Indonesians have no family name: one name (Suparman) or several given names. Do not force first/last fields or treat the last word as a surname. Some groups do use clan names (Batak marga such as Sitompul).
- "Bapak Budi" / "Pak Budi", "Ibu Sari" / "Bu Sari" for anyone older, senior or a client; first names alone only among peers or after an invitation. Asking how someone prefers to be addressed is polite.
- Capitalize Bapak, Ibu and "Anda" when they address the reader; lower case looks careless.
- In consumer copy pick "Anda" (polite) or "kamu" (youthful) and keep it. Brands often call customers "Kak" in chat. A company speaking of itself says "kami" (excludes the reader), not "kita" (includes them).
- Formal letters: "Yth. Bapak Budi Santoso" (Yth. = Yang terhormat), "Dengan hormat," as opening; "Kepada Yth. Bapak/Ibu" when the name is unknown. Close with "Hormat saya," (individual), "Hormat kami," (company), "Salam hormat," or "Terima kasih," then full name and position.
- Keep titles in names and signatures: H. (Haji), Hj. (Hajjah), Dr., Ir., S.E., M.M.
- In English: "Dear Pak Budi" or "Dear Mr Budi", using the name the person signs with.

## Structuring a message

- Greeting with title, a wellbeing line ("saya berharap Bapak/Ibu dalam keadaan sehat"), and "Assalamualaikum" only for Muslim readers who use it.
- Specific subject line. One line of context, then the purpose in the first paragraph, phrased as a request ("Kami mohon bantuan Bapak untuk..."). End with the action and a date, then "Terima kasih atas waktu dan perhatian Bapak/Ibu."
- WhatsApp is the daily business channel; email is for formal records, contracts and foreign partners. A read but unanswered WhatsApp can feel like a slight, and silence in a group chat does not mean agreement: ask people one to one.
- Do not write over your contact's head to their boss. Leave room for consensus; no ultimatums.
- Meetings may end with points vague: send a short written recap of decisions and next steps. Sensitive issues go by phone or in person, not in writing.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Never shame anyone in front of others or in a cc-heavy email. Criticism is private, indirect and wrapped in appreciation.
- "Ya" often means "I hear you". "Insya Allah" (God willing) may be a polite maybe; for devout writers it is also sincere, so never mock it.
- Juniors tell superiors what they want to hear ("Asal Bapak Senang"), so bad news travels up slowly. Ask for concerns privately and with open questions.
- Avoid raised voices, sarcasm or laughing at mistakes. Frame problems as something to solve together and offer a face-saving alternative. Serious disputes may go through a trusted intermediary.
- Set clear expectations at the start, because correcting someone later is hard.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Themes that work: family, togetherness (kebersamaan), community, local pride and the festive seasons (Ramadan, Lebaran, also Christmas, Chinese New Year, Nyepi in Bali). Ramadan copy should be heartfelt and spiritual in tone rather than hard selling; celebrity ads score low on trust.
- **Ad code (Etika Pariwara Indonesia) and Consumer Protection Law 8/1999**: superlatives ("terbaik", "paling", "nomor satu"), "100%", "asli" (genuine) and "satu-satunya" (the only) need accountable proof. "Gratis" only if nothing else must be paid. Every asterisk and "Syarat dan ketentuan berlaku" must say where the terms are. Comparisons only on like-for-like technical points, never belittling a competitor. Testimonials must be real, individual and backed by a signed statement. Ad promises bind the seller.
- No religious figures or state officials in commercial ads; do not exploit religious rituals. No eroticism or objectifying women.
- **Halal** (Law 33/2014 and PP 42/2024): mandatory for medium and large food and beverage businesses since 17 October 2024 and for micro and small ones from 18 October 2026 (check current status for other categories). Write "halal" or show the Label Halal Indonesia only with a BPJPH certificate; non-halal products must be labelled as such.
- **Language Law 24/2009**: product information, labels and manuals must be in Indonesian; agreements with Indonesian parties need an Indonesian version (bilingual contracts are standard, and an English-only contract has been voided in court).
- **Prices** must be in rupiah only; showing rupiah next to a foreign currency is banned (Bank Indonesia rule). USD quotes fit cross-border B2B offers only.
- Modest imagery: no revealing clothing, alcohol, pork or gambling for mainstream audiences; avoid LGBT themes.
- CV: photo common locally; GPA (IPK) when above 3.00; Indonesian CV for local firms, state companies and government, English for multinationals. Date of birth, religion and marital status only if asked. Readers are modest about achievements, so keep results factual.
- **Job ads**: a Manpower Ministry circular (SE M/6/HK.04/V/2025) tells employers not to set age caps, "berpenampilan menarik" (attractive appearance), marital status, height, ethnicity or skin colour filters unless the job truly needs them; the ad code also bars unexplained sex, religion or race filters and inflated pay. Old templates with "usia maks. 25 tahun" or "belum menikah" are out.

## Humor and emphasis

- Light, friendly humour once rapport exists; never at someone's expense. Obvious playful hyperbole is allowed in ads; parody of a competitor's ad is not.
- No jokes about religion, ethnicity, race or groups (SARA), or mocking the President or Vice President.
- Emoji, stickers and "!" are common in chat; formal letters are restrained. Emphasize with structure, not capitals.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **SARA** (ethnicity, religion, race, inter-group relations) is the recognized danger zone. Online hate on these grounds is a crime under the ITE Law (Art. 28(2)); religious insult falls under the Criminal Code's chapter on crimes against religion. Never use religious texts, symbols or figures casually. Six religions are officially recognized.
- **Criminal Code (Law 1/2023, in force since 2 January 2026)**: attacking the honour of the President or Vice President (Art. 218) is a crime, prosecuted only on their own complaint; policy criticism is not covered. At last check the Constitutional Court had struck down the offence of insulting the government or state institutions (Art. 240-241, August 2026). Sex outside marriage and cohabitation (Art. 411-412) are prosecuted only on complaint by a spouse, parent or child.
- **ITE Law defamation (Art. 27A)**: after a 2025 Constitutional Court ruling only individuals can complain, not institutions or companies; naming a real person online is still risky.
- **Communism**: spreading it is a crime (Criminal Code Art. 188); no hammer and sickle. The 1965 to 1966 killings, Papua and the Suharto era are divisive.
- **State symbols (Law 24/2009)**: the flag may not be used in commercial ads or overprinted with logos, the anthem may not be used in ads, and logos may not resemble the Garuda emblem.
- **Personal data (PDP Law 27/2022)**: consent must be recorded, separated from other purposes and in plain language. Fines reach up to 2% of annual revenue; a 2026 regulation (PP 33/2026) on fine calculation was reported by the press, check current status.
- **Israel**: strong public support for Palestine; avoid Israel-related content in Indonesian marketing.
- Aceh applies Sharia law (alcohol, gambling, same-sex relations punished). Avoid portraying Indonesia only through Bali or equating it with Malaysia. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Show giving, receiving and eating with the right hand; point with the thumb, not the index finger.
- The head is sacred: avoid images of touching heads. Soles of feet, feet on tables and hands on hips look rude.
- Red and white are the national colours; Independence Day (17 August) campaigns use the colours, not the flag itself.
- Hindu and Buddhist swastika-like symbols on temples are religious, not Nazi. Batik is a proud national symbol. Knives and scissors as gifts suggest cutting ties.
- No banknotes at 1:1 scale in ads; children may not advertise products unsuitable for them.

## Variation within the culture

- Javanese and Sundanese norms set the indirect, refined mainstream, strongest in civil service. Bataks of North Sumatra and Minang readers are often described as more direct.
- Bali is Hindu with its own calendar; Aceh applies Sharia; Eastern Indonesia and North Sumatra have large Christian communities.
- Jakarta professionals and multinationals are more used to Western directness and English; outside big cities regional identity shapes tone and formality more.
- Chinese Indonesians often use Western given names alongside Indonesian ones; avoid stereotypes, given a history of discrimination.
- Younger and digital-economy staff give feedback more openly but still use Bapak/Ibu with seniors.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No direct criticism or blunt refusal; feedback is private and wrapped in appreciation; nothing goes over the contact's head.
- [ ] Bapak/Ibu (capitalized) are used; single names are handled; "kami" for the company; one of "Anda"/"kamu" throughout.
- [ ] Text is Indonesian, not Malaysian Malay; product information and labels are in Indonesian.
- [ ] Every superlative, "100%", "asli" or "gratis" has proof; asterisks and "S&K berlaku" say where the terms are; no belittled competitors.
- [ ] Prices are in rupiah only; formats use 1.234,56, Rp, "M" = miliar and Indonesian month abbreviations.
- [ ] "Halal" or the halal label appears only with a BPJPH certificate.
- [ ] No SARA content, religious figures, mockery of the President, communism, Israel references, flag, anthem or Garuda-like logo.
- [ ] Job ads carry no age, appearance, marital, sex, religion or ethnicity filters without a job reason; pay is not inflated.
- [ ] Consent wording is separate and plain; imagery is modest and uses the right hand.
- [ ] Time zones are named; deadlines allow slack, avoid Ramadan, Lebaran and Friday midday, and are confirmed in writing.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (direct Western feedback):

> Your proposal has several mistakes. Fix the pricing table and resend it today.

**After** (tailored for an Indonesian reader, in English):

> Dear Pak Andi,
>
> Thank you very much for the proposal and the effort your team put into it. We noticed a few points in the pricing table that we would like to align together, especially the delivery costs. Would it be possible to review them with us this week? We would be happy to have a short call at a time convenient for you.
>
> Warm regards,

**Why:** Saying "mistakes" and giving an order threatens face. Appreciation, "align together" and a collaborative offer keep harmony while still making the correction clear, and the call moves the sensitive detail out of writing.
