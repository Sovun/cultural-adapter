# Qatar

## In brief

Qatar has two readerships. Qatari nationals, a small minority of residents, expect respectful Arabic or courteous formal English, correct titles, relationship before business and no public criticism. The expatriate majority works mostly in plain international English and is more task-focused, but still writes inside Qatari law and protocol. Arabic is legally protected: government correspondence, trade names, product information and prices must be in Arabic, with other languages beside it. Do not reuse Saudi or UAE copy unchanged: the work week, alcohol rules, media and hiring laws, time zone and currency all differ, and the 2017-2021 blockade by neighbouring states makes Gulf-generic framing a sensitive point.

## Voice and tone

- Respectful, calm and polished. A patient follow-up comes across as professional; an impatient one weakens the writer.
- Formal Arabic text uses Modern Standard Arabic. Gulf dialect fits social posts and casual ads aimed at Qataris, not letters, contracts or corporate pages. Some Qatari readers are sensitive to Arabic being sidelined by English.
- Arabic must be written or transcreated by native writers. Literal translation keeps English word order and repeats "your" on every noun, which sounds stiff; in UI text use the plain imperative ("افتح التطبيق") rather than "قم بفتح".
- English for Qatar is often a lingua franca between South Asian, Arab, Filipino and Western readers: short sentences, no idioms or slang.
- Specific praise for Qatar's development, culture or a recent national achievement works as a warm opener; keep it sincere, not scripted.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name, "bin" or "bint" (son or daughter of) plus the father's name, then the family or tribal name, often with "Al" (Hamad bin Khalid Al-Kuwari). Never shorten "Abdulaziz" to "Abdul"; "bin" is not a middle name.
- English: "Dear Dr Hamad Al-Kuwari" in a first message, then "Dear Dr Hamad". Use Eng., Dr or Mr/Ms before the given name.
- Ruling family members are "Sheikh" / "Sheikha" + given name. The Amir is "His Highness" (صاحب السمو); ministers and senior officials are "His/Her Excellency" (سعادة). Check the right form before writing.
- Arabic letter: "السيد/ حمد الكواري المحترم" (Mr Hamad Al-Kuwari, respected), then "السلام عليكم ورحمة الله وبركاته" (peace and God's mercy and blessings be upon you) and "تحية طيبة وبعد" (kind greetings, and then...). Senior readers are addressed in the respectful plural.
- Closings: "وتفضلوا بقبول فائق الاحترام والتقدير" (please accept our highest respect and appreciation) for formal letters, "مع خالص التحية" (with sincere greetings) for everyday mail. In English, "Kind regards" or "Best regards".
- In group mail or cc lines, the most senior person comes first. Do not ask about a counterpart's wife or daughters; a general "I hope your family is well" is fine. Do not assume handshakes across genders in event copy.

## Structuring a message

1. Greeting, a courtesy line (wellbeing, thanks for a meeting or hospitality).
2. Short context, then the purpose and the request.
3. Supporting facts and figures, kept concise; detail in attachments.
4. A next step framed as an invitation ("Would a short meeting next week suit you?") and good wishes.

- A first email that opens with a hard ask is premature; important matters are raised by phone or in person, and email confirms.
- Send a polite recap after meetings and reconfirm appointments on the day. Slow replies are normal; follow up gently, never as a complaint.
- Mail for government or semi-government bodies goes in Arabic or bilingual Arabic-English; state contracts require an Arabic version.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Phrase disagreement as a question or an option ("Could we also consider...?"), sent privately to the person concerned.
- No public correction: no reply-all criticism, no copying a senior on a complaint about a colleague.
- When a topic returns for the third time, treat it as careful review and answer without exasperation.
- "Inshallah" (God willing) can mean real intent, polite deflection or uncertainty. Read the context and confirm the next step softly.
- Online insult or defamation, and publishing details of someone's private or family life, are criminal offences even when true. Keep reviews, "name and shame" posts and complaint copy factual and private.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Trust, introductions through known contacts and the sender's reputation persuade more than feature lists. Never hint at favours or inducements.
- Consumer law bans false or misleading ads; prices must be clear and match what is charged, and instalment offers must show the cash price, instalments and total. Discount sales need a licence; fake reductions get punished.
- Prices, product information, trade names and trademarks must appear in Arabic, with the Arabic at least as prominent as any other language; outdoor ads must be in Arabic too. This applies to online and social sellers.
- Marketing email and SMS need prior explicit consent, a clear sender, a marketing label and a working opt-out. Get documented consent before publishing photos or video of identifiable people (a separate offence since 2025).
- Ramadan copy works best around family, charity and reflection; National Day (18 December) and National Sport Day (February) are strong themes. Check before using the national flag commercially.
- Job ads: Qatarization law gives Qatari nationals priority, then children of Qatari mothers. "Qatari nationals preferred" is lawful and common; do not paste Western boilerplate that contradicts it. Ads are usually in English (Arabic for government roles), and salary is rarely shown.
- CVs: two pages for most professionals, up to three for senior or technical roles; clean single-column PDF. Expats put nationality and visa or QID status near the top. State Arabic level and any local licence for regulated professions. A modest headshot is common for local private firms, hospitality and client-facing roles; multinationals and tech often prefer none, so follow the ad. Date of birth and marital status are still common but optional.

## Humor and emphasis

- Leave humour out of first contacts. Jokes about religion, the ruling family, the state, national symbols, gender or sex are a legal risk, not only a taste issue.
- Football, falconry, travel and Qatar's growth are safe, friendly topics.
- Emphasise with structure and bold, not capitals or exclamation marks.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Religion:** insulting God, the prophets, the Quran or the major faiths is a crime with long prison terms, as are images or symbols that may offend Islam. No decorative use of sacred text.
- **State and rulers:** no criticism of the Amir, the ruling family or the state. The 1979 press law still frames media content as far as could be checked; a replacement drafted in 2019 had no confirmed enactment. The cybercrime law covers online insult, privacy and false news that threatens public order.
- **Alcohol:** sold only to adults through licensed hotel venues and a single state-controlled outlet for permit holders; public drinking is an offence. No explicit ad statute was found, but in practice do not advertise alcohol or show drinking to the Qatari public. This sits between Saudi Arabia (full ban) and the UAE (wider licensing).
- **Morals:** gambling and sex outside marriage are crimes; show no betting themes, unmarried couples in intimate settings or public affection. Models cover shoulders and knees.
- **Ramadan:** no eating, drinking or smoking in public in daylight; avoid daytime food imagery in that month.
- **Regional politics:** keep out of the 2017-2021 blockade, Israel and Gulf rivalries; do not frame Qatar as an extension of Saudi or UAE markets.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The flag is maroon and white; maroon is the national colour and dominates National Day visuals. Green is linked to Islam.
- Show giving, receiving and eating with the right hand; avoid shoe soles and pointing fingers in imagery.
- Non-Qatari models should not wear Qatari national dress. Falconry, football and Qatar's modern development are positive themes.

## Variation within the culture

- **Qatari nationals:** Arabic-first for official and family-facing content, strong protocol, relationship-led. Many young Qataris were educated in English and write it fluently, yet value Arabic being treated as the primary language.
- **Expat professionals:** mixed Arab, South Asian, Filipino and Western readers; English working language, faster and more task-focused mail. Mass-market and worker-facing campaigns may also need South Asian languages or Tagalog.
- **Sectors:** government and semi-government bodies expect Arabic and formal protocol; family conglomerates centralise decisions at the top; multinationals may decide at head office abroad. Hospitality and retail weigh personal presentation in hiring more than technical sectors.
- **Free zones:** the Qatar Financial Centre and some free zones have their own rules (for example on data protection), so legal wording differs from onshore Qatar.
- Many Qatari women work, most of them in government; show them professionally and modestly without assumptions either way.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The reader is identified: Qatari national or government (Arabic-first, formal) versus expat business (plain international English).
- [ ] Titles and names are right: Sheikh/Sheikha, Excellency, Dr, Eng.; full name first, no shortened fragments.
- [ ] One courtesy line precedes business; no hard ask, ultimatum or "ASAP" pressure.
- [ ] Refusals and criticism are private, reasoned and framed as options; nothing corrects a senior publicly.
- [ ] Arabic is MSA for formal text, native-written, RTL-checked; prices, product data and trade names appear in Arabic at least as prominently as English.
- [ ] Copy is not recycled from Saudi or UAE campaigns: work week, time zone, currency and legal rules are Qatar's.
- [ ] No alcohol, drinking, gambling, public affection or revealing clothing in public-facing imagery.
- [ ] No criticism or jokes about religion, the Amir, the ruling family or the state; no blockade or regional politics.
- [ ] Photos of identifiable people have documented consent; marketing messages have consent, sender ID and opt-out.
- [ ] Claims and discounts are true and verifiable; no denigration of competitors.
- [ ] Job ads respect Qatarization (national priority is lawful); no contradictory equal-opportunity boilerplate.
- [ ] CVs for Qatar show nationality and visa/QID status; photo follows sector and ad.
- [ ] Dates, digits and currency follow one convention (DD/MM/YYYY, QAR); Western or Arabic-Indic digits, not both.
- [ ] Timing avoids Friday midday, Ramadan daytime clashes, Eid and announced holidays.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (a direct supplier follow-up to a semi-government contact):

> Hi Hamad, we still haven't received your sign-off. If it doesn't come by Thursday, we'll have to move our engineers to another client. Please reply ASAP.

**After** (tailored, in English with an Arabic greeting):

> Dear Eng. Hamad,
>
> As-salamu alaykum. Thank you again for your time at Sunday's meeting and for the team's helpful comments on the design.
>
> We have updated the proposal to reflect them and attached the revised version. To keep our engineers reserved for your project, it would help us to receive your approval, or any further questions, by Thursday 22 October. If it is easier, I would be glad to visit your office to walk through the changes.
>
> With best regards,

**Why:** The deadline and its reason stay, but the threat becomes a practical constraint, the request leaves room for questions, and an offer to meet in person respects the relationship-first, face-to-face style. The title with the given name, the Islamic greeting and the thanks for the meeting match what a Qatari reader expects; an expat project manager would also read it as clear.
