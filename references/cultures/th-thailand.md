# Thailand

## In brief

Thai professional readers expect a calm, courteous and face-saving tone. Refusals and criticism are rarely spelled out, and a polite "yes" may only mean the message was received. Rank and age shape the wording, but people are addressed by given name with "Khun", never by surname. The monarchy is protected by one of the strictest insult laws in the world, and Buddhist imagery carries real legal and public risk, so these two areas override any creative idea. Thai-language documents date years in the Buddhist Era (2026 = 2569).

## Voice and tone

- Calm, gentle, considerate. The ideal is "jai yen" (cool heart): even complaints stay composed. Sarcasm, crude words and visible impatience damage the writer's standing.
- Light warmth and a pleasant feel ("sanuk", fun) help; a cold, transactional tone feels unfriendly.
- Two Thai registers: official writing with fixed formulas, and corporate or consumer writing that is polite but plain. Over-formal Thai in marketing or UI sounds stiff or even domineering; use everyday words and adapt the meaning instead of translating line by line.
- Many readers handle English as a second language: short sentences, no idioms or slang. Read Thai senders' English for what it implies, not just what it says.
- Errors and machine-translated Thai are noticed fast and hurt credibility.

## Addressing people

- Thai names: given name first, then a long family surname. Address by "Khun" + given name ("Dear Khun Somchai"), in Thai and English. Never use the surname alone, and fix mail-merge templates that produce "Dear Mr Wongsuwan".
- Most people have a short nickname used at work. Use it (often with Khun) only after the person introduces themselves that way.
- "Phi" (older sibling) and "nong" (younger sibling) mark closeness and relative age between colleagues; outsiders should wait to be invited into them.
- Keep titles: "Ajarn" (teacher, lecturer), "Dr." + given name, professional titles. Very senior officials receive higher honorifics, and official letters to them use stronger salutation and closing forms; check protocol before writing to them.
- Formal Thai letter: เรียน (rian, "Dear") + Khun + name or position (เรียน ฝ่ายบุคคล, "Dear HR department"); close with ขอแสดงความนับถือ ("respectfully yours"). With no name, address the team or role, not "To whom it may concern".
- In emails and chat, Thai writers end polite sentences with ครับ (khrap, male) or ค่ะ / คะ (kha, female); leaving them out reads curt. Formal letters and UI text omit them. Brands pick one voice and avoid gendered "I" (ผม male, ดิฉัน female) in UI; ฉัน or omission is neutral.
- English sign-offs: "Best regards" or "Kind regards"; include full name and position.

## Structuring a message

1. Subject naming the topic (เรื่อง in Thai letters).
2. Salutation and a short greeting or thanks; skipping both looks rude even in a short email.
3. Reason or background first. Thai letters open with ด้วย ("with regard to"), เนื่องจาก ("because") or ตามที่...นั้น ("further to..."), then state the purpose: จึงเรียนมาเพื่อโปรดพิจารณา ("therefore submitted for your kind consideration").
4. The request, complete and easy to act on: bullets, exact dates, attachments named.
5. Thanks and openness to discuss, then the sign-off.

- Send agendas and material ahead; decisions often form in internal consultation afterwards. Follow calls with a short written summary.
- Raise sensitive matters by phone or in person and use email to confirm.
- In a reply that declines a request, phrase the subject as a neutral noun ("Request for budget approval"), not "Not approved".

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Avoid a flat "no". Thank, give a reason outside the person's control, and offer an alternative or a later chance. ขออภัย ("we apologize") and "not yet able to" keep the door open.
- Decode soft signals: "we will try", "maybe", silence or a postponed answer often mean no; "yes" can mean "I heard you". Confirm specific next steps instead of relying on agreement.
- Criticism is private. Never reply-all with a correction or copy a superior on a complaint; that causes loss of face for everyone.
- "Kreng jai" (reluctance to impose or to displease) keeps juniors from asking questions or reporting problems. Ask specific, low-stakes questions and offer a private channel; "Any objections?" gets silence.
- Frame problems as shared and solvable, acknowledge effort first, and leave the other side a graceful exit ("perhaps there was a misunderstanding").
- Suggest rather than correct when writing to seniors. Bangkok multinationals and staff educated abroad accept clearer, fact-based feedback, still in private.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- B2B: trust, introductions, reliability and long-term support persuade; Thai-language materials signal commitment.
- Consumer advertising often tells emotional stories of family, gratitude and kindness, or uses broad slapstick humour. Family and community benefit outperform individual-status appeals. LINE, Facebook, TikTok and YouTube dominate; promotions, discounts and reviews drive buying.
- Consumer protection law bans false or exaggerated ad claims, and authorities can demand proof: drop "best", "No. 1" or "100%" without evidence. Cosmetics and food may not claim therapeutic, slimming or curing effects; celebrities have been charged personally.
- Alcohol advertising has been banned since November 2025 except factual information, including influencer and celebrity endorsement. Prize draws need a permit and published rules. AI-edited or AI-generated ad images now need a visible label. Check current rules before launch.
- Criminal defamation covers companies and published ads, so comparative ads or public replies naming a competitor or a reviewer are risky.
- Data protection law requires a lawful basis, usually consent, and clear purposes. Consent requests should be separate from other terms and in plain language; religion, health and criminal records are sensitive data. Enforcement and fines are active.
- CV: local CVs often include a photo, date of birth or age, sometimes marital status or religion; multinationals expect less. Show results and qualifications factually; foreign degrees are respected.
- Job ads: local ads commonly state age range and gender. Foreign employers increasingly drop these and should consider their home-market rules; legal limits are a pointer, not advice.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour is welcome once rapport exists, but never at someone's expense and never about the monarchy, Buddhism or monks.
- Emphasis comes from politeness and clear repetition of the request, not capitals, "URGENT" or exclamation marks.
- Thai script has no spaces between words; a space marks a clause or sentence break and there is no sentence-final period. English punctuation pasted into Thai looks foreign.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Monarchy:** Criminal Code Section 112 punishes defaming, insulting or threatening the King, Queen, Heir-apparent or Regent with 3 to 15 years in prison per count. Courts have applied it to past monarchs, anyone can file a complaint, and online posts and shares have been prosecuted, often together with computer-crime charges. Foreigners are not exempt. Keep royal images, names, symbols, royal vocabulary and the number 112 out of commercial, humorous or casual content; consumer protection rules also restrict monarchy-related content in ads.
- **Section 112 reform** is itself politically explosive (a major party was dissolved over it in 2024). Take no position.
- **Buddhism:** insulting objects or places of religious veneration is a crime. Never use the Buddha as decoration, tattoo motif, product design or at foot level; a Buddha head shown alone and sexualized poses near statues draw strong public campaigns.
- **Monks:** show them with respect; women do not touch monks. Monks in commercial ads have drawn complaints, so use them rarely and with care.
- **Deep South:** the southern border provinces have a Malay-Muslim majority and a long-running security conflict; avoid casual references and Buddhist-only imagery there.
- Avoid hints of corruption or favours, and politics in general. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The head is the highest part of the body, the feet the lowest: no feet pointed at people, on tables or near Buddha or royal images, and no touching heads.
- Hand things over with the right hand or both hands. The "wai" (palms together, slight bow) is started by the junior person; handshakes are common with foreigners.
- Nine is lucky (it sounds like "step forward"); businesses choose it for dates and numbers.
- Black gift wrapping is linked to mourning; yellow and gold read positively.
- Restrained romance: public affection looks out of place in mainstream ads.

## Variation within the culture

- **Bangkok vs provinces:** Bangkok business is faster and more international, with English widely used; provincial readers expect a gentler, more traditional tone and Thai-language material.
- **Regions:** the Northeast (Isan) speaks a Lao-related language at home, the North has its own dialect and traditions, and the Deep South is largely Malay-speaking and Muslim, where alcohol, pork and dress in imagery need care.
- **Sino-Thai business families** play a big role in commerce; Chinese New Year and Chinese number symbolism matter to many.
- **Sector and age:** government follows the official correspondence rules strictly; tech, startups and multinationals use a plainer voice and more direct feedback. Younger professionals want quicker feedback and flatter teams but still avoid challenging seniors openly.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Every person is addressed as "Khun" (or a title) + given name, never by surname alone.
- [ ] No flat refusal or blunt criticism; a "no" carries thanks, a reason and an alternative.
- [ ] Criticism is not sent to a group or with seniors copied.
- [ ] The text opens with a greeting and brief context before the request; no push for a decision in a first contact.
- [ ] Requests to juniors invite questions explicitly; decisions are confirmed as specific next steps.
- [ ] Tone is calm: no capitals, "!!", "URGENT", sarcasm or impatience.
- [ ] No mention, image, symbol or joke touching the monarchy or the number 112 in commercial or casual content.
- [ ] No Buddha images as decoration or product design, nothing at foot level, no casual use of monks.
- [ ] Imagery shows no pointed feet or touched heads; items are handed with the right or both hands.
- [ ] Claims are provable: no unsupported superlatives, no health or slimming claims for cosmetics or food, no alcohol promotion, AI-made images labelled.
- [ ] Consent wording is separate from other terms and plain; no sensitive data requested without need.
- [ ] Thai dates use the Buddhist Era, or the era is labelled when CE is used.
- [ ] Thai copy uses natural, plain Thai (not literal translation), Thai punctuation, and a consistent particle or gender-neutral voice.
- [ ] Deadlines are exact and consider Songkran and public holidays.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (direct chaser, copied to the supplier's director):

> Your team missed the deadline again. We need the corrected files by Friday. Explain what went wrong.

**After** (sent only to the contact, in English):

> Dear Khun Narin,
>
> Thank you for all your team's work on the catalogue. Our printer has asked us to confirm the final files soon, so we wanted to check how the corrections are going. Would Friday 23 October be possible on your side? If anything is making this difficult, please let me know and we can look at it together, or arrange a short call.
>
> Kind regards,

**Why:** The deadline and the date stay clear, but blame, the order and the copied director are gone, so nobody loses face. The outside reason (the printer) explains the urgency, and the open invitation lets the contact report a problem despite kreng jai.
