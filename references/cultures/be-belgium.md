# Belgium

## In brief

Belgium has three language communities, and the first decision for any text is which language it goes out in: Dutch for Flanders, French for Wallonia, Dutch or French for Brussels depending on the reader, German in the small eastern community. This profile defaults to Flemish readers (nl-BE) and covers French-speaking Belgium under Variation. Flemish readers share a language with the Netherlands but not its bluntness: they expect "u", a courteous and calm register, clear facts and cushioned refusals. Across both communities, trust comes from reliability, so precise facts, realistic promises and written confirmation work better than enthusiasm. Language use with staff, customers and job applicants is regulated by law, and the language question itself is the most sensitive topic in the country.

## Voice and tone

- Calm, polite, factual. Write the way you would speak to a respected client, not in officialese: everyday words ("vragen" rather than "verzoeken", "over" rather than "aangaande") and active voice.
- Cut stock phrases from old templates such as "Hopende u hiermede van dienst te kunnen zijn" (hoping to have been of service) or "Inmiddels verblijven wij". Flemish readers see them as filler.
- Use Belgian Dutch, not Netherlands Dutch: vocabulary, legal terms and conventions differ ("gsm" for mobile phone, "tegen vrijdag" for "by Friday", "overlopen" for going through a document). Netherlands copy often feels too casual and too pushy in Flanders.
- Admitting a limitation or a risk builds trust; overselling makes readers check everything twice.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, then surname. Surname particles are common (De Smet, Van den Broeck, Vande Velde) and spelled as the person writes them. Many first names are gender-ambiguous (Kim, Dominique); check before writing "heer" or "mevrouw".
- Formal Dutch: "Geachte heer Peeters," / "Geachte mevrouw Janssens,". It is the most distant form, right for official, legal and senior first contact. If you do not know the preferred form, the full name works: "Geachte Kim Janssens,".
- Neutral Dutch business: "Beste mevrouw Janssens," or "Beste Sofie,". "Dag Sofie," is a friendly opener that is far more common in Belgium than in the Netherlands.
- Unknown reader: a role noun ("Beste klant", "Beste collega", "Beste allen"). "L.S." and "Mijne heren" are outdated.
- Function forms survive: "Meester" (fr. "Maître") for lawyers and notaries, "professor", "minister". Academic titles are otherwise rarely used in address.
- Closings: "Met vriendelijke groeten" fits almost everything; "Vriendelijke groeten" or "Hartelijke groeten" warmer; "Hoogachtend" only for very official letters, bad news or a strained relationship. "mvg" or "gr" look sloppy.
- Match the register: "Geachte" with "u" and a full closing, never with "Groetjes". Write "u" and "uw" in lowercase and do not switch between "u" and "je" in one text. A comma after the salutation is most common in Belgium; if you drop it, drop the one after the closing too.

## Structuring a message

1. A subject line that states the point ("Offerte website: prijs en planning"), not one word like "aanvraag".
2. Salutation and one courtesy line ("Hartelijk dank voor uw offerte").
3. The core message in the first or second paragraph; skip openers such as "Naar aanleiding van..." that delay it.
4. Reasons, figures and conditions in short paragraphs or bullets; details in an attachment whose purpose you name.
5. A clear next step with a date, a named contact person and how to reach them.

- Keep an email within one screen and a letter within one A4 page, two at most. Use bold for the core point only; no capitals or underlining.
- After any call or meeting, send a written summary of what was agreed. A deal is not settled until it is in writing, and contracts are read closely. Ask for meetings well in advance and allow time for internal approval.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagree about the issue, calmly, with reasons. Open confrontation in writing reads as rude and is remembered.
- Show willingness to meet halfway; take-it-or-leave-it positions and ultimatums usually stall a deal.
- Flemish speakers soften requests with small words ("even", "eventjes" = just, briefly; "een beetje" = a little). A Dutch-language text with none of this can sound harsh.
- When adapting text from the Netherlands, Germany or Israel, add a reason and a cushion to every refusal or criticism. "Dit is niet goed, doe het opnieuw" (this is not good, redo it) should become a specific point and a request.
- Do not read silence or a polite reply as agreement; ask for explicit confirmation.
- Candidate rejections: short, factual and based on the job requirements.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with facts, figures, references, low risk and proven results. New ideas meet caution, so show that something has worked elsewhere.
- Run separate campaigns per language community. Flemish audiences draw on Dutch-language and English media, French-speaking audiences on France and the wider francophone world; a translation alone is often not enough.
- Advertising is reviewed by a self-regulatory jury against the Belgian advertising code; gender stereotypes and alcohol aimed at young people draw most complaints. Comparative ads must be accurate and compare verifiable features. Price reductions must show the previous lowest price.
- Marketing email and SMS need prior opt-in consent that the sender can prove, plus an easy opt-out in every message.
- CV: one page for juniors, up to two for experienced profiles (Flemish advice allows two, Walloon advice prefers one). A short profile, reverse-chronological experience, education, and a languages section with levels (B2, C1). Photo, date of birth and nationality are optional; leave out marital status, children and health. Write it in the language of the ad.
- Keep CV and cover letter modest and concrete: figures, results, examples of cooperation. Do not criticise former employers.
- Job ads: gender-neutral titles or "M/V/X" ("H/F/X" in French), no age limits, no references to family or pregnancy, no photo request. Replace "steeds beschikbaar" (always available) with concrete hours. Readers compare packages (net pay, company car, meal vouchers, extra month), so state them clearly.
- Pay transparency: French-speaking public employers already face pay-range rules; federal transposition of the EU directive was still pending after its June 2026 deadline. Check current status.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour is dry, ironic and self-deprecating, with a surreal streak. It belongs at the edges of a relationship message, not in an offer or a negotiation.
- Belgians may joke about Belgium; outsiders, especially French or Dutch writers, should not. Do not compare Belgium with France or the Netherlands.
- One exclamation mark at most per message; intensifiers sparingly.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Language communities:** the most sensitive subject. Never write to a Flemish reader in French or a Walloon reader in Dutch; use their language or neutral English. Do not treat Belgium as French-speaking by default or Flanders as part of the Netherlands, and do not take sides on regional politics or autonomy.
- **Language law at work:** documents between employer and staff must be in Dutch in Flanders, French in Wallonia and German in the German-speaking area; in Brussels each employee gets Dutch or French according to their language. The location of the workplace decides. Non-compliant documents can be void, and the Flemish rules also cover English-only staff communication. Invoices issued in Flanders need their mandatory parts in Dutch. Labels are usually bilingual or trilingual.
- **Discrimination:** federal law protects more than a dozen grounds, including age, origin, wealth, language, political and union conviction and physical traits. Hiring questions about marriage, children or pregnancy are not allowed, and inspectors can run test applications.
- **History:** publicly denying or minimising the Holocaust is a criminal offence; incitement to hatred is covered by the anti-racism act. The colonial past in Congo and the monarchy are sensitive; handle with care.
- Avoid politics, religion, income and private life in business messages. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Chrysanthemums are for graves (All Saints' Day, 1 November), not gifts.
- Shared positive references across communities: comics, food, beer, chocolate, cycling, football. Comic characters are licensed property; refer to them, do not reproduce them.
- Each community has its own holiday (Flemish 11 July, French 27 September, German-speaking 15 November) besides the national day on 21 July. Community imagery is not interchangeable; do not present one community's symbols as national.

## Variation within the culture

- **French-speaking Belgium (fr-BE, Wallonia and most of Brussels):** more hierarchical (closer to 4) and more relationship-oriented; decisions rest with the boss after long discussion, and a text can carry more context before the ask. Use "vous", "Madame, Monsieur," for unknown readers and "Bonjour Madame Lambert," in email; close with "Cordialement" or "Bien à vous", and in formal letters with a full formula ("Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées."). Readers expect a warmer, more elaborated tone than Flemish readers, but less flourish than readers in France.
- **Belgian French is not France French:** "septante" (70) and "nonante" (90), while 80 stays "quatre-vingts"; "déjeuner" is lunch and "dîner" or "souper" the evening meal; "GSM" is a mobile phone and "portable" a laptop. Official French-Community texts avoid the median dot ("étudiant·e·s"); use double forms or neutral nouns. France copy needs localising, not just a check.
- **Brussels:** officially bilingual, French-dominant in daily business, very international around the EU institutions. English works as a neutral language; for staff and public communication, use both Dutch and French or each reader's own language. Timekeeping is slightly looser.
- **German-speaking Community (east):** German for staff documents; formal and title-conscious.
- **Flanders inside:** tech, start-ups and youth brands use "je" and more English terms; public sector, banking, law and older readers keep "u". Informal in-between Flemish ("tussentaal") belongs in TV fiction and chat, never in business writing.
- **Language skills:** educated Flemish readers often read English and French well; English copy reaches Flanders more reliably than Wallonia.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The text is in the reader's language (nl-BE, fr-BE, German or neutral English), never Dutch to a Walloon or French to a Fleming.
- [ ] Dutch text uses Belgian Dutch vocabulary and conventions, not Netherlands copy unchanged; French text uses Belgian forms (septante, nonante, GSM).
- [ ] First contact uses "u"/"vous" and Meneer/Mevrouw or Monsieur/Madame plus surname; the pronoun is consistent throughout.
- [ ] Salutation and closing match in register; no "L.S.", "Mijne heren", "mvg" or archaic template phrases.
- [ ] The core message appears in the first or second paragraph and the subject line states it.
- [ ] Claims carry figures, references or evidence; superlatives, hype and "!!" are removed.
- [ ] Refusals and criticism give a reason and a calm, specific request; no ultimatums.
- [ ] Next steps have exact dates and a named contact; nothing presses for an instant decision.
- [ ] Agreements from calls or meetings are confirmed in writing.
- [ ] Staff and HR documents follow the regional language rules; no single English or bilingual template for Brussels staff.
- [ ] Job ads use M/V/X or neutral titles, no age, family, photo or "always available" requirements.
- [ ] Marketing email relies on proven opt-in and has an opt-out; discount claims show the previous price.
- [ ] Nothing comments on the language divide, regional politics, the monarchy or Congo, and nothing compares Belgium to France or the Netherlands.
- [ ] Formats match the locale: nl-BE 17/10/2026, 1.234,50, € before the amount; fr-BE 1 234,50 € and 25 %.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt Netherlands-style email in English):

> Hi Wouter, your quote is too expensive and the delivery date doesn't work for us. Send a new version before Friday or we'll pick another supplier.

**After** (for a Flemish supplier after a first meeting, in Dutch with gloss):

> Dag meneer Wouters,
>
> Hartelijk dank voor uw offerte en voor het gesprek van dinsdag. We hebben ze intern besproken. De technische aanpak spreekt ons aan, maar de prijs ligt ongeveer 12% boven ons budget en een oplevering op 3 maart is voor ons te laat.
>
> Zou u kunnen bekijken of een aangepaste versie mogelijk is, bijvoorbeeld met een gefaseerde oplevering? Als we die tegen vrijdag 13 november ontvangen, kunnen we ze op 18 november aan onze directie voorleggen. Ik bel u graag even om alles te overlopen.
>
> Met vriendelijke groeten,

*(Gloss: "Hello Mr Wouters, thank you for your quote and for Tuesday's meeting. We discussed it internally. We like the technical approach, but the price is about 12% above our budget and delivery on 3 March is too late for us. Could you see whether a revised version is possible, for example with phased delivery? If we receive it by Friday 13 November, we can put it to our management on 18 November. I'd be glad to call you briefly to go through everything.")*

**Why:** Both problems stay explicit and are backed by numbers, but the threat becomes a dated request with a reason (the board meeting), and the offer to call leaves room to negotiate. "Meneer" and "u" fit a first business contact in Flanders, "Dag" and "even" keep it friendly, and "tegen" and "overlopen" mark it as Belgian rather than Netherlands Dutch. A Walloon version would be in French, use "vous" and add a little more context before the request.
