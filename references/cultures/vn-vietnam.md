# Vietnam

## In brief

Vietnamese has no neutral "you": every sentence picks a kinship term (anh, chị, em, ông, bà) that sets the writer's age and status relative to the reader. Get that choice wrong and the error is the first thing a reader notices. Professional writing is calm, courteous and modest. Refusals and criticism come softened and in private, and a "yes" often only means "I have heard you". People are addressed by their given name, the last part of the full name, so "Mr. Minh" is right and "Mr. Nguyen" is wrong. The legal limits are strict and they override style: nothing that criticises the Party or state or mocks national symbols, no unproven "best" or "number one" in ads, ads in Vietnamese, and maps that show Vietnam's islands and call the sea Biển Đông (East Sea).

## Voice and tone

- Calm, respectful and understated. Prefer "good" and "helpful" over "amazing". Harsh or impatient wording costs trust that is slow to rebuild.
- In Vietnamese, write modern, plain sentences. Archaic Sino-Vietnamese phrasing reads stiff, most of all in UI and consumer copy. Word-for-word translation from English sounds robotic and loses the honorifics.
- National content uses northern (Hanoi) standard vocabulary: "bố" over "ba", "gửi" over "gởi". Southern words suit only campaigns aimed at the South.
- Tone marks are essential. Without them words change meaning (ma, má, mà, mạ), and so can names. Write politeness words in full: "Vui lòng..." (please...), "Xin lỗi" or "Rất tiếc" (sorry, regrettably).
- In English for Vietnamese readers, use short sentences with no idioms or sarcasm. Many readers outside the big business centres read English carefully or through a translator.

## Addressing people

- **Name order:** family name, middle name, given name (Trần Thị Mai Anh). Address the person by the given name, with two syllables if it has two: "Ms. Mai Anh", "chị Mai Anh". "Mr. Nguyen" is a classic foreign mistake, since a very large share of people carry that family name. Some people reverse their name in English ("Minh Nguyen"), so check which part is the family name and mirror how they sign.
- The middle names Văn (male) and Thị (female) are dropped in address. Women keep their own family name after marriage.
- **Pronouns in Vietnamese:** for yourself, "tôi" is the neutral form and shows equal professional standing. For the reader, "anh" means a man around your age or older, "chị" a woman around your age or older, "em" someone younger. "Ông" and "bà" are for senior executives, elders and ceremonial letters. If you don't know the reader's age, "anh/chị" is the safe default. Juniors often call themselves "em" inside a company.
- **Groups and customers:** "Quý công ty" (your esteemed company), "Quý khách" (valued customer), "Quý vị" (ladies and gentlemen), "các anh chị" (you all). In UI and generic marketing, don't assume a gender: use "bạn" (friend, you) or "Quý khách". Don't use "bạn" for older readers or senior B2B contacts.
- **Company "we":** "chúng tôi" leaves the reader out (our company), while "chúng ta" includes the reader. Mixing them up changes the meaning.
- **Salutations:** "Kính gửi anh Minh" or "Kính gửi ông Tuấn" (Dear...), or "Kính gửi Phòng Nhân sự Công ty X" when you don't have a name. Keep "Xin chào" and "Chào anh/chị" for people you already know. In English: "Dear Mr. Minh", "Dear Ms. Lan".
- **Sign-offs:** "Trân trọng" (respectfully) is the standard. Warm wishes such as "Chúc anh một ngày tốt lành" (have a good day) suit established contacts. A full signature with your title helps the reader place you.

## Structuring a message

1. A specific subject line. Official letters (công văn) use "V/v" (regarding) before the subject.
2. "Kính gửi" plus name, then one courtesy line ("Cảm ơn anh đã phản hồi", thank you for your reply).
3. Context and the reason, then the request, phrased politely and with a date.
4. A gentle process question if authority is unclear ("Bên anh cần thêm ai duyệt không?", does anyone else on your side need to approve?).
5. Thanks, any attachments named in the body, and "Trân trọng".

- Keep emails short; detail goes in attachments, key documents in Vietnamese and English. Email carries formal business; Zalo follows once there is rapport.
- After a meeting, send a written summary of decisions, owners and dates. A friendly meeting is a step, not a decision.
- Decisions are consensus-based and often involve people who weren't in the room, such as owners or state partners. Don't push for an immediate answer.
- Formal letters from organisations follow a fixed state layout: the national motto block, a reference number, place and date, "Kính gửi:", and a signing block with authority markers (TM., KT., TL.). Many companies copy it.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with thanks, a reason that keeps the reader's face intact, and an alternative. Avoid a bare "no" or "this is wrong".
- Recognise soft refusals: "để tính sau" (let's think about it later), "khó quá", "tôi sẽ cố gắng", vague promises to check, or silence. Silence or a missing objection is not agreement.
- Ask open questions ("Anh thấy có khó khăn gì không?", what difficulties do you see?) instead of yes/no ones that invite a polite "yes". It can help to state that a no is acceptable ("Nếu không được, anh cứ cho tôi biết", if it isn't possible, just let me know).
- Keep criticism private, describe the issue rather than the person, and end on encouragement. Never correct someone in a group thread or with their manager copied.
- Present a complaint as a shared problem: "Chúng ta có một vấn đề nhỏ về..." (we have a small issue with...). A promise counts as binding, so don't write commitments you can't keep.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with track record, certificates, local references, partnership and long-term commitment. Word of mouth, reviews and recommendations from people readers trust carry more weight than self-praise. Don't pitch Vietnam only as a low-cost location. Lead with capability and partnership.
- **Advertising law:** "nhất" (most), "tốt nhất" (best), "duy nhất" (only) and "số một" (number one) are banned without documentary proof, such as a registered market survey or a competition certificate, and the ad must cite it. Comparisons with competitors need legal supporting documents. Ads must not use a person's image without consent, must not mislead, and must not exploit children.
- Ads must be in Vietnamese, apart from brand names, slogans and proper names. Any foreign text must be smaller, at most three quarters of the size, and placed below the Vietnamese. Since 2026, influencers must disclose paid content and online ads must be clearly labelled.
- Tet (Lunar New Year) is the peak season for ads. Family reunion, gratitude and respect for elders work well, and a light touch suits younger audiences.
- **Data consent:** sign-up and cookie text needs explicit consent for each stated purpose. Pre-ticked boxes and bundled consent are not allowed.
- **CV:** short, results-focused and free of exaggeration, jokes or criticism of past employers. A simple professional photo is the norm. Date of birth is common, but current HR advice is to leave out marital status, religion, ID number and health.
- **Job ads:** the labour code bans discrimination by gender, age, ethnicity, marital status, pregnancy, religion, disability and other grounds. Drop lines like "nam, dưới 30 tuổi" (male, under 30) unless the trait is truly part of the job. Offers of team belonging and growth appeal alongside pay.

## Humor and emphasis

- Keep humour out of first contacts, CVs and formal letters. No sarcasm or teasing aimed at the reader.
- Emphasise with structure and clear wording, not capitals, "!!" or emoji. Ads that clash with history, culture or social ethics are prohibited, which limits edgy humour. Sentence case only. English-style Title Case ("Chọn Tất Cả") is wrong in Vietnamese.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **The state and the Party:** online-content and advertising laws prohibit material that opposes the state, distorts history, or insults the nation, flag, emblem, anthem, national heroes, Ho Chi Minh or leaders. Keep politics, the war and national symbols out of commercial copy, and never use them as decoration or for jokes.
- **Sea and maps:** Vietnamese readers call the sea "Biển Đông" and Vietnam's own English uses "East Sea". "South China Sea" in copy for Vietnam reads as taking a side. Any map must show the Hoàng Sa (Paracel) and Trường Sa (Spratly) islands as Vietnamese, and graphics resembling the nine-dash line are unacceptable. A film has been denied a licence over one. Content made for both China and Vietnam cannot share a map.
- Don't use disputed places as examples. Use "người Trung Quốc" (Chinese people) and never slurs.
- The North-South divide, poverty and comparisons with neighbouring countries are sensitive. Avoid regional, ethnic, gender or disability stereotypes in images and copy. Corruption is not a topic to raise with partners. Keep any gift mentions modest.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Red means luck and celebration, and yellow means warmth. Both dominate Tet. Black, or black with white, signals mourning, so avoid it for gifts and festive visuals.
- Yellow chrysanthemums and handkerchiefs are poor gift imagery. Knives, scissors and clocks are unlucky Tet gifts. Chopsticks standing upright in rice evoke funerals.
- 4 sounds like "death" and is avoided. 8 (prosperity) and 9 (longevity) are favoured in prices, phone numbers and product counts.
- The Vietnamese zodiac has the cat where the Chinese one has the rabbit. Don't reuse Chinese New Year assets for Tet.
- In images, avoid the "OK" hand sign, beckoning with one finger and pointing. Showing cards or gifts passed with both hands reads as respectful.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | Day first: 18/10/2026; short 18/10/26. Months are numbered: 18 tháng 10, 2026 (long), "ngày 18 tháng 10 năm 2026" in letters. Official documents write "Hà Nội, ngày 05 tháng 02 năm 2026". |
| Time | 24-hour: 14:30, often written 14h30. In text, "sáng/chiều/tối" (morning/afternoon/evening) replace AM/PM. |
| Numbers | 1.234.567,89 (period groups, comma decimal); 1.500 is one thousand five hundred. |
| Currency | 150.000 ₫, with no decimals. "đ", "VNĐ" and "đồng" are common in everyday text; the ISO code is VND. |
| Percent | 25% (no space). |
| Week starts | Monday. Thứ Hai (Mon) to Thứ Bảy (Sat), Chủ nhật (Sun); short forms T2-T7, CN. |
| Phone | +84 91 234 56 78; nationally 0912 345 678 (10-digit mobiles). Landlines: 024 (Hanoi), 028 (HCMC) + 8 digits. |
| Address | Since July 2025: house number and street, phường/xã (ward/commune), tỉnh/thành phố (one of 34 provinces or cities). No district line. Older documents still show quận/huyện. |
| Lists and quotes | "A, B và C" (no serial comma); “ ” quotation marks. |
| Other | Metric. Tet (late January to mid February) stops business for one to two weeks. |

## Variation within the culture

- **North vs South:** Hanoi and the North lean more formal and hierarchical and are slower to build trust. Ho Chi Minh City and the South are faster, more entrepreneurial and somewhat more direct, though still indirect by Western standards. Kinship terms and vocabulary differ (bố/ba, mẹ/má).
- **Sector:** state bodies and state-owned firms follow official letter templates and formal address. Foreign-invested firms, tech companies and startups use lighter email, first names in English and more open feedback. In family businesses the decision may sit with a family member, whatever the titles say.
- **Age and place:** young urban readers accept a conversational brand voice and "bạn". Older and rural readers expect respectful forms and Vietnamese-language material; English is good in the big business centres and limited elsewhere.
- **Ethnic minorities:** dozens of minority groups have their own languages, mostly in the highlands. Show them respectfully and avoid exotic or stereotyped portrayals.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt, family-name address):

> Mr. Tran, your team missed the deadline again. We need the design files by Friday. No more excuses.

**After** (to Trần Quốc Hùng, a supplier's project lead of similar age, in Vietnamese with gloss):

> Kính gửi anh Hùng,
>
> Cảm ơn anh và đội ngũ đã đồng hành cùng dự án trong tháng qua. Hiện bên tôi vẫn chưa nhận được bộ file thiết kế dự kiến bàn giao ngày 9/10. Khách hàng cần duyệt bản này vào thứ Hai, 26/10, nên anh vui lòng gửi giúp trước thứ Sáu, 23/10. Nếu đang vướng về nhân sự hay thông tin đầu vào, anh cứ cho tôi biết để hai bên cùng tìm cách xử lý.
>
> Trân trọng,

*(Gloss: "Dear Hùng [anh: a respected peer], thank you to you and your team for working with us on the project this past month. We still haven't received the design files due on 9 October. The client needs to approve them on Monday 26 October, so please send them by Friday 23 October. If staffing or input information is holding things up, just tell me and we'll work out a solution together. Respectfully,")*

**Why:** The delay and the new date stay clear. The reader is addressed by his given name with "anh", the problem is described rather than blamed on him, the deadline comes with its reason, and the closing invites him to raise obstacles instead of forcing a hollow "yes".
