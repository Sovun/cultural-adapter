# Israel

## In brief

Mainstream Israeli professional culture is very direct ("dugri", straight talk), informal and fast. First names, short emails and open disagreement with superiors are normal, and elaborate politeness tends to read as evasive or unsure. Confidence ("chutzpah") is valued, especially in tech. Hebrew is gendered, so every text that addresses the reader needs a gender strategy. The society is diverse (secular and religious Jews, Arab citizens, Russian-speaking and Ethiopian immigrants), and the conflict and the war that began in October 2023 are the most charged topics.

## Voice and tone

- Brief, practical, conversational. In Hebrew, prefer everyday words over bureaucratic ones ("להשתמש" not "לעשות שימוש", "עכשיו" not "כעת").
- Rewrite from the intent; literal translation of English marketing produces stiff Hebrew that readers spot as foreign.
- Avoid deferential padding, over-thanking and apologies; "with all due respect" style openers sound odd. Write as if the email may be forwarded: casual, but no slang.
- Hebrew is gendered (verbs, adjectives, "you"). Generic masculine is increasingly read as excluding women. Prefer plural address ("לחצו", "תוכלו"), doubled forms ("עובדות ועובדים"), or gender-free wording ("אפשר ל...", "שלך", past tense). Hebrew language authorities reject slashes ("מהנדס/ת"); use them only as a last resort, and never mix strategies in one text.
- English is widely used in business and tech; Israeli English is short and can read as curt to outsiders.
- Russian (over a million speakers) and Arabic (about a fifth of citizens are Arab) widen consumer reach; some Russian speakers prefer not to be targeted in Russian.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name, surname. Many names are unisex (Tal, Shai, Amit, Yuval), so never guess gender; in Hebrew a wrong guess shows in every verb. Use a nickname or spelling only as the person signs it.
- Business email: "שלום דנה," or the more casual "היי דנה,". Formal or official mail: "לכבוד [name/title]" as a heading, then "שלום רב,"; group mail: "לקוחות יקרים," or doubled "חברות יקרות וחברים יקרים,".
- Closings: "בברכה," (standard), "המשך יום נעים" (warm); "Shabbat shalom" on Friday, "Chag sameach" before holidays.
- In English, "Hi Dana," from the first email. A title and surname fit only a very first formal message, mainly in medicine and academia.
- Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) contacts expect more formality and modesty; they keep honorific address forms secular Hebrew has dropped, and many avoid cross-gender physical contact such as handshakes.
- Arab citizens: a slightly more formal first contact and a short personal opener are a safe default; "Ahlan" or "Salaam" is a friendly touch. Do not apply generic Gulf etiquette.

## Structuring a message

- Point and ask in the first lines; short paragraphs, bullets, a clear bottom line.
- Close with next steps: who does what by when, and any approval requested explicitly.
- Replies and follow-up questions come fast; phone and WhatsApp carry day-to-day work, and money or sensitive issues move to a call.
- "Urgent" is taken seriously; slow replies read as low interest.
- Send an agenda with objectives before meetings; counterparts come prepared. Reconfirm meetings booked at short notice.
- Expect negotiation: the first offer is often an opening position.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement is direct and expected, including upward, and is not a personal attack. Being challenged usually means your input is taken seriously.
- Feedback is blunt and praise sparing; a feedback sandwich hides the message.
- Coded softeners ("that's interesting") get missed: say "I disagree because...". Silence is not agreement.
- Requests and criticism can be direct but land best in a collaborative "we" frame ("I suggest we...").
- When adapting Israeli text for indirect cultures (Japan, UK, Gulf, Thailand), soften substantially.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Word of mouth, peer recommendation and local testimonials persuade; value and practicality beat polish.
- Consumer copy addresses the reader by first name, in the singular or plural, with playful, colloquial language; even banks do it.
- Israelis hate being a "fraier" (sucker): steep discounts read as an inflated base price, and hidden charges trigger distrust.
- Consumer Protection Law: no misleading ads; the regulator can demand proof of any factual claim; advertorials must be labelled; ads to minors may not exploit their inexperience; prices must include VAT and mandatory charges; product labels in Hebrew. Distance sales must disclose seller, price, delivery and cancellation rights.
- Kosher certification is expected by many retailers and consumers for food.
- Respect Shabbat (Friday sunset to Saturday night) and holidays: many businesses close and religious audiences resent sends then. The autumn holidays (September-October) slow everything.
- CVs: one page (two for senior roles), clean, no photo; minimal personal data (city, phone, email); short summary; tailored to each ad with its keywords. Military or national service (unit, role) is commonly listed. A Hebrew CV gets more responses outside tech; cover notes are a few lines or none.
- Job ads: see legal red lines.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour and wordplay are common, even in financial ads; self-important campaigns invite parody.
- Edgy or dark humour is a risk for foreign brands. Outsiders should never joke about the Holocaust, terror, fallen soldiers, hostages or the war.
- Exclamation marks and intensifiers are fine in informal and marketing copy.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Israeli-Palestinian conflict and the war**: the most charged subject. Since October 2023, references to security, hostages, Gaza, Lebanon or Iran are highly sensitive. Terms (West Bank or Judea and Samaria, occupation, settlements, East Jerusalem, Jerusalem as capital) signal a position to every audience. Avoid unless the content is about it; then have local experts review it for each audience. Maps need the same review.
- Security, military service and Holocaust memory are central to Jewish Israeli identity; trivialising them offends. No promotional content on Holocaust Remembrance Day or Memorial Day.
- Avoid antisemitic tropes; blaming Jews collectively for Israeli government actions is widely read as antisemitic.
- Polarised domestic issues: the Haredi draft exemption, religion and state, the judicial system. Stay out in commercial copy.
- Naming signals politics: "Israeli Arabs", "Arab citizens of Israel" and "Palestinian citizens of Israel" read differently. "Arab citizens" or "Arab society" (החברה הערבית) is the neutral business choice. Avoid stereotypes of Druze, Bedouin and Ethiopian Israelis.
- **Spam (Communications Law, s.30A)**: ad email, SMS and automated calls need prior opt-in; silence is not consent. Narrow exception for existing customers offered a chance to refuse, for similar products. Each message must be marked "פרסומת", show sender details and offer free opt-out. Courts award damages per message without proof of harm; class actions are common.
- **Job ads (Equal Employment Opportunities Law)**: write in both genders or gender-inclusive form; no criteria on sex, age, religion, nationality, residence, reserve duty and similar unless the job requires it. Demanding military service or a specific unit is unlawful without real cause, as it excludes Arab and Haredi applicants. Images should show varied people.
- **Privacy (Privacy Protection Law, Amendment 13)**: in force since 14 August 2025. Forms must say truthfully why data is collected, who receives it and whether giving it is mandatory; deceptive collection is now an offence. Fine levels vary by summary; check current status.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Star of David and menorah are national and religious symbols; blue and white are the national colours. Nazi imagery is deeply offensive.
- 18 ("chai", life) is a lucky number; gifts and donations in multiples of 18 are common.
- No pork or meat-with-dairy for Jewish audiences; no pork or alcohol for Muslim audiences.
- Haredi media are far more restrictive: often no images of women, modest dress throughout. Avoid cross-gender contact images for religious audiences.
- Business imagery is smart-casual; suits and ties look foreign outside senior or government settings.

## Variation within the culture

- The dugri style fits secular, native-born Jewish Israelis best; Haredi, Arab and some immigrant readers expect more formality.
- Tel Aviv is secular and tech-driven; Jerusalem more religious and politically charged; Haifa mixed Jewish-Arab.
- Haredi communities have their own media and norms of modesty; mainstream patriotic messaging may not land.
- Russian speakers and Arab citizens follow their own media; one message for "Israel" underperforms.
- Tech is the most informal and English-heavy sector; older institutions keep formal letter frames.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (formal British-style email to an Israeli startup):

> Dear Mr Levi, I hope this message finds you well. I was wondering whether it might be at all possible, at your convenience, to perhaps arrange a brief call in the coming weeks to explore potential synergies.

**After** (tailored for an Israeli reader):

> Hi Yoni, we help B2B SaaS companies cut churn with predictive alerts. I think it could fit Acme's growth plans. Do you have 20 minutes Tuesday or Wednesday next week?

**Why:** Israeli readers want the value and the ask at once, with a first name and a concrete time. The cushioned version sounds unsure and costs them time.
