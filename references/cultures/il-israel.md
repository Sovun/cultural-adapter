# Israel

## In brief

Mainstream Israeli professional culture is famously direct ("dugri", straight talk), informal and fast. First names, short emails and open disagreement with superiors are normal; elaborate politeness can read as evasive or as a sign that something is being hidden. Confidence and assertiveness ("chutzpah") are valued, especially in the startup sector. Israeli society is also diverse (secular and religious Jews, Arab citizens, immigrants from the former Soviet Union and Ethiopia), and the Israeli-Palestinian conflict is the most sensitive topic of all.

## Voice and tone

- Brief, direct, practical. Israelis value substance over form.
- Avoid flowery or overly deferential language; "with all due respect" style padding sounds strange.
- Hebrew is gendered: verbs, adjectives and pronouns change for male and female readers. UI and marketing copy must handle gender (common solutions: both forms with a slash, plural forms, or neutral constructions).
- English is widely used in business and tech; many Israelis write English directly and briefly, which non-Israeli readers may perceive as curt.
- Russian and Arabic versions may be needed for consumer reach (about 1 million Russian speakers; about 20% of citizens are Arab).

## Addressing people

- Names: given name, surname. Many Hebrew names are unisex (Tal, Shai, Amit, Yuval), so do not assume gender from the name.
- Business email in Hebrew opens with "שלום דנה," (Shalom Dana) or "היי דנה,"; there is no equivalent of "Dear" in normal usage. Closing: "בברכה," (regards), "תודה," (thanks).
- In English: "Hi Dana," is normal from the first email. Formal titles (Dr., Prof.) are used in medical and academic contexts.
- Ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) and religious contacts expect more modesty and formality, and may avoid handshakes or one-on-one meetings with the opposite sex.

## Structuring a message

- The point in the first line; short paragraphs; clear action items with owners and dates.
- Expect quick replies and quick follow-up questions; phone and WhatsApp are common.
- Meetings are short and focused; written summaries list action items.
- Expect negotiation: the first offer is often an opening position.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement is direct and expected, including upward. It is not a personal attack.
- Feedback is blunt; praise is sparing. "Not bad" is positive.
- When adapting Israeli source text for indirect cultures (Japan, UK, Gulf states, Thailand), substantial softening is required.
- Israelis dislike being a "fraier" (sucker); messaging that feels manipulative, overpriced or full of hidden terms triggers resistance.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Value, innovation, practicality and personal recommendation persuade. Bold startup-style claims are culturally acceptable but consumers are skeptical of being taken advantage of.
- Israeli Consumer Protection Law prohibits misleading advertising; Hebrew labeling is required for consumer goods; food imports require kosher certification for most retail channels.
- Respect Shabbat (Friday sunset to Saturday night) and Jewish holidays: many businesses close; scheduling campaigns, deliveries or emails during Shabbat can alienate religious audiences. Many Jewish holidays cluster in September and October.
- CVs: one to two pages; military service (unit, rank, role) is commonly listed and relevant; university and tech experience matter. Military service requirements in job ads can indirectly exclude Arab citizens and are sensitive.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor is common, including irony, sarcasm and self-mockery; tall tales (chizbat) are part of social banter.
- Jokes about the Holocaust, terrorism, military losses or the conflict are taboo for outsiders.
- Exclamation marks are fine in informal communication.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Israeli-Palestinian conflict**: the most sensitive topic. Terminology is contested in every direction (West Bank vs Judea and Samaria; Palestinian Authority vs State of Palestine; occupation; settlements; East Jerusalem). Unless the content is about the conflict, avoid it entirely. If unavoidable, have experts review the wording for each audience.
- Jerusalem's status as capital is not broadly internationally recognized; Israeli audiences see it as the capital. Maps and place labels require careful review.
- The Holocaust (Shoah) is a central collective memory; Holocaust Remembrance Day (Yom HaShoah) and Memorial Day (Yom HaZikaron) are solemn; never schedule promotional content on them.
- Antisemitic tropes and imagery must be avoided absolutely. Criticism of Israel framed in collective terms about Jews is antisemitic.
- Religion vs secularism (ultra-Orthodox military exemptions, Shabbat rules) and judicial politics are polarized domestic issues.
- Arab citizens of Israel, Druze, Bedouins and Ethiopian Israelis face distinct issues; avoid stereotypes.
- Religious modesty: imagery for Haredi audiences may require no images of women at all.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Star of David and menorah are national and religious symbols; use with respect.
- Blue and white are national colors.
- 18 (chai, "life") is a lucky number; donations in multiples of 18 are common.
- Imagery including pork or mixing meat and dairy alienates kosher-observant consumers.
- Swastikas and Nazi imagery are deeply offensive.

## Variation within the culture

- Tel Aviv is secular, liberal, international and tech-driven; Jerusalem is more religious and politically charged; Haifa is mixed Jewish-Arab.
- Ultra-Orthodox communities (Bnei Brak, parts of Jerusalem) have very different norms: modesty, separate media, limited internet.
- Russian-speaking Israelis and Arab citizens of Israel have distinct media consumption and cultural references.
- Business culture in multinational and tech firms is close to US style but even more informal.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (formal British-style email to an Israeli startup):

> Dear Mr Levi, I hope this message finds you well. I was wondering whether it might be at all possible, at your convenience, to perhaps arrange a brief call in the coming weeks to explore potential synergies.

**After** (tailored for an Israeli reader):

> Hi Yoni, we help B2B SaaS companies cut churn with predictive alerts. I think it could fit Acme's growth plans. Do you have 20 minutes Tuesday or Wednesday next week?

**Why:** Israeli readers want the value and the ask immediately. The formal, hedged version sounds evasive and wastes their time.
