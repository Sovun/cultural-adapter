# Chile

## In brief

Chilean business writing is courteous, fairly formal and more reserved than that of most neighbours: "usted", "Estimado/a" and a title by default, a short courtesy line, then the purpose. Refusals are rarely a plain "no", so phrases like "lo vamos a ver" or "te confirmo" need decoding, and anything agreed by phone should be confirmed in writing. Modesty counts: facts, numbers and references persuade, self-praise does not. Spoken Chilean Spanish is full of local slang and informal verb forms that do not belong in business text. The dictatorship, the 2019 unrest and the constitutional votes that followed, and the Mapuche conflict are live, divisive topics to keep out of commercial copy.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous, measured, respectful. A calm, collaborative tone works; hard selling, urgency and pushy follow-ups read as disrespect.
- Write Latin American Spanish with Chilean choices where they matter: "computador", "celular", "ustedes" (never "vosotros"). Spain forms ("ordenador", "móvil", "vale") look foreign.
- Spoken Chilean Spanish uses informal voseo verb endings with "tú": "¿cómo estái?", "¿querís?", "¿cachái?" (get it?). These belong to friends and family; in business text they sound sloppy or vulgar. The pronoun "vos" itself often sounds rude, unlike in Argentina.
- Local slang ("al tiro" right away, "pega" job, "fome" boring, "bacán" great, "po") should stay out of business mail. A foreign brand sprinkling it to sound local usually reads forced; consumer copy can use a little if a native writer checks it.
- Diminutives ("un momentito") soften speech and chat but look too casual in formal writing. Drop exclamation marks carried over from English UI ("¡Error!"); a plain sentence reads more professional.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s), paternal surname, maternal surname (Catalina Fuentes Araya). Address by the first surname: "Sra. Fuentes", never the second alone. Married women keep their own surnames; do not add "de" plus the husband's name.
- Titles at first contact: "Ingeniero/a", "Doctor/a", "Abogado/a" plus surname where known; otherwise "Sr./Sra." plus surname. "Don/Doña" plus first name shows respect to older or senior people.
- Salutations end with a colon: "Estimado Sr. Fuentes:", "Estimada Ingeniera Soto:", "Estimados/as:" for a group. Once there is contact, "Hola, Catalina:" is fine.
- "Usted" is the safe default regardless of the reader's status. Peers and younger people switch to "tú" fast; mirror them and keep one form per text.
- Openers: "Junto con saludar, me dirijo a usted para..." (along with greeting you, I am writing to...) is common in institutional mail; it can sound formulaic, so "Espero que se encuentre bien" or a direct "Le escribo para..." also work.
- Closings: "Saludos cordiales" (standard), "Atentamente" or "Saluda atentamente" (formal), "Un abrazo" (established contacts). A bare "Saludos" can feel curt to a new contact.

## Structuring a message

1. Subject line naming the topic.
2. Salutation and one courtesy line.
3. Purpose early, then reasons in short paragraphs; detail in attachments.
4. Next step with owner and exact date, plus an offer to talk or meet.
5. "Quedo atento/a a sus comentarios" (I look forward to your comments) and the sign-off.

- Important or delicate points are raised by phone or in person and confirmed by email. Frame the recap as alignment ("Para quedar alineados, le resumo los próximos pasos..."), not as distrust.
- Propose meetings well ahead and reconfirm the day before. WhatsApp follow-ups after a meeting are increasingly normal.
- Avoid deadlines and launches in January-February (summer holidays), the July winter school break and the week of 18 September.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with a reason and an alternative: "Por ahora no nos es posible avanzar con esta opción, pero podríamos..." rather than "No podemos".
- Decode soft answers: "lo vamos a ver", "ya veremos", "te confirmo" and "te llamo" are not commitments. Reply with a concrete question ("¿Le acomoda el jueves 22 a las 10:00?").
- Criticism stays private: no corrections in group threads, no senior people added to the CC line of a complaint. Disagreement with a senior is put tentatively, as a question or an option.
- Many employees say they get too little feedback; kind but explicit feedback with a clear next step is welcomed.
- When adapting Chilean text for direct cultures, turn the hedges into explicit yes, no and dates.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with references, known local clients, after-sales support and introductions through a mutual contact ("pituto"). Never suggest a connection can get around rules or officials.
- Proposals: clear, concise, built around concrete short- and medium-term results, with standards and certifications that reduce perceived risk.
- Advertising self-regulation requires proof for objective claims; measurable superiority claims ("el más rápido", "N.º 1") need verification. Comparative ads may name rivals if fair, verifiable and not denigrating. No false exclusivity, no guaranteed health or luck results, no fear tactics to sell. Paid influencer content must be identifiable as advertising.
- Prices in ads are shown in Chilean pesos; dollars or UF (an inflation-indexed unit used in property and contracts) only alongside. Retail leans on seasonal sales and CyberDay events.
- Consumers may refuse promotional contact: every promotional email needs a working unsubscribe, contact must stop after opt-out, and there is a national do-not-disturb register. Unsolicited mass calls use the 809 prefix. Junk-food ("alto en") advertising to children under 14 is restricted.
- CV: one page for juniors, up to two for seniors; a 2-5 line profile; reverse-chronological experience with month and year; achievements with action verbs and numbers ("Reduje los costos en un 12%"); PDF in a plain layout. No photo, birth date or marital status unless asked; the RUT (national ID number) is optional; give comuna and city, not the full address. "Referencias disponibles a solicitud" is the usual line.
- Job ads: the Labour Code bars requirements based on race, sex, age, marital status, union membership, religion, political opinion, nationality, socioeconomic status, language, sexual orientation, gender identity, appearance, illness or disability. The labour regulator treats "buena presencia", a photo, age ranges, criminal or credit-record certificates and "own car" as discriminatory unless essential to the job. Say whether pay is "bruto" or "líquido" (net).

## Humor and emphasis

- Light humour helps once there is rapport, but business itself is treated seriously. Chilean teasing does not transfer to writing from an outsider.
- Class labels ("cuico" posh, "flaite" lowlife) and jokes about someone's background or neighbourhood are risky in any text.
- Emphasise with bold, not capitals. Opening "¿" and "¡" and accents are mandatory.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Dictatorship (1973-1990):** the coup of 11 September 1973, Pinochet, Allende and human-rights crimes still divide families and politics. No evaluative references, nostalgia or jokes in business or marketing text.
- **The 2019 unrest and the constitution:** mass protests from 18 October 2019 over inequality led to two constitutional drafts, rejected by voters in 2022 and 2023; the amended 1980 constitution remains. Whether October 2019 was a "social outburst" or mainly violence is itself disputed; take no side and avoid the imagery.
- **Mapuche:** the largest Indigenous people, with land claims and periods of violence and states of emergency in Araucanía and Biobío. Words like "terrorismo", "conflicto" or "Wallmapu" carry political weight. Show Indigenous people as people, not costume, and say "pueblo mapuche".
- **Class and inequality:** surnames, schools and neighbourhoods signal class; do not use them to characterise readers. Avoid comments on wealth or income.
- Relations with Peru and Bolivia, abortion, divorce and church influence are contested; leave them out. Any hint of bribery is a serious red line.
- Ads must not offend national symbols or institutions, discriminate or use degrading gender stereotypes. The new data protection law (Ley 21.719) is due on 1 December 2026, with a bill to postpone it a year pending; check its status. Legal items are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The flag shows the blue square with the star at the top left; careless or inverted flags are noticed. Display is mandatory on 18-19 September and 21 May.
- September ("el Dieciocho": empanadas, cueca, kites, fondas) is the main patriotic season; summer campaigns run December-February. Seasons are reversed; Christmas is in summer.
- Yellow roses and knives or scissors carry negative meanings as gifts.
- Show Chile beyond Santiago: the mining north, wine valleys, the south, Patagonia, Rapa Nui. Wine, food, football and landscapes are safe themes.

|---|---|
| Date | CLDR: 17-10-2026, short 17-10-26 (hyphens); 17/10/2026 also common. Long: 17 de octubre de 2026 (lowercase month). Never month-first. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Santiago:** corporate districts (Las Condes, Providencia, Vitacura) are the most formal and the most English-friendly; when unsure, write more formally.
- **Regions:** fewer English speakers, Spanish-only materials matter more; shops may close for a midday break. Sector context differs: mining in Antofagasta and the north, ports in Valparaíso, wine and agriculture in the Central Valley, forestry and universities in Concepción, salmon and logistics in the south.
- **Generations:** younger readers use "tú" quickly and expect lighter copy; many older readers expect "usted" and need extra care on the dictatorship, divorce and religion.
- **Sectors:** mining, tech, startups and multinationals are flatter and may hire in English; banking, law, government and family firms keep titles and "usted". Consumer apps lean to "tú", enterprise software to "usted".
- **Class and accent:** vocabulary and accent signal social class more than region, which is why slang choices in copy are noticed.
- **Indigenous peoples:** Mapuche communities in the south and in Santiago, Aymara in the far north, Rapa Nui on Easter Island; national campaigns should not treat any of them as folklore.
- **Calendar:** summer holidays January-February, winter break in July, Fiestas Patrias around 18 September; the legal working week is falling step by step to 40 hours by 2028.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (upbeat US sales style, neutral Spanish, tú):

> ¡Hola Rodrigo! Somos la plataforma de logística número 1 de la región. Agenda una demo esta semana y obtén 30% de descuento. ¡No te lo pierdas!

**After** (first contact with a Chilean operations manager, in Spanish with gloss):

> Estimado Sr. Morales:
>
> Junto con saludar, le escribo de parte de Paula Rivas, de Agrícola Los Andes, quien me sugirió contactarlo. Trabajamos con empresas exportadoras de la zona central para reducir los tiempos de despacho; en Los Andes bajamos el plazo promedio de 4 a 3 días. Si le parece útil, me gustaría presentarle el caso en una reunión de 30 minutos. ¿Le acomodaría el martes 3 o el jueves 5 de noviembre, a las 10:00?
>
> Saludos cordiales,

*(Gloss: "Dear Mr Morales, along with greeting you, I am writing on behalf of Paula Rivas of Agrícola Los Andes, who suggested I contact you. We work with exporters in the central zone to cut dispatch times; at Los Andes we brought the average down from 4 to 3 days. If it seems useful, I would like to present the case in a 30-minute meeting. Would Tuesday 3 or Thursday 5 November at 10:00 suit you? Kind regards,")*

**Why:** The unprovable "número 1" and the countdown pressure go, replaced by a referral, one verifiable result and two concrete meeting options. "Usted", title, surname and a colon fit first contact; the exclamation marks disappear. A Chilean reader can say yes to a date without being pushed.
