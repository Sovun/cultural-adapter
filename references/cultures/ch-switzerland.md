# Switzerland

## In brief

Swiss professional readers expect accurate, complete and calm text, and they read commitments literally: a date or a "yes" is a promise. The content can be clear, but the form is softer than in Germany. Requests go in the conditional, "Sie müssen" sounds harsh, and German-German bluntness can come across as arrogant. Switzerland has four national languages, so copy for French- or Italian-speaking readers needs its own adapted version, not a translation of German or a France or Italy text. Swiss Standard German has visible markers: no "ß", Swiss vocabulary, «guillemets» and franc amounts like CHF 1'250.00.

## Voice and tone

- Factual, precise and polite. Small errors in names, figures or spelling are read as carelessness, so proofread.
- Courtesy shows in the conditional and in "bitte", not in adjectives: "Dürfte ich Sie bitten, ...?", "Wäre es möglich, ...?", "Würden Sie ...?". These sound old-fashioned in Germany but are current in Switzerland.
- Write Swiss Standard German for Swiss readers. Common swaps: Ferien (Urlaub), Spital (Krankenhaus), Velo (Fahrrad), parkieren (parken), Traktandenliste (Tagesordnung), Pendenzen (offene Punkte), innert (innerhalb), allfällig (eventuell), "das E-Mail". Even federal texts use these words.
- Business writing is in Standard German. Swiss German dialect appears in chat, social media and some local ads; a foreign writer imitating it looks forced.
- English is common in Zurich, Zug, Basel and Geneva multinationals, but writing in the reader's language signals respect.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, surname. Use Herr/Frau + surname; "Frau" for every adult woman, never "Fräulein".
- German openers: "Sehr geehrte Frau Keller," (safe, required for authorities and applications), "Guten Tag Herr Brunner," (modern neutral, keep it consistent), "Liebe Frau Keller," (warm but still formal, for regular contacts). Unknown reader: "Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren,".
- "Grüezi Frau Keller" works only inside German-speaking Switzerland and never with a first name. "Hallo" is risky in business; "Hoi" and "Ciao" are for close contacts. An email with no salutation reads as cold.
- Titles: "Dr." and "Prof." are abbreviated, never stacked; role matters more than degree, and many people do not use their title in daily mail. Job titles take the person's gender ("Frau Direktorin").
- Sign-offs: "Freundliche Grüsse" (versatile default), "Mit freundlichen Grüssen" (formal letters; some younger readers find it dated), "Herzliche Grüsse" (known contacts), "Liebe Grüsse" only with first names. Never "MfG" or "LG" to clients.
- Capitalise Sie, Ihr, Ihnen. Do not start "du" yourself; a first-name signature from a senior Swiss contact can be the offer.

## Structuring a message

1. A specific subject line naming the matter ("Offerte 2026-31: Liefertermin Position 4").
2. Salutation and one courteous line, then the request in the first few lines.
3. Facts, figures, conditions and attachments in short, ordered paragraphs.
4. A dated next step with an owner; a closing line such as "Für Fragen stehe ich Ihnen gerne zur Verfügung."
5. Sign-off and a full signature: name, function, full registered company name, address, direct phone.

- After meetings, send minutes with decisions, owners and deadlines; they become the record. Ask for meetings well ahead, reply within a working day or two, and do not demand action in evenings or at weekends.
- A short personal wish at the end ("Schöne Ferien!", "Einen guten Start in die Woche") is welcome once there is contact.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Base disagreement on facts and consequences. "Wir sehen das etwas anders, weil..." is fine; sweeping or personal wording is not.
- A refusal is clear but cushioned: "Das ist uns leider nicht möglich, weil..." plus an alternative where you have one.
- A Swiss "ja" is checked before it is given, so do not push for a quick answer before the reader has consulted colleagues. Pressure and inflated opening offers damage trust.
- Detailed critical questions signal interest, not hostility. Work references (Arbeitszeugnisse) must by law be truthful and benevolent, so readers decode careful positive wording and notice what is missing. Treat faint praise in Swiss evaluations as a signal.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with quality, durability, precision, data privacy and sustainability, backed by certifications, warranties, test results and real reviews. Show the full price including fees. Luxury display and status bragging put readers off.
- Fair-trading law and the advertising self-regulator require true and clear claims: no advertising of features that are standard or legally required, testimonials only from real people with consent, tests only if neutral, special offers available for a reasonable minimum period, paid content labelled ("Anzeige", "Publireportage"). Sexist imagery, needless disparagement of competitors and vague green claims ("klimaneutral") without stated scope and proof are unfair.
- "Swiss made", "Schweizer Qualität" and the Swiss cross are regulated (Swissness rules): products must meet origin thresholds, and the cross in a shield is reserved for the state. Partial claims like "Designed in Switzerland" must not carry a prominent cross.
- Marketing email and SMS need prior consent (existing customers may get similar offers until they object) and a free opt-out in every message. Data protection law requires a clear privacy notice.
- CV: tabular, reverse chronological, two pages at most, with a professional photo (expected in German-speaking areas), date of birth, nationality and permit type, and CEFR language levels. Attach work references and diplomas; referees on request. Cover letter: one page, tailored, ending with a request for an interview.
- Job ads: state the workload as a percentage ("Projektleiterin/Projektleiter 80-100 %"), use pair or neutral forms, and do not discriminate by sex, including via family status (Gender Equality Act). Age limits are not banned outright but employers' bodies discourage them; leave them out. For occupations with high unemployment, vacancies must first be reported to the public employment service, which delays public ads by five working days.
- Gender-inclusive spelling: federal texts reject the star and colon and use pair forms ("Kundinnen und Kunden"), neutral nouns or a slash in short text. Some cantons and firms use the colon. Pair and neutral forms are the low-risk choice.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour stays out of first contact. Later it is dry and understated: irony and self-mockery, never aimed at the reader. A reader unsure whether you are joking will ask.
- Cantons and language regions tease each other; outsiders should not join in. Emphasise with data and bold, not capitals, "!!" or intensifiers.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Salary, wealth, age and family are private; do not raise them in networking or sales text.
- Do not present Switzerland as part of Germany or France, use German or EU symbols for it, or compare it unfavourably with neighbours. Do not assume EU law applies.
- Neutrality, the wartime past and the army are poor material for casual or marketing copy.
- The four language communities are treated as equals; a national campaign in German only excludes about a third of readers.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Red and white with the Swiss cross mean Swiss origin to buyers; use them only for products that qualify.
- Nature, mountains, precision, reliability and sustainability are positive themes.
- People shown in ads should have a natural link to the product; use children with restraint.

| Time | 24-hour: 14:30 (CLDR) or the distinctly Swiss 14.30 Uhr. |

## Variation within the culture

- **French-speaking Switzerland (fr-CH, about a quarter of the population):** "vous" and "Madame/Monsieur" by default; "Madame, Monsieur," for unknown readers; closing "Meilleures salutations" or "Avec mes meilleures salutations", not the long France-French "sentiments distingués" formulas. Tone is warmer and more relationship-oriented than in German-speaking areas, with more small talk and more respect for rank; disagreement is more indirect than in de-CH but franker than in France. Swiss usage: "septante", "nonante" (and "huitante" in Vaud, Valais and Fribourg), "postuler" and "postulation" for job applications, hyphenated inclusive forms (collaborateur-trice-s) in public texts. CLDR fr-CH uses a decimal comma for general numbers but "1'234.50 CHF" for money. CV photos are optional. Write or adapt for Romandie directly; a France-French text or a translated German one is noticed.
- **Italian-speaking Switzerland (it-CH, Ticino and southern Grisons, about 8 %):** "Lei", "Gentile Signora Rossi", "Cordiali saluti". Warmer and livelier than de-CH, still more reserved than Italy. Swiss Italian words are normal even in formal text ("natel" for mobile phone, "classatore" for binder, "a dipendenza di" for depending on); copy for both Ticino and Italy needs neutral Italian. Swiss formats (CHF 1'234.50, apostrophe grouping) apply, not Italian ones. Letters often put place and date top right. Romansh (about 0.5 %, Grisons) is also a national language; a Romansh line in regional copy is a courtesy.
- **Sector and city:** banking, insurance, public administration and traditional SMEs keep "Sie" and full formulas; startups, tech and English-speaking multinationals use first names. International Geneva works largely in English and often prefers CVs without photos.
- **Generation:** younger readers move to first names and lighter closings faster, but often keep formal salutations until they have met you.
- **Germany vs Switzerland:** same written standard, different spelling, vocabulary, formats and politeness. Swiss words like "Traktanden" or "Pendenzen" are unknown in Germany, so mixed DACH texts need neutral choices. Public holidays differ by canton.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No "ß" anywhere; "ss" throughout (Grüsse, Strasse).
- [ ] Swiss vocabulary where it exists (Ferien, Spital, Velo, parkieren); no Germany-only words in consumer copy.
- [ ] Amounts read CHF 1'234.50 or Fr. 20.-, never 1.234,50 € style; dates 17.10.2026; quotes «...».
- [ ] Requests use "bitte" and the conditional or a question; no "Sie müssen" or bare imperatives toward the reader.
- [ ] "Sie" (capitalised) unless the reader has offered "du"; salutation fits the relationship; no "Hallo" or "Grüezi" with a surname in a first email.
- [ ] Every request states what, who and by which date; vague timing replaced by dates.
- [ ] No promise or offer the writer does not intend to keep.
- [ ] Refusals and criticism give a reason and stay factual and impersonal.
- [ ] No hype, unproven superlatives, claims about standard features, or vague green claims.
- [ ] "Swiss made", "Swiss quality" and the Swiss cross appear only if Swissness criteria are met; no cross in a shield.
- [ ] Prices show the full amount including fees; paid content is labelled.
- [ ] Marketing email has prior consent or an existing-customer basis, plus an opt-out.
- [ ] Signature and footer carry the full registered company name.
- [ ] Job ads give the workload percentage, use pair or neutral forms and state no sex, family or age criteria.
- [ ] French and Italian versions are adapted for Swiss readers, with Swiss formats, not copied from France or Italy.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (German-German default):

> Sehr geehrter Herr Huber,
>
> Sie müssen uns die fehlenden Pläne bis Freitag schicken, sonst können wir den Liefertermin nicht halten. Die Kosten der Verzögerung tragen Sie.
>
> Mit freundlichen Grüßen

**After** (for a Swiss client in Bern):

> Guten Tag Herr Huber,
>
> vielen Dank für Ihre Bestellung vom 6. Oktober. Damit wir den vereinbarten Liefertermin vom 14. November einhalten können, fehlen uns noch die Konstruktionspläne zu den Positionen 2 und 5. Dürften wir Sie bitten, uns diese bis Freitag, 24. Oktober, zuzustellen? Falls das nicht möglich ist, rufe ich Sie gerne an, damit wir gemeinsam einen neuen Termin festlegen. Allfällige Mehrkosten würden wir vorgängig mit Ihnen besprechen.
>
> Freundliche Grüsse

*(Gloss: "Good day Mr Huber, thank you for your order of 6 October. To meet the agreed delivery date of 14 November, we still need the design drawings for items 2 and 5. Might we ask you to send them by Friday 24 October? If that is not possible, I will gladly call you so we can set a new date together. We would discuss any extra costs with you in advance. Kind regards,")*

**Why:** The deadline and the consequence stay explicit, with exact dates, as Swiss readers expect. The command and the threat about costs become a polite question, a shared fallback and an advance promise to talk. "ß" is gone, and "zustellen", "allfällig" and "vorgängig" mark the writer as someone who knows Swiss business German.
