# Netherlands

## In brief

Dutch readers are among the most direct in the world and among the most egalitarian. They want honest, short, factual text and are allergic to both hype and excessive politeness, which they read as insincere. Status claims and boasting backfire; arguments built on evidence and fairness work. Most professionals read English very well, but this does not mean English-only copy is right for consumers.

## Voice and tone

- Short sentences, plain words, friendly but no-nonsense.
- Excessive politeness, flattery and formulaic courtesy feel distrustful.
- Honest acknowledgement of limitations increases trust ("Our product doesn't do X yet").
- Humor tends to be cheeky; irony is less reliable than in the UK because statements may be taken literally.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, then surname. Surname particles (van, de, van der, 't) are lowercase when the given name precedes them ("Jan van Dijk") but capitalized when the surname stands alone ("Mr Van Dijk", "meneer Van Dijk").
- Many people use a calling name (roepnaam) that differs from their official first name. Use the name they sign with.
- Business email salutation: "Beste Sanne," (neutral, very common) or "Hoi Sanne," (informal). Formal: "Geachte heer De Vries," / "Geachte mevrouw Bakker,". "Geachte" is now mostly used in legal, governmental and very formal letters.
- Titles (Dr., Prof., mr. for lawyers, ir. for engineers) appear in formal correspondence but are rarely used in conversation.
- Sign-offs: "Met vriendelijke groet," (standard), "Groet," (short), "Hartelijke groet," (warm).

## Structuring a message

- Purpose in the first line. Short paragraphs. Bullet points welcome.
- Include the reasoning and facts that support any proposal; expect questions.
- Make clear what you expect from the reader and when.
- Avoid long warm-up paragraphs and repeated thanks.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Feedback is direct, specific and expected in both directions, including from junior to senior.
- "Nee" is a complete answer. Refusals do not need elaborate cushioning.
- Disagreement during debate is not personal and does not harm the relationship.
- When adapting Dutch source text for indirect cultures (UK, Japan, Indonesia, Gulf states), considerable softening is needed. Dutch-written English can sound harsh to British or American readers.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Value for money is a powerful argument: the Dutch are cost-conscious and skeptical of premium claims without substance.
- Show facts, comparisons and fair pricing. Hidden costs or fine print damage trust badly.
- Avoid superlatives and "too good to be true" promises; these trigger suspicion.
- The Dutch Advertising Code (Reclamecode) applies; misleading or unsubstantiated claims can be challenged.
- CVs: concise (one to two pages), factual, often with a short profile. Photos are fairly common but optional. Modest wording with concrete results works best.
- Consumer marketing in Dutch performs better than English, even though English is widely understood. B2B and tech can use English.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor is welcome in many contexts, including business, but jokes based on superiority or mocking others fall flat.
- Exclamation marks: rare in business email.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- "Holland" is only two provinces. Use "the Netherlands" in professional text; people from other provinces may object.
- Politically divisive: immigration and asylum, the Freedom Party (PVV), Zwarte Piet, farmers and the nitrogen/manure crisis, housing shortage.
- Intolerance of minorities or LGBT people is strongly disapproved of. Same-sex marriage has been legal since 2001.
- Do not ask about income or wealth.
- Religion plays a modest role for most people, but the "Bible Belt" (from Zeeland to Gelderland) is strongly Reformed Christian and conservative.
- Criticism of the monarchy may offend some older people; younger people tend to be indifferent.
- Do not overplay cannabis and red-light clichés in content about the Netherlands; many Dutch find this reductive.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Orange is the national color (House of Orange-Nassau), used on King's Day (27 April) and at football events.
- Tapping a finger on the forehead signals "crazy" and is an insult.
- Cycling, water and design are positive, authentic imagery; windmills, clogs and tulips read as tourist clichés.

## Variation within the culture

- The Randstad (Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht) is more international, liberal and English-speaking than the rest of the country.
- The south (Brabant, Limburg) is historically Catholic, warmer and somewhat more relationship-oriented ("Burgundian" lifestyle).
- Flanders (Belgium) speaks Dutch but is more formal and indirect; do not treat Flemish and Dutch audiences as identical.
- Large communities of Turkish, Moroccan, Surinamese and Indonesian heritage.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The main point appears in the first sentence or two.
- [ ] Hype, superlatives and empty compliments are removed.
- [ ] Claims are backed by facts, numbers or comparisons; costs are transparent.
- [ ] No appeals to status, prestige or hierarchy ("as a senior partner I...").
- [ ] The country is called "the Netherlands", not "Holland", in formal text.
- [ ] Dutch-language consumer text uses "je" or "u" consistently and appropriately for the audience.
- [ ] Surname particles follow Dutch capitalization rules.
- [ ] Dates, numbers and currency use Dutch formats.
- [ ] If Dutch source text is adapted for indirect cultures, criticism and refusals are softened.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (British-style indirect):

> I was just wondering whether it might perhaps be possible to have a quick look at the figures at some point, if it's not too much trouble?

**After** (tailored for Dutch readers):

> Could you check the Q3 figures by Thursday? The total for region North looks about 8% too high.

**Why:** Dutch readers value clarity and efficiency. Layers of hedging hide the actual request and can seem insincere.
