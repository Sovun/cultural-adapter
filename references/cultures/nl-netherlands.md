# Netherlands

## In brief

Dutch readers are among the most direct in the world and among the most egalitarian. They want honest, short, factual text and are allergic to both hype and excessive politeness, which they read as insincere. Status claims and boasting backfire; arguments built on evidence and fairness work. Consumer law is strict on email consent, honest pricing and ad disclosure, and job ads are checked closely for age and gender wording. Most professionals read English very well, but this does not mean English-only copy is right for consumers.

## Voice and tone

- Short sentences, plain words, friendly but no-nonsense.
- Excessive politeness, flattery and formulaic courtesy feel distrustful.
- In Dutch copy, cut officialese: "ook" not "tevens", "over" not "betreffende" or "met betrekking tot", no "bovengenoemde".
- Honest acknowledgement of limitations increases trust ("Our product doesn't do X yet").
- Humor tends to be cheeky and self-deprecating. Dry irony exists, but in writing it may be read literally, so keep it light.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, then surname. Surname particles (van, de, van der, 't) are lowercase after a given name or initial ("Jan van Dijk") and capitalized when the surname stands alone ("meneer Van Dijk"). Flemish rules differ.
- Many people use a calling name (roepnaam) that differs from their official first name. Use the name they sign with.
- First contact with a named reader: "Beste Sanne de Vries," (the usual opener, also gender-neutral). Once you know each other: "Beste Sanne," or informal "Hoi Sanne,".
- Formal and distant: "Geachte heer De Vries," / "Geachte mevrouw Bakker,". Stiff in email but still standard for official, legal and some institutional first contact. Never "Geachte meneer". Unknown name: "Geachte klant," or "Beste lezer,".
- After the salutation comma, the body starts with a capital letter.
- Titles are written lowercase (dr., prof., drs., mr. for lawyers, ir. for engineers) and appear in formal correspondence, rarely in conversation.
- je/u: choose one form and keep it throughout; "u" and "uw" are lowercase, and the verb keeps its -t ("vindt u", but "vind je"). Part of the older public finds a brand's "je" too familiar.
- Sign-offs: "Met vriendelijke groet," (standard), "Vriendelijke groet," (shorter), "Hartelijke groet," (warm). "Hoogachtend" only after "Geachte"; "Groetjes" only for close contacts.

## Structuring a message

- Purpose in the subject line and first sentence. Short paragraphs. Bullet points welcome. Dutch writing guides advise against opening a letter with "Ik".
- Include the reasoning and facts that support any proposal; expect questions.
- Close by restating what you expect from the reader and by when ("Graag ontvang ik uw reactie voor vrijdag").
- Avoid long warm-up paragraphs and repeated thanks.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Feedback is direct, specific and expected in both directions, including from junior to senior.
- A plain "nee" with a short reason is clearer than a soft refusal, which may be read as a yes.
- Criticism targets the work, not the person. Disagreement during debate does not harm the relationship.
- Foreign writers should aim for clear and neutral, not imitated bluntness: harshness and personal remarks are rejected here too.
- When adapting Dutch source text for indirect cultures (UK, Flanders, Japan, Indonesia, Gulf states), considerable softening is needed. Dutch-written English can sound harsh to British or American readers.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Value for money is a powerful argument: the Dutch are cost-conscious and skeptical of premium claims without substance.
- Show facts, fair comparisons and transparent pricing; offer iDEAL in web shops. Hidden costs or fine print damage trust badly.
- Avoid superlatives and "too good to be true" promises; these trigger suspicion.
- Consumer marketing in Dutch performs better than English, even though English is widely understood. B2B and tech can use English.
- CVs: one to two A4 pages, reverse chronological, a short profile, concrete results instead of adjectives. Leave out nationality, marital status, religion, health and BSN. Photo and date of birth are contested and optional; leaving them out is safe. Add a one-page motivation letter written for the specific vacancy.
- Job ads: add "(m/v/x)" and use gender-neutral job nouns. Ask for a job-related Dutch level ("goede beheersing van de Nederlandse taal"), not "native" or flawless Dutch. Minimum experience is fine.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor is welcome in many contexts, including business. Teasing is a common Dutch register but can read as unkind to outsiders; ads must not be needlessly offensive.
- Exclamation marks: rare in business email. Specific facts carry emphasis.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Advertising:** the Dutch Advertising Code (Nederlandse Reclame Code, complaints to the Reclame Code Commissie) puts the burden of proof on the advertiser. Comparisons must be like with like, testimonials genuine, and ads recognisable as ads. Sector codes cover alcohol, sustainability claims, food, children and gambling.
- **Influencers:** the social media code requires any paid or gifted relationship to be disclosed in the post itself ("#ad", "#reclame", "Betaald partnerschap met..."), not buried among hashtags. Check current version.
- **Prices and pressure (ACM):** show the total price with all compulsory costs up front. A struck-through "was" price must be the lowest price of the previous 30 days. Countdown timers must be real; permanent "sale" claims and dark patterns are misleading.
- **Email and phone marketing:** prior opt-in for email, SMS and WhatsApp (Telecommunicatiewet art. 11.7, enforced by ACM); no pre-ticked boxes. Existing customers may get offers for similar products if they can opt out. Every message names the sender and has a free opt-out. Telemarketing to consumers needs prior consent since 1 July 2021, with exceptions for (recent) customers. Check current status.
- **Job ads:** the College voor de Rechten van de Mens has repeatedly found age wording discriminatory: no age ranges, "jong", "starter", "student", "pas afgestudeerd" or "junior" as a candidate label, and no maximum years of experience. Gendered words ("enthousiaste jongen", "secretaresse") and nationality requirements also fail. Describing the company as a "jong team" is accepted.
- "Holland" is only two provinces. Use "the Netherlands" in professional text; people from other provinces may object.
- Politically divisive: immigration and asylum, the populist right (PVV), farmers and the nitrogen crisis, the housing shortage.
- **Zwarte Piet:** the blackface helper in the Sinterklaas tradition is contested. Most municipalities and national broadcasts now use soot-smudged "roetveegpieten", while some places keep black make-up and protests continue. Never use blackface imagery and do not take sides in marketing; check current context before any Sinterklaas content.
- Intolerance of minorities or LGBT people is strongly disapproved of. Same-sex marriage has been legal since 2001.
- Do not ask about income or wealth. Religion is kept out of the workplace and plays a modest role for most people, but the "Bible Belt" (from Zeeland to Gelderland) is strongly Reformed Christian and conservative.
- Criticism of the monarchy may offend some older people; younger people tend to be indifferent.
- Do not overplay cannabis and red-light clichés in content about the Netherlands; many Dutch find this reductive.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Orange is the national color (House of Orange-Nassau), used on King's Day (27 April, or 26 April when the 27th is a Sunday) and at football events.
- Tapping a finger on the forehead signals "crazy" and is an insult.
- White lilies and chrysanthemums suggest mourning; business gifts are modest.
- Cycling, water and design are positive, authentic imagery; windmills, clogs and tulips read as tourist clichés.

## Variation within the culture

- The Randstad (Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht) is more international, liberal and English-speaking than the rest of the country; many international firms work in English.
- The south (Brabant, Limburg) is historically Catholic, warmer and somewhat more relationship-oriented ("Burgundian" lifestyle).
- Sector and age shape the je/u choice: tech, start-ups and youth brands use "je"; banks, insurers, law and government lean towards "u".
- Consensus can make decisions slower than outsiders expect, but once made they are rarely reopened.
- Flanders (Belgium) speaks Dutch but is more formal and indirect, with more "u"; do not treat Flemish and Dutch audiences as identical.
- Large communities of Turkish, Moroccan, Surinamese and Indonesian heritage.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The main point appears in the first sentence or two, and the ask with a deadline is restated at the end.
- [ ] Hype, superlatives and empty compliments are removed; claims can be substantiated.
- [ ] Prices show the full cost; discounts refer to the real 30-day price; no fake timers or urgency.
- [ ] Marketing email, SMS or calls go only to people who opted in, with sender name and opt-out.
- [ ] Paid or gifted posts are clearly labelled as advertising.
- [ ] Job ads have no age or gendered wording, use "(m/v/x)" and a job-related Dutch level.
- [ ] No appeals to status, prestige or hierarchy ("as a senior partner I...").
- [ ] The country is called "the Netherlands", not "Holland", in formal text.
- [ ] Dutch text uses "je" or "u" consistently, lowercase "u", correct verb endings, plain words instead of officialese.
- [ ] Salutation and sign-off match ("Beste" + full name for first contact; "Hoogachtend" only after "Geachte").
- [ ] Surname particles and titles follow Dutch capitalization rules.
- [ ] Dates, numbers and currency use Dutch formats.
- [ ] If Dutch source text is adapted for indirect cultures, criticism and refusals are softened.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (British-style indirect):

> I was just wondering whether it might perhaps be possible to have a quick look at the figures at some point, if it's not too much trouble?

**After** (tailored for Dutch readers):

> Could you check the Q3 figures by Thursday? The total for region North looks about 8% too high.

**Why:** Dutch readers value clarity and efficiency. Layers of hedging hide the actual request and can seem insincere.
