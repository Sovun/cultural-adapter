# Austria

## In brief

Austrian professional readers expect accurate, complete and sober text, delivered with more ceremony than in Germany. Academic and professional titles matter: getting "Mag.", "Dr." or "DI" wrong, or leaving them out, is the most visible mistake a foreign writer makes. "Sie" is the default until the Austrian side offers "du". Clear statements are fine, but a short courteous lead-in and a willingness to compromise land better than German-style bluntness. Austria is not Germany: German-German vocabulary ("Januar", "lecker", "Tüte"), the euro sign after the amount and German symbols mark a text as foreign.

## Voice and tone

- Factual, polite and complete. Courtesy shows through correct forms of address, a lead-in sentence and a thank-you line, not through enthusiastic adjectives.
- Write Austrian Standard German (de-AT) for Austrian readers. Common swaps: Jänner (Januar), Paradeiser (Tomaten), Erdäpfel (Kartoffeln), Marillen (Aprikosen), Topfen (Quark), Schlagobers (Sahne), Sackerl (Tüte), Sessel (Stuhl). Food and retail copy is where readers notice most.
- Austrian forms also show up in office language: "das E-Mail" is often neuter, and formal requests often use "ersuchen" ("Wir ersuchen um Rückmeldung").
- Hype, pressure and promises that sound too good read as insincere. So does a jovial, chummy tone with a stranger.
- Machine-translated or German-German text is understood but feels imported; consumer copy deserves its own de-AT version.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, surname. Use Herr/Frau + highest title + surname: "Sehr geehrter Herr Dr. Bauer," / "Sehr geehrte Frau Mag. Huber,". Use only the highest title, abbreviated.
- Degree placement is official: Mag., Dr. and DI (Dipl.-Ing.) go before the name; BA, BSc, MA, MSc, MBA and PhD go after it ("Anna Berger, MSc"). Post-nominal degrees are usually left out of the salutation.
- Public sector, schools and universities often use feminine forms ("Mag.a", "Dr.in") and job or honorary titles ("Frau Direktorin", "Herr Hofrat"). Copy exactly what the person uses in their own signature.
- Many private firms, startups and tech teams drop titles in daily mail. Still use them in first contact unless the recipient signs without one.
- Unknown recipient: "Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren," remains safe; a team address ("Sehr geehrtes Service-Team,") also works. "Guten Tag Frau Huber," is a modern, slightly less distant opener.
- "Hallo" goes only with a first name. Never "Fräulein". With several recipients, avoid masculine-only forms like "Liebe Kollegen" if women are included.
- Traditional letters may end the salutation with "!" ("Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren!"). It is correct in Austria; a comma is the safer default for foreign writers.
- "Du" is offered by the older or more senior person. Signing a first email with just a first name can read as offering it. "Grüß Gott" (spoken standard) and "Servus" (people you know) do not belong in a first email.
- Sign-offs: "Mit freundlichen Grüßen" (formal), "Freundliche Grüße" / "Beste Grüße" (neutral), "Liebe Grüße" (established contacts, common in Austria), "Hochachtungsvoll" (only for authorities or formal complaints). "LG" is too casual for clients.

## Structuring a message

1. Specific subject line naming the topic in keywords.
2. Salutation with the correct title, then one lead-in line that connects to earlier contact or states the occasion.
3. Purpose and reasons in short, complete paragraphs; figures, conditions and attachments clearly listed.
4. A dated next step and, where fitting, a thank-you line ("Vielen Dank für Ihre Unterstützung.").
5. Sign-off and a full signature block: name, title, position, company, address, phone.

- After meetings, send a written summary of decisions and next steps. Request meetings weeks ahead. Replies slow in July and August, around Christmas and in the week before Easter.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement can be stated plainly and is not taken personally, but frame it as a path to a solution both sides accept. Pressing for an answer or insisting on being right backfires.
- Even a cancellation or refusal usually opens with a polite line about the cooperation so far before the decision.
- German-German phrasing that reads as efficient in Hamburg can read as brusque in Vienna; add the lead-in and a reason.
- Do not criticise third parties in writing. Praise is sparing and specific. Decoding: spoken Austrian irony (Schmäh) is often deadpan; a remark that sounds odd may be a joke, not a complaint.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with track record, founding year, certifications, references and a reliable long-term offer. Urgency tactics and hard selling put readers off. A referral from a mutual contact opens doors.
- Consumer advertising often favours understatement, wit and lifestyle imagery over punchy specification slogans.
- Electronic advertising (email, SMS, messenger) needs prior consent for consumers and businesses alike; the existing-customer exception is narrow and senders must respect the national opt-out list. Commercial websites need an imprint, and so do frequent newsletters. Sexist ads draw public stop calls from the self-regulatory advertising council.
- Job ads must be gender-neutral and must not discriminate by ethnic origin, religion or belief, age or sexual orientation. "(m/w/d)" is widely used. Every ad must state the minimum gross pay (collective-agreement rate or negotiation basis) and any readiness to pay more: "Für diese Position gilt ein Bruttomonatsgehalt ab € 3.200 mit Bereitschaft zur Überzahlung." EU pay-transparency rules are still being transposed; check current status.
- CV (Lebenslauf): tabular, reverse chronological, at most two pages, CEFR language levels, achievements with figures. A photo is common and often expected in sales or hospitality, but legally optional. Date of birth is often included. Add certificates and work references (Arbeitszeugnisse). Include your title, and address the contact by the title used in the ad.
- Cover letter: tailored, concrete, no stock opening like "Hiermit bewerbe ich mich", closing with a request for an interview; a scanned signature is appreciated.
- Gender-inclusive spelling is contested: Lower Austria's administration bans the star, colon and internal capital I, while others use them. Pair forms ("Kundinnen und Kunden") and neutral nouns are the least contested.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour is dry, ironic and often dark, strongest in Vienna, but rarely belongs in business text. A misplaced joke reads as unprofessional.
- Dialect words feel warm in local consumer copy and wrong in formal correspondence. Emphasise with bold or layout, not capitals or "!!".

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Nazi era:** glorifying or trivialising Nazi crimes is a criminal offence, and Nazi symbols and those of several banned groups are prohibited, with fines raised in 2023. Austria's own role in the period is still debated; keep WWII references, comparisons and jokes out.
- **Germany:** do not treat Austria as part of Germany, use German flags or Bavarian clichés for it, or compare it unfavourably with Germany.
- Migration is divisive; religion and politics stay out of openers. Personal finances and private life are off-limits.
- Permanent neutrality is a point of pride for many; avoid framing Austria as part of a military bloc.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Mountains, skiing, hiking, lakes, music, coffeehouses, wine and regional food are positive themes. Chrysanthemums are funeral flowers; gifts are modest.
- Twenty-three Austrian food terms (Marillen, Paradeiser, Karfiol and others) have equal legal standing with their German equivalents in EU law, so use them on Austrian packaging and menus.

## Variation within the culture

- **Sector:** public administration, schools, universities, law, healthcare and engineering are the most title-conscious. Tech, startups, creative agencies and many multinationals use "du" internally and drop titles.
- **Vienna vs regions:** Vienna is more international and English-friendly, with the strongest Schmäh. Each of the nine federal states has a strong identity; Vorarlberg's dialect is closer to Swiss German.
- **Tourism:** Alpine hotels and hospitality brands often address guests with "du"; this does not carry over to B2B.
- **Generation:** younger readers relax "Sie/du" rules faster and use more German-German words; older and rural readers react more to "Piefkinesisch" (German-German) vocabulary.
- **Minorities:** Slovene (Carinthia), Croatian and Hungarian (Burgenland), Czech, Slovak and Romani have legal protection; bilingual place names exist in some areas.
- **Austria vs Germany:** same written standard, different vocabulary, more titles, warmer closings and a softer edge. Do not reuse de-DE copy unchanged.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Recipient's titles are correct, abbreviated, highest only, and placed per Austrian rules (Mag./Dr./DI before the name; MA/MSc/MBA after and left out of the salutation).
- [ ] "Sie" throughout unless the reader has offered "du"; capitalised Sie/Ihr/Ihnen.
- [ ] Salutation fits the relationship; "Hallo" only with a first name; no "Fräulein".
- [ ] One courteous lead-in line before the request; refusals carry a reason.
- [ ] No pressure, ultimatums or "insist on our position" phrasing; a compromise path is offered.
- [ ] Requests state what, who and by when; vague timing replaced by dates.
- [ ] No hype words or unsupported superlatives; claims backed by references or data.
- [ ] Austrian vocabulary: "Jänner", Austrian food and household terms; no "lecker", "Tüte" or "Quark" in consumer copy.
- [ ] Currency reads "€ 1.234,56"; dates "17.10.2026"; 24-hour time.
- [ ] Signature block complete (title, position, company, address, phone).
- [ ] Job ads are gender-neutral, free of age or origin criteria, and state minimum gross pay plus any overpayment readiness.
- [ ] Marketing email has prior consent and an opt-out; imprint linked where required.
- [ ] No Nazi-era references or imagery; Austria not presented as Germany or with German symbols.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (German-German default, curt):

> Hallo Herr Leitner,
>
> Ihr Angebot ist zu teuer. Schicken Sie uns bis Freitag ein neues, sonst gehen wir zu einem anderen Anbieter.
>
> Gruß

**After** (for an Austrian engineer after a first meeting):

> Sehr geehrter Herr DI Leitner,
>
> vielen Dank für Ihr Angebot und das angenehme Gespräch am Dienstag. Wir haben es intern geprüft: In der vorliegenden Form liegt der Preis leider über unserem Budget für 2027. Wäre es möglich, die Positionen 3 und 4 noch einmal zu kalkulieren? Unser Gremium entscheidet am 28. Oktober, daher ersuchen wir um Ihre Rückmeldung bis Freitag, 24. Oktober.
>
> Gerne besprechen wir das auch telefonisch.
>
> Mit freundlichen Grüßen

*(Gloss: "Dear Mr DI Leitner, thank you for your offer and the pleasant conversation on Tuesday. We reviewed it internally: as it stands, the price is unfortunately above our 2027 budget. Could items 3 and 4 be recalculated? Our committee decides on 28 October, so we would ask for your reply by Friday 24 October. We are happy to discuss it by phone. Kind regards,")*

**Why:** The price problem and the deadline stay clear, but the title, the thank-you lead-in, a reason for the date and the offer to talk replace the threat. "Hallo" with a surname and the bare "Gruß" read as careless to an Austrian reader, and "ersuchen" signals a writer who knows Austrian business style.
