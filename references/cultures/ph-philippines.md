# Philippines

## In brief

Philippine professionals read and write business English fluently, and formats look American, but the expectations under the words are Asian: rank, face and harmony shape every message. Readers expect warmth, a respectful title ("Sir", "Ma'am", "Atty.", "Engr.") and requests that leave room to say no without embarrassment. A "yes" or silence does not always mean agreement, so good texts close with a specific confirmation request. Consumer copy often switches into Filipino or Taglish and leans on family, humour and emotion. Advertising claims, the national flag, age in job ads and personal data are regulated.

## Voice and tone

- Use clear, correct, polite English. Readers are educated in English, so do not write simplified "beginner" English, which reads as condescending; plain and friendly is right.
- Keep the tone calm and kind. Blame, irony and anything that makes a person look foolish cause "hiya" (shame, loss of face) and damage the relationship more than a slow answer would.
- State opinions modestly ("I think", "maybe we can consider"). A lecturing or know-it-all voice reads as arrogant.
- Philippine office English has its own normal formulas: "Good day!", "Please be informed that...", "Kindly see attached", "May we request...". Do not "correct" a local writer's use of them; foreign writers do not need to copy them.
- Taglish (mixed English and Filipino) is normal in chat and consumer copy, not a sign of weak English. Outsiders should only use it when written or checked by a native writer.
- Filipino copy should be everyday Filipino with familiar English loanwords (i-save, naka-attach), not stiff dictionary words. Even direct instructions use the "paki-" request prefix: "Paki-check po" (please check).

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s), then the mother's maiden surname as a middle name or initial, then the father's surname (Maria Cristina R. Santos). Address by the last surname: Ms. Santos, never Ms. Reyes. Use double given names in full unless invited otherwise.
- Professional titles are forms of address: "Atty. Cruz", "Engr. Bautista", "Dr. Lim", "Arch. Reyes". Keep them in salutations and signatures.
- "Sir" and "Ma'am" are everyday courtesy toward superiors, clients and customers, alone or with a first name ("Hi Ma'am Joy"). Do not read them as servile or cold, and do not make a point of dropping them.
- Nicknames (often playful or repeated syllables) are common; use one only after the person signs with it or invites it. Never joke about a nickname.
- In Filipino, "po" and "opo" (respectful yes) plus plural "kayo" mark respect to elders, superiors and customers: "Kumusta po kayo?" (How are you?). Leaving them out with such readers can offend. Kinship terms (Kuya, Ate, Tito, Tita) fit service and informal chat, not B2B email from a foreign company.
- Sign-offs: "Best regards" and "Thank you" are safe; "Respectfully yours" is normal upward; "Salamat po" (thank you, respectful) is warm in Filipino. Formal Filipino letters open with "Ginoo:" or "Ginang:" and may close "Lubos na gumagalang," (most respectfully).

## Structuring a message

1. A clear subject line; salutation with title; a warm opener ("Good day!", "I hope you are well").
2. Purpose in the first paragraph. In a request to a senior person, put a short reason just before the ask.
3. Details in short paragraphs or bullets, with examples or screenshots when you want something done a certain way. "Make it more professional" will not get the result you want.
4. A specific confirmation request: "Could you confirm you can send the files by Thursday, November 12?" rather than "Does this make sense?"
5. Thanks and a courteous close.

- Copy only people who need to know, with the most senior first. Adding someone's boss to push them causes loss of face.
- Viber, WhatsApp and SMS are normal business channels; a short friendly chat nudge after an email is expected, not pushy.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Do not phrase a request so the reader must refuse openly. Offer options ("If Friday is tight, Monday also works") so they can choose without losing face.
- Decode soft replies: "We'll try our best", "Let me check", a long pause or a vague "yes" may mean no or not yet. Confirm what will happen and by when.
- Refuse with appreciation, a reason and an alternative. Disagree by suggesting another way rather than contradicting.
- Criticism goes privately, one to one, never in a group chat or reply-all. Praise can be shared openly; corrections stay between the two of you.
- Frame problems as shared and about the work: "The report needs a few adjustments in section 2" rather than "You got this wrong". Open with something specific that went well and close with encouragement and a date.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- B2B: buyers weigh whether they like and trust the people behind an offer, not only the numbers. Hard selling and promises that sound too good raise suspicion.
- Consumer: family, love, everyday struggle, humour and local settings work well; emotion must feel sincere. Taglish headlines and influencers carry weight. Promotions, vouchers and payday or double-date sales (9.9, 11.11) drive online buying.
- The advertising self-regulator requires independent proof for "No. 1", "best", "most trusted" and absolute claims ("guaranteed", "100%"), including inside hashtags. Comparisons must state their basis and never disparage a rival. Testimonials must be genuine and framed as personal opinion.
- Prices: advertised prices carry "SRP"; discounts show the earlier price; a "was" price must be real and recent; installment offers show the total payable. Nationwide contests and raffles need a permit applied for well before launch. Ads should respect family values and not show the country or its people in a demeaning way.
- CVs: one page for juniors, up to two for experienced people, reverse chronological, with metrics (BPO roles cite KPIs and shift experience). A 2x2 photo and birth date are still common for local, government and customer-facing employers; multinationals and foreign employers usually prefer neither. Follow the ad. "Biodata" (with civil status, height, religion) is a separate, traditional format.
- Job ads: no age limits or age preferences, and application forms may not demand age or birth date. Asking for marital status, religion or health is sensitive data that needs a clear reason and consent.

## Humor and emphasis

- Light, warm humour helps in established relationships and consumer copy; bittersweet "hugot" lines are a popular format. No humour at a person's expense, and no jokes upward in formal mail.
- Ads may not mock anyone for gender, class, religion, ethnicity, appearance, disability or age, or ridicule a competitor.
- Exclamation marks in greetings and thanks are normal; capitals and "!!" in complaints read as shouting.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Criticism of the Philippines, its politics, corruption or recent presidents by a foreigner lands badly, even as a joke. Stay neutral on the South China Sea dispute; Philippine readers call their part of it the West Philippine Sea.
- **Religion:** the country is majority Catholic, with a significant Muslim population mainly in Mindanao. Religious references must be respectful, Catholic imagery (Santo Niño, fiestas) is familiar but never flippant, and not all readers are Christian.
- **Flag and anthem:** the national flag may not appear in ads, logos or merchandise, or be altered; the anthem is restricted. National heroes and landmarks only appear respectfully.
- **Libel:** defamation is a crime, and online defamation carries a heavier penalty. Do not name and shame a supplier, employer or competitor in posts or reviews.
- **Personal data:** collection needs a lawful basis, a clear privacy notice and specific, recorded consent; sensitive data (age, marital status, religion, health) has stricter rules. Marketing messages need consent and an easy opt-out.
- Ads involving women and children must not demean or endanger them; ads may not exploit superstition. Check the regulator's current rules before using AI-generated people or voices.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Holy Week is solemn: many businesses close on Holy Thursday and Good Friday, and loud promotions then feel wrong. The Christmas season is long, and work slows from mid-December.
- Round shapes and polka dots signal prosperity at New Year. Some readers avoid 13; Chinese-Filipino business families observe Lunar New Year and may favour 8 and avoid 4.
- Pointing fingers and hands on hips read as aggressive in imagery. The barong tagalog is formal business dress for men and a positive image.

## Variation within the culture

- **Language:** Filipino (Tagalog-based) and English are official; Cebuano, Ilocano, Hiligaynon and other regional languages are first languages for many readers. Nationwide, English often reads as more neutral than Tagalog, especially in the Visayas and Mindanao.
- **Mindanao and Muslim communities:** Bangsamoro and other Muslim readers need halal-aware food copy, Ramadan and Eid timing, and no Christian-default greetings.
- **Sector:** BPO, tech, multinationals and start-ups use first names, quicker replies and lean CVs; government, family-owned firms and traditional SMEs keep titles, letters and biodata. Government contacts may use personal email or messaging apps while keeping formal content.
- **Generation:** younger readers mix English and Filipino freely and like humour; older readers expect po, titles and sentimental rather than ironic tone.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt, task-first style):

> Hi Jun, the onboarding guide has too many errors. Fix sections 3 and 4 and send it back by Friday. Let me know if you have questions.

**After** (for Engr. Arnel "Jun" Villanueva, a client-side team lead, after a first call):

> Good day, Engr. Villanueva!
>
> Thank you for sharing the onboarding guide. The process flow in section 2 is very clear, and our trainers liked it.
>
> We noticed that some steps in sections 3 and 4 no longer match the new system screens, so we have marked them in the attached file. Would it be possible for your team to update those parts? If the revised version could reach us by Friday, November 20, we can still prepare the training slides on time.
>
> Could you confirm if that date works for you? If it is tight, we are happy to discuss another schedule by call.
>
> Thank you very much, and have a great week ahead!
>
> Best regards,
> Karen Lopez

**Why:** The nickname waits until he uses it, and his professional title leads. Specific praise comes first and "errors" become a factual mismatch with a reason, so no one loses face. The deadline stays but turns into a request with a purpose, and an alternative lets him raise problems. The close asks him to confirm the date instead of relying on silence, and the warm sign-off fits Philippine email.
