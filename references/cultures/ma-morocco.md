# Morocco

## In brief

In Morocco the first decision is the language. Private-sector business, banking and procurement mostly write in French; government, law and official texts use Modern Standard Arabic (MSA); consumer ads and everyday speech use Darija, the Moroccan Arabic vernacular; Tamazight, written in Tifinagh, is co-official and spreading through public signage and forms. Whatever the language, readers expect courtesy, respect for rank and a warm relationship before a hard request, and a "no" usually arrives softly or as a delay. Moroccan Arabic is not Egyptian or Gulf Arabic: digits, month names and vocabulary differ. Three topics are legal red lines: the monarchy, Islam and the country's territorial integrity, including how the Sahara is named and mapped.

## Voice and tone

- **Pick the language deliberately.** French for B2B with private firms, banks, insurers and most multinationals; MSA for ministries, courts, public bodies and religious or institutional content; Darija for mass-market consumer copy and social media; English only where you know the reader prefers it. When unsure, ask which language the contact works in.
- French-only copy for a mass market can read as elite, since French fluency is concentrated in higher-income groups. Pure MSA ad copy can feel stiff and distant.
- Code-switching (French terms inside Arabic or Darija) is normal in speech, chat and ads, especially for tech words. Keep one language in contracts and formal letters.
- Darija appears in Arabic script (broad audiences, older readers) or in Latin script with digits for Arabic sounds, such as 3 and 7 (young, digital audiences). Choose per channel.
- Religious phrases such as "inshallah" (God willing) and "hamdoullah" (thanks be to God) are everyday language. You need not add them, but do not strip them from a native text.
- Use Moroccan forms, not Egyptian or Gulf ones: Latin digits, Moroccan month names, local vocabulary. A Mashreq-flavoured text is spotted at once.

## Addressing people

- **French:** "Madame," / "Monsieur," or "Bonjour Monsieur Benali," in email; titles count ("Docteur", "Professeur", "Maître" for lawyers). Letters to an office holder open with the function ("Monsieur le Directeur,"). Closings follow French usage, from "Cordialement" to the full formal formula in official letters; mirror the reader.
- **Arabic:** a title, the name and "المحترم/المحترمة" (respected), then "تحية طيبة وبعد" (warm greetings, and then). Moroccan administrative practice has its own set phrases; have formal Arabic letters checked by a native writer.
- Spoken courtesies: "Salam" or "As-salamu alaykum" (peace be upon you), "Labas?" (all well?), "choukran" (thanks), "baraka llahou fik" (God bless you, warm thanks), "mabrouk" (congratulations). These belong to speech and chat, not formal email.
- "Sidi" and "Lalla" are respectful forms, and "Lalla" precedes princesses' names. Darija verbs and apologies change with gender and number, so direct-address copy must handle men, women and groups.
- **The King:** official Arabic uses "صاحب الجلالة الملك محمد السادس نصره الله" or at least "جلالة الملك"; French uses "Sa Majesté le Roi" (SM le Roi), and royal family members "Son Altesse Royale" (SAR). A bare surname or casual reference reads as disrespect.
- Say "Amazigh" (people) and "Tamazight" (language) in public copy; many find "Berber" outdated or pejorative.

## Structuring a message

1. Subject line naming the topic.
2. Salutation, greeting and one line of goodwill ("J'espère que vous allez bien").
3. Short context, then the purpose and request.
4. Next step with date, owner and place; mention attachments.
5. Courteous close with an offer to call or meet; full signature with title and company.

- After any call or meeting, send a written recap of prices, commitments and dates. If email goes unanswered, a phone call or WhatsApp message often gets a faster reply.
- Formal steps often need signed, stamped documents. Company documents carry legal identifiers (ICE, IF, RC); Moroccan partners expect yours too.
- Send meeting requests early and reconfirm a day or two before. Avoid Friday midday prayer time; during Ramadan, working days shrink to about six hours.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with thanks, a reason and an alternative: "Nous apprécions votre proposition ; le budget ne permet pas cette option cette année, mais la phase 1 reste possible."
- Criticism stays private: no corrections in group threads, no widening the copy list on a complaint. Describe the issue, not the person, since disagreement is easily felt as personal.
- Decode soft signals: "inshallah", "on va voir", praise without a date, or silence are not commitments. Ask a specific, easy follow-up.
- Older and more traditional readers avoid open commitment more than young graduates do. Many staff expect explicit thanks for effort, and sparing praise can read as not valuing them.
- No ultimatums, public pressure or "final offer" language. Give decision-makers time to consult.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with references, known local partners, guarantees, after-sales support and family or community benefit. Football and national pride resonate in consumer copy; humour and wordplay in Darija are standard in ads.
- Consumer protection law requires ads to be identifiable and to name the advertiser; bans misleading claims on price, origin, results or quantity; allows comparative ads only on verifiable, like-for-like facts; requires prices including VAT and all charges; requires filed rules and a prize list for contests; and gives a 7-day withdrawal right for distance sales. Labels and manuals must be at least in Arabic.
- Marketing email and SMS need prior consent (with a narrow exception for existing customers) and a working opt-out. Forms collecting personal data carry a notice citing Law 09-08 and the data regulator (CNDP) filing number.
- The audiovisual regulator criticises ads that keep women in domestic roles and men in authority; show balanced roles.
- CVs: one or two pages, in French or Arabic depending on the employer; flawless French matters. Many traditional employers still see a photo, age and marital status; multinationals and tech firms lean to minimal personal data. Bilingual or trilingual skill is a strong selling point.
- Job ads: the labour code bans discrimination on race, colour, sex, disability, marital status, religion, political opinion, union membership, national ancestry and social origin. Drop "jeune", gender and marital-status filters; avoid age limits too.

## Humor and emphasis

- Warm, light humour helps relationship messages. Never joke about God, the Prophet, Islam, the King or the Sahara; such content can be criminal, not just off-tone.
- Sexual innuendo has no place in business text. Praise of a person or child is often paired with a blessing such as "tbarkallah"; do not mock this.
- Latin-script Darija stretches vowels for spoken emphasis in youth copy; keep it out of formal text.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Monarchy, Islam, territorial integrity:** the penal code (art. 267-5, as reported in press coverage) and the press code punish insulting Islam or the monarchy and inciting against territorial integrity, with heavier penalties for public or online content. People have been jailed for social posts.
- **The Sahara:** Moroccan readers, media and officials say "Moroccan Sahara" / "Sahara marocain" / "الصحراء المغربية" or "provinces du Sud". Maps that leave out the Sahara or draw a line across it have triggered boycotts and public apologies from brands and organisations. The territory's status is disputed internationally, the UN uses "Western Sahara", and Algerian and Sahrawi readers see it differently. For Moroccan audiences, show the full map as Moroccans expect or use no map.
- **Israel and Palestine:** strongly felt; keep it out of business content.
- **Sexuality and morals:** same-sex relations and sex outside marriage are criminal offences; keep imagery and copy conservative.
- **Officials:** naming and criticising officials in public copy carries defamation risk.
- **Ramadan:** no daytime eating, drinking or smoking in imagery or event plans; do not assume every reader is Muslim (there is a historic Jewish community and a growing sub-Saharan population).
- Legal items are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Mint tea is a natural image of welcome. Show giving and eating with the right hand; avoid soles of feet facing people.
- Dress modestly in imagery; no intimacy between unrelated men and women. Do not assume alcohol in gifts or promotions.
- Tifinagh text must come from a competent translator; errors on signs draw public criticism.

## Variation within the culture

- **Language and class:** a large share of Moroccans speak only Darija; French and English cluster among the young, urban and higher-income. Rural and older audiences need Arabic script and plain Darija or MSA.
- **Amazigh regions** (Rif, Middle Atlas, Souss) speak Tamazight varieties at home; public bodies are adding Tamazight to signs, documents and websites in stages to about 2034. Business and ads still rarely use it.
- **North and south:** Spanish is common around Tangier and Tetouan; Hassaniya Arabic is spoken in the far south.
- **Sectors:** finance, consulting and multinationals in Casablanca and Rabat write French, hold deadlines and are more direct; ministries and public firms work in Arabic and move slowly; tech and startups mix French, Darija and English and use first names sooner.
- **Generations:** young graduates commit and refuse more openly; elders expect more deference and blessing formulas.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Language matches the reader: French for private B2B, MSA for official or public bodies, Darija for mass consumer copy; not English-only by default.
- [ ] Arabic uses Moroccan conventions: Latin digits, Moroccan month names, no Egyptian or Gulf dialect.
- [ ] French uses "vous", "Monsieur/Madame" + surname or function title, and correct French spelling and spacing.
- [ ] A greeting and goodwill line precede the request; the request still appears in the first paragraph or two.
- [ ] Refusals carry thanks, a reason and an alternative; criticism is private and about the issue.
- [ ] Next steps name date, owner and place; nothing relies on "inshallah" or silence as agreement.
- [ ] No ultimatums or pressure for an immediate decision.
- [ ] The King and royals carry full honorifics; nothing mocks the monarchy, Islam or national unity.
- [ ] Any map shows Morocco with the Sahara and no dividing line, or there is no map; wording does not treat the territory as separate.
- [ ] Claims are true and verifiable; comparisons are like-for-like; prices include VAT and all charges.
- [ ] Contests have filed rules and a prize list; marketing messages have consent and an opt-out; data forms cite Law 09-08 and the CNDP number.
- [ ] Job ads have no gender, marital-status, religion, origin or age filters.
- [ ] Imagery is modest, gender roles balanced, no daytime eating in Ramadan scenes, right hand used.
- [ ] Deadlines avoid Ramadan, Eid and August; the time zone is stated near the Ramadan clock change.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt international style, in English):

> Hi Karim, your invoice has errors again. Fix it and resend today, otherwise payment slips to next month.

**After** (to a supplier's finance manager in Casablanca, in French with gloss):

> Bonjour Monsieur Alaoui,
>
> J'espère que vous allez bien, et merci encore pour la livraison de la semaine dernière. En préparant le paiement, nous avons relevé deux écarts sur la facture n° 2481 : le taux de TVA et la quantité de la ligne 3. Le détail figure en pièce jointe. Pourriez-vous nous envoyer une facture rectifiée d'ici le jeudi 22 octobre ? Elle pourra ainsi passer dans le paiement de fin de mois. Je reste disponible par téléphone si un point mérite d'être vérifié ensemble.
>
> Bien cordialement,

*(Gloss: "Hello Mr Alaoui, I hope you are well, and thank you again for last week's delivery. While preparing payment we found two discrepancies on invoice 2481: the VAT rate and the quantity on line 3. Details attached. Could you send us a corrected invoice by Thursday 22 October? It can then go into the end-of-month payment run. I am available by phone if a point is worth checking together. Kind regards,")*

**Why:** French is the natural language for a finance exchange with a Moroccan firm, and "Monsieur" + surname replaces the first name. Thanks come first, "errors again" becomes two specific points about the document, not the person, and the threat becomes a dated request tied to a business reason. The phone offer lets the supplier raise a problem privately before the deadline.
