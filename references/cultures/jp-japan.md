# Japan

## In brief

Japanese professional communication is high-context, highly polite and hierarchy-aware. Harmony (wa) and face are protected by indirectness: refusals and criticism are rarely stated outright, and readers infer meaning from tone and omission. Written Japanese has elaborate honorific systems (keigo) and set formulas for openings and closings. Humility is expected; bold self-promotion damages trust. Precision, reliability and meticulous detail are signs of respect.

## Voice and tone

- Polite, humble, precise, calm. Apologies are frequent and are a courtesy, not necessarily an admission of fault.
- In Japanese, the choice between plain form, polite form (です/ます) and keigo (sonkeigo for the reader's actions, kenjōgo for one's own) is critical. Machine-translated text with inconsistent politeness levels is immediately noticeable.
- When writing in English for Japanese readers: plain, short sentences; no idioms, slang, sarcasm or culturally specific jokes. Many Japanese professionals read English better than they speak it.
- Avoid casual tone with customers. Consumer brands may be friendly, but are still more polite than Western equivalents.

## Addressing people

- Name order in Japanese: family name, then given name (山田太郎 = YAMADA Taro). Since 2020 the government recommends the same order in English (YAMADA Taro), but many people still use Western order in English. Capitalizing the family name avoids ambiguity.
- Use family name plus honorific: -sama (様) in business correspondence and for customers; -san (さん) among colleagues. Never use an honorific for yourself or members of your own company when speaking to outsiders.
- Business email address block: company name, department, title, name + 様. E.g. 株式会社ABC 営業部 部長 山田様.
- For organizations: 御中 (onchū) after the company or department name when no person is specified.
- In English: "Dear Mr Yamada" or "Dear Yamada-san". Avoid first names unless invited.
- Titles by role (部長 bucho, 社長 shacho, 先生 sensei for doctors, teachers, lawyers) can replace names.

## Structuring a message

Typical Japanese business email:

1. Addressee block (company, department, name + 様).
2. Opening set phrase: いつもお世話になっております (thank you for your continued support). First contact: 初めてご連絡いたします and a self-introduction with company and role.
3. Main content: background first, then the request, stated politely and precisely.
4. Closing set phrase: よろしくお願いいたします. Formal letters may begin with a seasonal greeting and end with a matching closing.
5. Signature with full company details.

- Send detailed information and documents in advance; Japanese counterparts prepare thoroughly.
- Follow up meetings with a written summary.
- Paper and PDF documents with visuals and tables are often preferred over long slide decks.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- When writing for Japanese readers: never refuse bluntly. Use cushion words (クッション言葉) such as 恐れ入りますが (I'm afraid), 申し訳ございませんが (I'm sorry, but), and offer an alternative.
- Criticism is given privately, indirectly and often through intermediaries. Frame problems as shared challenges ("we would like to discuss how we might improve...").
- When interpreting Japanese source text for direct cultures: decode soft signals. "We will consider it positively" may still mean no; "it is somewhat difficult" usually means no.
- Silence or delay is not necessarily rejection; consensus takes time.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with reliability, quality, detail, safety, track record and long-term partnership. Data tables and specifications are welcome.
- Endorsements, rankings, awards and customer testimonials carry weight, but must be genuine.
- Japan's Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations (景品表示法) prohibits misleading superiority claims ("No. 1" needs objective basis and source). Since October 2023 undisclosed sponsored content (stealth marketing) is illegal.
- Cute characters (kawaii, mascots) and seasonal themes (cherry blossom, autumn leaves) are mainstream marketing devices, even for serious institutions.
- CV: the standard rirekisho (履歴書) format, traditionally with photo, education and work history in chronological order; mid-career applicants add a shokumu keirekisho (職務経歴書). Japanese-language CVs follow a fixed template; Western-style CVs are accepted mainly by foreign-affiliated firms.
- Self-introductions emphasize the company and role before the individual.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor is rare in business writing with outsiders and does not translate well. Avoid irony and sarcasm.
- Emoji and kaomoji are common in personal and some internal chat, not in external business email.
- Emphasis is shown through politeness intensity and structure rather than exclamation marks.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- WWII and Japan's wartime conduct are sensitive. Do not frame individual Japanese people as responsible ("you Japanese...").
- Territorial disputes: Senkaku Islands (with China), Takeshima/Dokdo (with South Korea), Northern Territories/Southern Kurils (with Russia). The Sea of Japan / East Sea naming is disputed by Korea. Maps for Japanese audiences that use the "wrong" names or borders provoke complaints, and vice versa for Korean and Chinese audiences.
- The Rising Sun flag is accepted in Japan but highly offensive in Korea and China; never use it in pan-Asian content.
- Discrimination against Burakumin, Ainu, Okinawans and Zainichi Koreans is a sensitive social issue.
- Domestic politics and the Imperial family: avoid comment; jokes about the Emperor are taboo.
- Natural disasters (2011 earthquake and tsunami, Fukushima) must not be trivialized.
- Avoid "exotic and mysterious Japan" stereotypes (nihonjinron clichés, samurai/geisha imagery for modern business).

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- 4 (shi, sounds like "death") and 9 (ku, sounds like "suffering") are unlucky. Avoid them in prices, sets of gifts, room numbers or product counts where possible.
- White and black together, white chrysanthemums and white envelopes are associated with funerals. Red and white are celebratory.
- Chopsticks standing upright in rice evoke funerals.
- The Buddhist manji (卍) appears on maps to mark temples and has no Nazi connotation in Japan, but should be avoided in content for Western audiences.
- Tattoos carry yakuza associations for many people; tattooed models may be poorly received in mainstream ads.
- Hand gestures: beckoning is palm-down; pointing with a finger is impolite.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | YYYY/MM/DD (2026/03/07) or 2026年3月7日. The Japanese era calendar is used in official forms: 令和8年 (Reiwa 8) = 2026. |
| Time | 24-hour in schedules (14:30); 午後2時30分 in text. Business hours may be written past 24:00 (e.g. 25:00 = 1 a.m.). |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89, but large numbers are grouped by 10,000 (万 man) and 100 million (億 oku): 1億2,345万. Convert carefully; "10 million" is 1,000万. |
| Currency | ¥1,234 or 1,234円; yen has no decimals in practice. |
| Week starts | Sunday in most calendars. |
| Units | Metric; traditional units for some items (tsubo for floor area, jō for tatami rooms). |
| Phone | +81 3-1234-5678 |
| Address | Written from largest to smallest unit: postal code (〒100-0001), prefecture, city, district, block numbers, building, then name. |
| Typography | Full-width Japanese punctuation (、。「」). No spaces between words. Line breaking rules (kinsoku) matter in UI. |

## Variation within the culture

- Tokyo is more formal and hierarchical; Osaka and the Kansai region are known for directness, humor and a merchant pragmatism.
- Startups, gaming and IT companies are more casual; traditional corporations, finance, government and manufacturing keep strict forms.
- Younger professionals use chat apps (LINE, Slack) with lighter politeness internally but still adapt strongly to rank.
- Foreign-affiliated companies (外資系) are closer to Western norms.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No blunt refusals, direct criticism or "you're wrong" phrasing; cushion words and alternatives are used.
- [ ] Politeness level is consistent and appropriate (keigo in external business text).
- [ ] Addressee uses family name + 様 (or Mr/Ms + family name in English); no first names by default.
- [ ] Set opening and closing phrases are present in Japanese business email.
- [ ] Self-promotion and superlatives are toned down; claims cite objective sources.
- [ ] Exclamation marks, slang, idioms and sarcasm are removed.
- [ ] Deadlines, specifications and logistics are exact.
- [ ] No references to 4 or 9 in pricing/sets where avoidable; no funeral color combinations.
- [ ] Maps and place names follow Japanese usage for Japanese audiences; no Rising Sun flag in pan-Asian content.
- [ ] Large numbers are correctly converted to 万/億 grouping.
- [ ] Dates use Japanese formats; era years where official forms require them.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (direct Western rejection):

> Unfortunately, your proposal doesn't meet our requirements. The price is too high and the timeline is unrealistic. We're going with another vendor.

**After** (tailored for Japanese readers, in English):

> Thank you very much for your detailed proposal and for the time your team has dedicated to it. After careful internal review, we regret that we are unable to proceed with it on this occasion, mainly due to budget and schedule constraints on our side. We sincerely appreciate your understanding, and we would be grateful for the opportunity to consult with you on future projects.

**Why:** The refusal is still clear, but it thanks the vendor, takes responsibility for the constraint ("on our side"), avoids judging their work and preserves the relationship for the future.
