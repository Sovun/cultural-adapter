# Japan

## In brief

Japanese professional communication is high-context, highly polite and shaped by rank and by the line between one's own side and the other (uchi/soto). Harmony (wa) and face are protected by indirectness: criticism is wrapped and private, and a refusal is cushioned and placed late, though Japanese etiquette guides still want it recognisable. Written Japanese has an elaborate honorific system (keigo) and set formulas for openings and closings. Claims need proof; bold self-promotion damages trust. Precision, reliability and attention to detail are signs of respect.

## Voice and tone

- Polite, humble, precise, calm. Apology formulas are frequent and work as courtesy (for a late reply, a first contact, an imposition), not only as admissions of fault.
- In Japanese, choose consistently between plain form, polite form (です/ます) and keigo (sonkeigo for the reader's actions, kenjōgo for one's own). Mixed levels, typical of machine translation, are noticed at once.
- Phrase requests as polite questions, not imperatives: ご確認いただけますでしょうか rather than 確認してください.
- Two registers: B2B correspondence uses full keigo; app, help and consumer copy uses friendly です/ます with few honorifics (ご確認ください, お問い合わせください). Business-letter keigo in an app sounds stiff; app casualness in a client email sounds rude.
- Do not translate English literally: drop "you" (あなた) and "we", use role nouns (お客様) or subjectless forms, and rewrite punchy copy rather than translate it.
- When writing in English for Japanese readers: plain, short sentences; no idioms, slang or sarcasm. Many professionals read English better than they speak it.

## Addressing people

- Name order in Japanese: family name, then given name (山田太郎). In English either order appears; capitalizing the family name (YAMADA Taro) avoids ambiguity.
- Family name plus 様 (-sama) in business correspondence and for customers; さん once the relationship is established or among colleagues.
- Address block: full official company name (never drop 株式会社), department, title, name + 様: 株式会社ABC 営業部 部長 山田太郎様. Several recipients and cc lines go in order of seniority.
- 御中 after a company or department when no person is named; 各位 for a group (関係者各位). Never stack honorifics: not 御中 plus 様, not 社長様 (write 山田社長 or 社長の山田様).
- The reader's company is 貴社 in writing (御社 is spoken); one's own is 弊社.
- Uchi/soto: to outsiders, one's own colleagues and boss get no honorific and humble verbs: 弊社の山田が申しておりました, not 山田部長がおっしゃいました.
- Do not ask a senior directly about their wishes or abilities (〜したいですか); offer, or ask about facts.
- In English: "Dear Mr Yamada" or "Dear Yamada-san". Avoid first names unless invited. Role titles (部長, 社長, 先生 for doctors, teachers, lawyers) can replace names.

## Structuring a message

Typical Japanese business email:

1. Subject line that states the purpose, often tagged: 【ご確認】, 【お願い】; 【再送】 for a resend.
2. Addressee block (company, department, title, name + 様).
3. Greeting: いつもお世話になっております; お世話になります for a first message; 突然のご連絡失礼いたします for cold contact; お疲れ様です internally.
4. Self-identification: 株式会社〇〇の山田と申します.
5. The purpose in one or two lines, then details. Apologise first for a late reply (返信が遅くなりまして申し訳ございません).
6. Closing: よろしくお願いいたします (more humble: 何卒よろしくお願い申し上げます); for proposals ご検討のほどよろしくお願いいたします; for attachments ご査収のほどよろしくお願い申し上げます.
7. Signature block below a divider line with company, department, name, phone, email.

- Japanese emails use short lines, blank lines between topics and no colour or bold. Keep them concise.
- Reply the same day if possible; if the full answer takes longer, acknowledge and give a date. Report problems early (horenso).
- Send agendas and documents in advance; follow meetings with a written summary of agreements, owners and next steps.
- Your reader may not have authority to commit. Ask who else should be involved instead of pressing for a yes.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Never open with the "no". Order a refusal: thanks, restate the request, show it was seriously considered, give one reason in full sentences (not bullets), the cushioned refusal, an alternative, then a line about the continued relationship (今後ともよろしくお願いいたします).
- Cushions: 恐れ入りますが, 大変恐縮ですが, 誠に残念ながら, お手数ですが. Polite inability: できかねます / いたしかねます rather than できません. Standard decline: 大変恐縮ではございますが、今回は見送らせていただきます.
- One apology and one honest reason; never blame or belittle the other party.
- Criticism is given privately and indirectly. No reply-all corrections, no cc of seniors on a peer's mistake. Frame problems as shared ("we would like to discuss how we might improve...").
- Decoding Japanese replies: 検討します, 考えておきます and ちょっと難しいです often mean no; "we will consider it positively" may still be a no. Japanese etiquette writers criticise vague refusals, so do not copy the ambiguity when you write.
- "Yes" (はい) or silence often means "I heard you", not agreement, and a missing reply is not consent. Check understanding with open questions.
- Silence or delay is not necessarily rejection; consensus takes time.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with reliability, quality, detail, safety, track record and long-term partnership. Put proof (satisfaction figures, local testimonials, awards) in the first impression, not in fine print. Dense specifications are welcome.
- Typos, inconsistent terms and "good enough" copy hurt credibility more than in most markets. Witty Western copy needs transcreation.
- Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations (景品表示法, enforced by the Consumer Affairs Agency): no misleading quality or price claims; the agency can demand evidence for performance claims, so every "No.1", "100%" or efficacy claim needs a documented basis and source next to it.
- Since October 2023 stealth marketing is illegal under the same Act. The advertiser is liable for posts it requested or controlled. Label them clearly in Japanese (広告, PR, プロモーション); an English-only "#ad" or a tag buried among hashtags is risky. The first enforcement order (2024) hit discounts given for good reviews.
- Online shops must carry a 特定商取引法に基づく表記 page (price including shipping, payment, delivery, return terms, seller name, address, phone) and a clear final order screen (Specified Commercial Transactions Act).
- Seasonal themes (cherry blossom, autumn leaves) and cute characters (mascots) are mainstream, even for serious institutions.
- Job ads: no age limits or preferences (20代活躍中, 若者歓迎), with narrow legal exceptions that must be named; no sex preference (女性歓迎, 男性限定); gender-neutral titles (営業職 not 営業マン, 看護師, 保育士).
- CV: the rirekisho (履歴書) is a fixed form with photo, chronological education and work history, motivation (志望動機) and self-PR; the 2021 Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare model form makes gender optional. Mid-career applicants add a shokumu keirekisho (職務経歴書). Tone: modest but specific, with figures; never criticise a former employer. Western CVs suit mainly foreign-affiliated and IT firms.
- Self-introductions put the company and role before the individual.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor is rare in business writing with outsiders and does not translate well. Drop idioms rather than swap in Japanese ones; avoid irony and sarcasm.
- Emoji and kaomoji are common in personal and some internal chat (LINE, Slack), not in external business email.
- Emphasis comes from politeness intensity, structure and 【】 brackets, not exclamation marks, colour or bold.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- WWII and Japan's wartime conduct are sensitive. Do not frame individual Japanese people as responsible.
- Territorial disputes: Senkaku (China), Takeshima/Dokdo (South Korea), Northern Territories/Southern Kurils (Russia); Sea of Japan / East Sea naming. Maps follow Japanese usage for Japanese audiences and provoke complaints when used for Korean or Chinese ones, and vice versa.
- The Rising Sun flag is accepted in Japan but highly offensive in Korea and China; never use it in pan-Asian content.
- Discrimination against Burakumin, Ainu, Okinawans and Zainichi Koreans is a sensitive issue. The Imperial family: no comment, no jokes. Do not trivialize disasters (2011 earthquake, Fukushima) or use "exotic Japan" clichés (samurai, geisha) for modern business.
- Marketing email needs prior opt-in consent and must show the sender's name, postal address, a contact and an opt-out link (Act on Regulation of Transmission of Specified Electronic Mail).
- Personal data: Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI), regulator the Personal Information Protection Commission. Privacy notices state the purpose of use. A 2026 amendment (administrative fines, stricter rules for children's and biometric data) was promulgated in July 2026 but was not yet in force at last check; check current status.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- 4 (shi, "death") and 9 (ku, "suffering") are unlucky. Avoid them in prices, gift sets and product counts where possible.
- White chrysanthemums, lilies and black-and-white combinations evoke funerals; red and white are celebratory. Chopsticks standing upright in rice evoke funerals.
- The manji (卍) marks temples on maps with no Nazi connotation in Japan, but avoid it in content for Western audiences.
- Tattoos carry yakuza associations for many people; tattooed models may be poorly received in mainstream ads.
- Many companies close over New Year (about 29 Dec-3 Jan), Golden Week (late April-early May) and Obon (mid-August); plan deadlines and campaigns around them.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | 2026/03/07 or 2026年3月7日(土). Era calendar on official forms: 令和8年 = 2026. Do not mix both systems in one document. |
| Time | 24-hour in schedules (14:30); 午後2時30分 in text. Business hours may run past 24:00 (25:00 = 1 a.m.). |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89, but large numbers group by 万 (10,000) and 億 (100 million): 1億2,345万. "10 million" is 1,000万. |
| Currency | ¥1,234 or 1,234円; no decimals in practice. |
| Week starts | Sunday in most calendars. |
| Units | Metric; tsubo for floor area, jō for tatami rooms. |
| Phone | 03-1234-5678; +81 3-1234-5678 |
| Address | Largest to smallest: 〒100-0001, prefecture, city, district, block numbers, building, then name. |
| Typography | Full-width punctuation (、。「」『』); half-width digits and Latin letters; no spaces between words; line-breaking rules (kinsoku) matter in UI. |

## Variation within the culture

- Tokyo is more formal; Osaka and Kansai are known for directness, humor and merchant pragmatism.
- Startups, gaming, IT and foreign-affiliated firms (外資系) are more casual; traditional corporations, finance, government and manufacturing keep strict forms. Family-run and smaller firms often decide top-down and faster.
- Younger professionals use lighter politeness in internal chat but still adapt strongly to rank.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No blunt refusals or "you're wrong" phrasing; any refusal is cushioned, placed late, still clear and offers an alternative.
- [ ] Politeness level consistent: keigo in client mail, light です/ます in UI copy; no stacked honorifics, no honorifics for one's own staff.
- [ ] Address: full company name, family name + 様 (Mr/Ms + family name in English); 御中/各位 used correctly.
- [ ] Subject line, greeting, self-identification, purpose up front and set closing are present in Japanese business email.
- [ ] Superlatives toned down; every "No.1" or performance claim has a sourced basis; sponsored posts labelled in Japanese.
- [ ] Job ads have no age or sex preference and use neutral titles.
- [ ] Marketing email has opt-in, sender identity and opt-out; online shops show the SCTA disclosure.
- [ ] Exclamation marks, slang, idioms and sarcasm removed; English is plain.
- [ ] Deadlines, specifications and logistics exact; "yes" not taken as agreement.
- [ ] No 4 or 9 in sets where avoidable; no funeral imagery; maps follow audience usage; no Rising Sun flag in pan-Asian content.
- [ ] Large numbers converted to 万/億; dates use one calendar system.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (direct Western rejection):

> Unfortunately, your proposal doesn't meet our requirements. The price is too high and the timeline is unrealistic. We're going with another vendor.

**After** (tailored for Japanese readers, in English):

> Thank you very much for your detailed proposal and for the time your team has dedicated to it. After careful internal review, we regret that we are unable to proceed with it on this occasion, mainly due to budget and schedule constraints on our side. We sincerely appreciate your understanding, and we would be grateful for the opportunity to consult with you on future projects.

**Why:** It follows the Japanese refusal order: thanks, careful consideration, one reason placed on "our side", a clear but cushioned no, and a forward-looking close. It never judges the vendor's work.
