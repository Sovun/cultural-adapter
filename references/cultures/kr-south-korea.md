# South Korea

## In brief

Korean professional communication is shaped by strict hierarchy (age, rank, company), face (chemyeon) and a fast-paced, competitive work culture ("ppalli-ppalli", hurry-hurry). Language encodes status through speech levels and honorifics, so tone must match the reader's position. Refusals are indirect, but decisions can be quick. National pride is strong and historical issues with Japan are highly sensitive. Presentation quality matters: polished, visually strong documents signal respect.

## Voice and tone

- Polite, respectful, efficient. Visual polish (design, charts, clean layout) matters a lot for credibility.
- In Korean, choose the speech level deliberately: formal deferential (합쇼체: -습니다) for external business, polite informal (해요체: -요) for friendly consumer communication. Never use plain or casual forms (반말) toward customers or seniors.
- English for Korean readers: clear, simple, no idioms or sarcasm. Reading is usually much stronger than speaking.
- Trend-awareness (K-culture, new slang, memes) drives consumer marketing; it ages quickly and must be checked by locals.

## Addressing people

- Name order: family name, then given name (김민수 = KIM Minsu). About half of Koreans are named Kim, Lee, Park, Choi or Jung, so full name plus title avoids confusion.
- Never address someone by family name alone. Use full name or given name with a suffix:
  - -님 (nim): respectful, used with titles and customers (김민수 님, 고객님 "dear customer").
  - Title + 님: 부장님 (department head), 팀장님 (team lead), 대표님 (CEO), 교수님 (professor). Very common in email: "김 부장님".
  - -씨 (ssi): for peers or juniors; inappropriate for seniors or customers.
- Email opening: "안녕하세요, 김민수 부장님." followed by a self-introduction ("ABC 회사 마케팅팀 이지은입니다.").
- Closing: "감사합니다." and full signature with company, team and title. "수고하세요" (keep up the work) should not be said to seniors.
- In English: "Dear Mr Kim" or "Dear Director Kim". Many Koreans use English names internationally; use the name they give.

## Structuring a message

- Greeting with name and title, self-introduction with company and team.
- Short context and purpose, then a clear proposal or request.
- Attach well-designed materials; slides and visuals are expected.
- Confirm agreements in writing after meetings and calls.
- KakaoTalk is used for quick business communication once a relationship exists.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refusals are softened: "검토해 보겠습니다" (we will review it), "어려울 것 같습니다" (it seems difficult).
- Do not criticize openly or show anger; it causes loss of face and can end the relationship.
- Do not joke with or contradict senior people in front of others.
- Compromise can be seen as losing; frame proposals as win-win.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with rankings, awards, market share, partner logos and testimonials; plus speed, convenience and trendiness.
- Korean Fair Labeling and Advertising Act prohibits false or exaggerated claims; "No. 1" claims require objective evidence and source disclosure. Influencer content must disclose sponsorship.
- K-beauty, K-pop and design-led aesthetics influence expectations; visual quality must be high.
- Hiring: the Fair Hiring Procedure Act (2019) prohibits asking job applicants for appearance details, birthplace, marital status or family property information. Public institutions use "blind hiring". Photos on CVs are declining and not required by law; avoid demanding them in job ads.
- CVs still typically list education prominently; university prestige matters.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor is enjoyed, especially in consumer and social media content (wordplay, internet slang, cute characters).
- Avoid humor with seniors and in formal B2B contexts.
- Exclamation marks and emoji (^^, ㅎㅎ, ㅋㅋ) are common in chat but not in formal email.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Japan**: colonial occupation (1910 to 1945), "comfort women" and forced labor are deeply sensitive. The Rising Sun flag is as offensive as Nazi symbols to many Koreans; never use it.
- **Dokdo**: Korea administers the islets and Koreans reject the Japanese name "Takeshima". **East Sea** (동해): Koreans reject "Sea of Japan" alone; dual naming "East Sea (Sea of Japan)" is a compromise in some international contexts. Korea-facing maps must use Korean usage.
- **North Korea**: avoid praising it; avoid casual jokes about it; the division of families is a real trauma. Pro-North content can be illegal under the National Security Act.
- Do not confuse Korean culture with Chinese or Japanese; disputes with China over cultural heritage (kimchi, hanbok) are sensitive.
- Domestic politics are polarized; foreigners commenting are not welcome.
- Gender issues are polarized among younger people; certain hand gestures in ads (a pinching gesture) caused major controversies as perceived anti-male symbols. Have local reviewers check imagery.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- **4** (사, sa, sounds like death) is unlucky; buildings often label the 4th floor "F".
- Writing a living person's name in **red** ink suggests death.
- Use the right hand or both hands when giving or receiving; left-hand-only gestures look disrespectful in imagery.
- Taegeukgi (national flag) must be shown correctly; errors cause complaints.
- Buddhist manji (卍) on maps marks temples, not Nazism.

## Variation within the culture

- Seoul is fast, trend-driven and international; Busan and Gyeongsang regions are known for blunter speech.
- Chaebol (Samsung, Hyundai, LG, SK, Lotte) and government are hierarchical and formal; startups and IT are flatter and may use English names internally to reduce hierarchy.
- Young Koreans (MZ generation) push back on hoesik (after-work drinking) culture and rigid hierarchy.
- Strong evangelical Christian and Buddhist communities; religion is generally personal.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Correct speech level and honorifics; title + 님 for business addressees; never family name alone.
- [ ] No blunt refusals or public criticism; win-win framing.
- [ ] Response times and deadlines are specific and fast.
- [ ] Materials are visually polished.
- [ ] No Rising Sun flag; "East Sea" and "Dokdo" used in Korea-facing content.
- [ ] No positive framing of North Korea; no jokes about the division.
- [ ] Korean culture not conflated with Chinese or Japanese culture.
- [ ] "No. 1" and superlative claims are substantiated with source.
- [ ] Job ads do not request photos, birthplace, marital status or family assets.
- [ ] Avoid 4 in prices and sets; no names in red.
- [ ] Dates and currency use Korean formats; large numbers correctly converted.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (casual English to a senior Korean partner):

> Hey Minsu, the numbers you sent look off. Can you fix them ASAP?

**After** (tailored for a senior Korean reader, in English):

> Dear Director Kim,
>
> Thank you for sending the Q3 figures so quickly. While preparing our summary, we noticed that the totals for the Busan branch differ slightly from our records (attached). Could your team kindly double-check them by Thursday? We would be happy to review them together if helpful.
>
> Best regards,

**Why:** The original addresses a senior by given name, criticizes directly and uses informal urgency. The revision shows respect for rank, frames the issue as a shared check and keeps a clear deadline, which matters in fast-paced Korean business culture.
