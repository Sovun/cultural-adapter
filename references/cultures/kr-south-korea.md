# South Korea

## In brief

Korean professional communication is shaped by strict hierarchy (age, rank, company), face (chemyeon) and a fast, competitive work culture ("ppalli-ppalli", hurry-hurry). The language encodes status through speech levels and honorifics, so tone must match the reader's position. Refusals are indirect, but once a relationship exists decisions and replies can be quick. Historical issues with Japan and territorial names are sensitive. Korea also has specific legal rules for ad messages, endorsements, personal data and job ads.

## Voice and tone

- Polite, respectful, efficient. Detailed data, clearly presented, builds credibility; claims are judged on track record more than promises.
- Choose the speech level deliberately and keep one level throughout: formal 합쇼체 (-습니다) for external business letters and legal terms, polite 해요체 (-요, -세요) for consumer, web and app copy. A switch between terms pages and marketing pages is normal. Casual 반말 toward customers or seniors is a mistake; youth campaigns use it only as a deliberate, locally reviewed choice.
- Drop "you": 당신 sounds odd or confrontational. Use 고객님 (customer), name + 님, or 귀사 (your company), and humble 저희 rather than 우리 toward clients. Rework "you have / your" phrasing; literal calques read as translationese.
- Honour the person, not the object: "커피 나오셨습니다" or "품절이십니다" is incorrect and makes service copy sound clumsy.
- Avoid pushy absolutes (반드시 "must", 절대로 "never") in marketing and UI; invite with -해 보세요 ("try..."). Ask for confirmation with -시겠습니까 ("계속하시겠습니까?"), not a literal "원하십니까?".
- Machine translation is easy to spot and hurts credibility. Transcreate taglines and have native speakers review; slang and memes age quickly.
- English for Korean readers: clear and simple, no idioms or sarcasm.

## Addressing people

- Name order: family name, then given name (김민수 = KIM Minsu), no middle name. A few surnames (Kim, Lee, Park, Choi, Jung) cover about half the population, so full name plus title avoids confusion. Many Koreans reverse the order or use an English name abroad; mirror their signature.
- Never address someone by family name alone or by an older person's given name alone. Use:
  - Title + 님: 부장님 (department head), 팀장님 (team lead), 대표님 (CEO), 교수님 (professor); in email "김민수 부장님" or "김 부장님". Ranks vary by company; check the signature.
  - -님 alone with names and customers (김민수 님, 고객님). 담당자님 ("person in charge") when the title is unknown.
  - -씨 (ssi) for peers or untitled colleagues; too low for seniors, clients or older people.
- In customer copy avoid family terms (어머님) and 아가씨 as address; many readers dislike them.
- When writing to a senior about another senior, keep honorifics for both.
- Email opening: "안녕하세요, 김민수 부장님." (or 안녕하십니까), then a self-introduction ("ABC 회사 마케팅팀 이지은입니다."), even if they know you.
- Closing: a warm wish (좋은 하루 보내세요), "감사합니다.", then name + 드림 (standard) or 올림 (more deferential, toward seniors), and a full signature with company, team, title and contacts. Avoid "수고하세요" toward seniors and clients.
- In English: "Dear Mr Kim" or "Dear Director Kim" until invited otherwise.

## Structuring a message

- Subject line: company and purpose, e.g. "[회사명] 1분기 기획서 전달드립니다" (sending the Q1 plan). Recipients triage by subject.
- Greeting with name and title, self-introduction, then purpose with set phrases (관련하여 메일 드립니다, 요청드립니다, 확인 부탁드립니다).
- Announce attachments in the body (첨부파일 확인 부탁드립니다). Proposals go to the most senior person with detailed data.
- Confirm agreements in writing after meetings and calls; a thank-you within a day is appreciated.
- KakaoTalk is used for quick business updates once rapport exists; read receipts create pressure to reply fast. Tone stays polite and emoticons sparing; do not add people to group chats without asking.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refusals are softened: "검토해 보겠습니다" (we will review it), "어려울 것 같습니다" (it seems difficult). Silence or no objection is not agreement.
- Requests upward are framed softly: "~해 주시면 감사하겠습니다" (I would be grateful if...). Bare imperatives toward seniors feel presumptuous.
- Do not criticize openly or show anger; it costs face and can end the relationship. Criticism goes one-to-one, never with others copied or in a group chat.
- Do not joke with or contradict senior people in front of others.
- Counterparts dislike being seen to lose; frame proposals as win-win.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with track record, data, rankings, awards, references and third-party introductions; plus speed, convenience and design quality.
- **Fair Labeling and Advertising Act** (enforced by KFTC): no false or exaggerated claims; "No. 1" and comparative claims need objective substantiation.
- KFTC endorsement guideline: any review or post with an economic tie (free product, fee, purchase-link commission) must disclose it clearly. Since December 2024 text posts must put the disclosure in the title or at the very start; vague wording such as "may receive a small commission" does not count. Hidden paid posts (뒷광고) are fined.
- **Network Act** (Art. 50) for ad SMS, email, push and messenger: prior opt-in, with the consent request plainly labelled as advertising; "(광고)" at the start of the message, sender name and contact, and a free opt-out (often an 080 number, "무료수신거부"). Sending between 21:00 and 08:00 needs separate night-time consent. A narrow exception for recent customers buying similar goods exists; check current rules and KISA guidance before relying on it.
- **PIPA**: consent items shown separately (필수 required, 선택 optional); marketing consent flagged as its own optional item; children under 14 need guardian consent. Regulator: PIPC.
- Local platforms (Naver search and reviews, KakaoTalk channels, Coupang) and mobile-first design matter; K-culture aesthetics raise visual expectations.
- CVs: Korean employers usually expect 이력서 (tabular résumé) plus 자기소개서 (self-introduction essay). Education, test scores and certificates ("spec") are prominent. A formal photo is still common in private firms (about two-thirds of entry-level postings in 2024) but declining; public bodies use blind hiring and multinationals accept Western CVs.
- Job ads: the **Fair Hiring Procedure Act** (firms of 30+) bans asking for appearance, height, weight, birthplace or family domicile, marital status, assets, or family members' education, occupation and assets; an ID photo is allowed. The **Age Discrimination in Employment Act** bans age limits or birth-year ranges without a job-based reason. Use gender-neutral job titles (의사, not 여의사). Regulator: MOEL.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor, wordplay and cute characters work in consumer and social media content; recreate wordplay in Korean rather than translating it.
- Avoid humor with seniors and in formal B2B contexts.
- ^^, ㅎㅎ and ㅋㅋ are common in personal chat but out of place in formal email. From a Korean contact, ^^ or ㅎㅎ often softens a message rather than meaning "funny"; laughter can signal discomfort.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Japan**: colonial rule (1910 to 1945), "comfort women" and forced labor are deeply sensitive. Many Koreans see the Rising Sun flag as a symbol of imperial aggression; never use it or similar imagery.
- **Dokdo** and **East Sea**: Korean readers expect "독도 / Dokdo" (Korea administers the islets and regards them as Korean territory) and "동해 / East Sea". "Takeshima", "Liancourt Rocks" or "Sea of Japan" alone will offend; maps omitting Dokdo have triggered boycott calls and brand apologies. In neutral international texts "East Sea (Sea of Japan)" is a common compromise. Avoid decorative maps in Korea-facing material unless checked.
- **North Korea**: avoid unprompted discussion, praise or jokes; family separation is a real trauma. Content praising the North can fall under the National Security Act. Avoid casual DMZ references in examples.
- Avoid comparing Korea with Japan or China or conflating their cultures; heritage disputes with China (kimchi, hanbok) are sensitive.
- Domestic politics are polarized; foreigners commenting are not welcome.
- Gender issues are polarized among younger people; certain hand gestures in ads (a pinching gesture) caused major backlash as perceived anti-male symbols. Have local reviewers check imagery.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- **4** (사, sa, sounds like death) is unlucky; avoid sets of four in gifts and packs. Lifts often label the 4th floor "F".
- Writing a living person's name in **red** suggests death; avoid red name fields in designs.
- Show giving and receiving with both hands or the right hand supported; the left hand alone looks disrespectful. Avoid index-finger pointing and the fist with thumb between fingers (obscene).
- Taegeukgi (national flag) must be shown correctly; errors cause complaints.
- Buddhist manji (卍) on maps marks temples, not Nazism.

## Variation within the culture

- Seoul is fast, trend-driven and international; Busan and Gyeongsang regions are known for blunter speech.
- Chaebol (Samsung, Hyundai, LG, SK, Lotte), finance and government keep rank titles and formality. Some tech firms flatten titles, usually to name + 님; English names are rarer and some firms have reverted.
- Younger Koreans (MZ generation) push back on hoesik (after-work drinking) and rigid hierarchy, and mind casual address less; office and professional workers are more sensitive to it.
- Strong Christian and Buddhist communities; religion is generally personal.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (casual English to a senior Korean partner):

> Hey Minsu, the numbers you sent look off. Can you fix them ASAP?

**After** (tailored for a senior Korean reader, in English):

> Dear Director Kim,
>
> Thank you for sending the Q3 figures so quickly. While preparing our summary, we noticed that the totals for the Busan branch differ slightly from our records (attached). Could your team kindly double-check them by Thursday? We would be happy to review them together if helpful.
>
> Best regards,

**Why:** The original addresses a senior by given name, criticizes directly and uses informal urgency. The revision shows respect for rank, frames the issue as a shared check and keeps a clear deadline, which matters in fast-paced Korean business culture.
