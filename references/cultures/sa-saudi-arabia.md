# Saudi Arabia

## In brief

Saudi readers expect respectful, relationship-oriented and religiously aware communication. Islam shapes public life, law and everyday language; honor and family reputation are central. Criticism is private and indirect, and warmth sits in greetings and blessings. The country is changing fast under Vision 2030 (entertainment, tourism, women at work), so assuming ultraconservatism is as wrong as ignoring conservative norms. Published text sits inside detailed rules: media licensing and content controls, online insult and public-order offences, Arabic for prices and contracts, and strict job-ad law. Do not reuse UAE copy unchanged: Saudi Arabia works Sunday to Thursday, runs on UTC+3 and bans alcohol for the public.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous, warm, respectful and calm. Visible anger, sarcasm or pressure damages the relationship. Religious expressions (Inshallah, Mashallah, Alhamdulillah) are everyday language at all levels of devotion; blessings often replace a plain "thank you".
- Modern Standard Arabic for formal and official text; Saudi dialect for social and consumer campaigns. Najdi (Riyadh) and Hijazi (Jeddah) audiences may each want their own version, and Egyptian or Levantine words can mislead (laban is yogurt drink here, not milk).
- Arabic must be native-written or transcreated. In UI text use the plain imperative ("افتح التطبيق", not "قم بفتح"), avoid "الخاص بك" on every noun, and prefer gender-inclusive forms. Modern admin writing cuts ornamental introductions after the formula.
- English is widely used in business, but many readers use it as a second language: short sentences, no idioms. Vulgar language in published content is a media violation.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name, father's, grandfather's, then family or tribal name (Ahmad Hussain Khalid Al-Shammari); "bin/ibn" (son of), "bint" (daughter of). Do not treat the father's name as a surname.
- Title + given name: "Dr Ahmad", "Eng. Faisal", "Ustadh Khalid". In English, "Dear Dr Ahmad" is the safe default; "Assalamu alaikum" is a welcome first line.
- Arabic honorifics follow rank: معالي for ministers, سعادة for deputy ministers and senior officials, المكرم or الأستاذ الفاضل for others. Royalty is "Your Royal Highness". Too high a title misfires as much as too low; check the form.
- Arabic letter: "سعادة الدكتور/ أحمد الشمري المحترم" or "السيد/ ... المحترم", then "السلام عليكم ورحمة الله وبركاته" and "تحية طيبة وبعد". Close with "وتفضلوا بقبول فائق الاحترام والتقدير" (formal) or "مع خالص التحية" (everyday).
- Most senior person first in address lines and cc. Do not ask men about their wives or daughters; wish "the family" well. Women may shake hands or place a hand on the heart; do not assume either.

## Structuring a message

1. Title, Islamic greeting and a courtesy line; link to the earlier letter or meeting ("بالإشارة إلى خطابكم رقم ...").
2. The purpose, then short paragraphs (3-5 sentences) with figures; full documents attached.
3. A request formula ("نرجو التكرم بالاطلاع والاعتماد", or "بانتظار توجيهاتكم" to a senior) and good wishes.

- Formal letters carry a specific subject (الموضوع:), a date and a reference number; letters without them cannot be tracked. Bare "urgent" labels read badly.
- Make proposals self-contained: they travel up several approval layers. Arabic versions of decks and cards are appreciated.
- Face-to-face meetings for important matters; email confirms. No artificial deadlines or "last chance" pressure.
- Schedule around prayer times, Ramadan and the Sunday to Thursday week; avoid Friday.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Never criticize or correct someone in front of others, including reply-all or wide cc; it attacks their honor.
- Pair a critical point with praise and frame it as a suggestion or option. Do not phrase requests so the reader must refuse outright.
- Decode "we will see", "it may be difficult" or "let us study it" as possible refusals; a sudden drop in replies can mean offence.
- Warning or disciplinary letters stay impersonal: the fact, its date, the rule, a response deadline, no threats.
- If offence is caused, acknowledge it; an apology from someone senior on your side can restore the relationship.
- Online defamation and invading privacy with camera devices are offences under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law. Keep complaints and reviews about named people or firms factual and private.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Strong themes: family, hospitality, national pride and "made in Saudi Arabia", Vision 2030 achievements, Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, National Day (23 September), Founding Day (22 February). Campaigns must feel authentically Saudi, not global ads with local faces.
- The General Authority for Media Regulation (GAMR, formerly GCAM) oversees media. Anyone advertising a product, service or event on social media needs a Mawthooq licence, a contract and a clear ad label, paid or not. Check current fees and penalties.
- 2025 content controls ban boasting about wealth, cars, tribe or region, showing children or domestic workers in everyday content, mockery and name-calling, family disputes as content, and revealing or tight clothing.
- Prices, product data and offers must be in Arabic; "starting from X" without a top price is banned; sales need a discount licence with before and after prices.
- Marketing with personal data needs consent and an easy opt-out under the Personal Data Protection Law (SDAIA). Amendments drafted in October 2026 are not law yet; check current status.
- Avoid alcohol, pork, gambling, romance, public affection, same-sex themes and immodest dress (cover shoulders, upper arms and knees). Never use God's name or Quranic verses decoratively.
- Ramadan: family, generosity, reflection; no daytime food or drink imagery in public contexts.
- Job ads (MHRSD rules, 2025, at last check): employer, duties, qualifications, hours, salary, benefits and deadline; posted only on approved platforms; no gender, age, disability or marital-status filters; gender-inclusive Arabic titles ("معلم ومعلمة"). Nitaqat quotas mean many roles target Saudi nationals through platforms, not ad wording.
- CVs: expats use English, Western layouts, with nationality and iqama (residence) status near the top; nationals often submit Arabic. A photo is common for smaller employers; large ones screen with ATS. Show credentials and field-specific results early; leave out Israel references.

## Humor and emphasis

- Self-deprecating humour is safe. Do not tease seniors or strangers, and never joke about family (especially women), religion, the government, the country, tribe, sect or sexuality; insulting religious values is a crime.
- Emphasis through repetition and eloquent phrasing is natural in Arabic; trim it for Western readers.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **State and religion:** no criticism of the King, Crown Prince, royal family, government or Islam, and no content giving a negative picture of national values. Content harming public order, religious values or public morals is an offence under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law.
- **Politics:** the media regulator acts against sectarian content and content insulting Arab, Islamic or friendly countries. Stay out of Israel, Iran and regional rivalries.
- **Religion:** public practice of other religions and proselytizing are illegal. Avoid religious comparison or debate.
- **Morals:** same-sex relations are illegal; no LGBT content for Saudi audiences. Gambling promotion and pornography are criminal.
- **Privacy:** photos or video of identifiable people, women above all, need consent.
- **Women:** about a third of Saudi women are in the labour force (36% in early 2025) and many are managers and clients. Do not presume oppression from dress or an all-male audience.
- Do not stereotype Saudis as uniformly wealthy or closed. Use "Arabian Gulf", not "Persian Gulf". Legal items are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The flag carries the Shahada: never at half-mast, altered, used as a trademark or in commercial advertising, or printed on disposable items.
- Green is linked to Islam and the flag; use it respectfully.
- Show eating, giving and receiving with the right hand; avoid shoe soles, pointing and the "OK" sign (less offensive than before). Hand on heart is a respectful greeting.
- Compliments on a person, child or home are followed by "Mashallah". Arabic coffee, dates and oud are positive imagery.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | Gregorian is the official basis for periods and pay since 2022; Hijri still appears on government and religious documents. d/M/y: ١٩/١٠/٢٠٢٦ or 19/10/2026; months يناير to ديسمبر. Mark the calendar (هـ Hijri, م Gregorian). Older software defaults ar-SA to Hijri; check output. |
| Time | 12-hour with ص (AM) and م (PM): ٢:٣٠ م / 2:30 PM. UTC+3, no daylight saving (UAE is UTC+4). |
| Numbers | Locale default ١٬٢٣٤٬٥٦٧٫٨٩; Western 1,234,567.89 is equally common. Use Western digits for digital, UI, price and bilingual content; Arabic-Indic suits formal printed Arabic. Match the client's material and never mix both. |
| Currency | New riyal sign (2025, U+20C1): the central bank places it left of the number with a space; locale data and many systems still print ١٬٢٣٤٫٥٠ ر.س. after it. English: SAR 1,234.50. Check font support; do not use the old ﷼ sign. Non-breaking space between amount and unit. |
| Week starts | Sunday. Work week Sunday to Thursday, weekend Friday and Saturday (like Qatar, unlike the UAE). |
| Phone | +966 11 123 4567 (Riyadh); mobiles start with 5. |

## Variation within the culture

- Riyadh and the Najd are more conservative; Jeddah and the Hejaz more cosmopolitan; the Eastern Province has a large Shia community. Treat KSA as several markets.
- Younger Saudis, many educated abroad, are digital and mobile-first and expect deep localisation; older generations are more traditional.
- About 40% of residents are expatriates (South Asian, Arab, Filipino, Western); expat-facing English can be more international, and mass campaigns may need several languages.
- Government letters keep full formulas; giga-projects and multinationals move faster; family businesses centralise decisions.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No criticism of the royal family, government, Islam or national values; no political or regional statements.
- [ ] No alcohol, pork, gambling, romance, immodest dress, LGBT themes, boasting, or children and domestic workers in everyday content.
- [ ] No decorative use of God's name, Quranic verses or the flag.
- [ ] Criticism private, indirect and balanced with praise; no public correction or pressure tactics.
- [ ] Titles + given names and rank-correct Arabic honorifics; greeting, reference and closing formulas present.
- [ ] Arabic is native-written (MSA or fitting dialect) and RTL-checked; prices and offers appear in Arabic.
- [ ] Influencer content is Mawthooq-licensed and labelled; no "starting from" prices; discounts licensed.
- [ ] Marketing to personal data has consent and opt-out; identifiable people gave consent.
- [ ] Job ads list salary and deadline, use approved platforms and have no gender, age or marital filters.
- [ ] Dates name the calendar; one digit system; riyal sign placement and font checked.
- [ ] Timing respects prayer times, Ramadan hours, moving Eids and the Sunday to Thursday week.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (Western marketing post for a Saudi audience):

> Celebrate Friday night with our new cocktail menu! Grab your date and party till dawn.

**After** (tailored for Saudi readers, in English):

> Weekend gatherings are better together. Discover our new selection of signature mocktails and Arabic coffee blends, perfect for sharing with family and friends.

**Why:** Alcohol is banned for the public, and dating and party framing clash with media content rules. Family hospitality, shared gatherings and non-alcoholic signature drinks fit Saudi values and law.
