# Malaysia

## In brief

Malaysian readers expect courteous, calm text that protects face: refusals and criticism are softened, pressure backfires, and a friendly opening line comes before the request. Rank matters a great deal, and getting a name or an honorific wrong (a Malay father's name used as a surname, "Dato'" written as "Datuk") is the most visible mistake a foreign writer makes. The audience is multi-ethnic (Malay and other Bumiputera, Chinese, Indian, and the indigenous peoples of Sabah and Sarawak), so names, festivals, food and imagery must not assume one group. Private-sector business runs largely in English with British spelling; government, GLCs and broad consumer audiences need Bahasa Malaysia, which is not Indonesian. Race, religion and royalty ("3R") are legal red lines, and halal claims are tightly controlled.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous, soft and steady. Politeness ("budi bahasa") is read as character, so a curt or aggressive line damages trust more than in Western text.
- English business text uses British spelling (colour, organise) and plain sentences. Manglish particles ("lah", "meh") belong to chat among locals; outsiders using them in copy sound mocking.
- Malay copy must be Malaysian Malay, never reused Indonesian: vocabulary (pejabat, not kantor; tiket, not karcis; jenama, not merk), spelling (kualiti, beza) and false friends ("butuh" is crude in Malaysia) give it away at once.
- In Malay, "anda" (you) is lowercase mid-sentence; a capitalised "Anda" looks Indonesian. "Saya" is the safe "I". "Kita" includes the reader, "kami" excludes them: a company speaking to customers says "kami".
- Formal Malay uses full words; chat shorthand ("utk", "tak") and mixed Malay-English read casual. Consumer and app copy can be friendlier and plainer than official letters.

## Addressing people

- **Malay names:** no surname. Given name + "bin" (son of) or "binti" (daughter of) + father's name. Razak bin Osman is Encik Razak or Mr Razak, never Mr Osman. Men named Muhammad or Abdul plus another name usually go by the second name; mirror their signature.
- **Chinese names:** family name first (Tan Mei Ling is Ms Tan); many add an English given name (Jason Tan).
- **Indian names:** given name + "a/l" (anak lelaki) or "a/p" (anak perempuan), or s/o, d/o, + father's name; address by the given name. Singh and Kaur are Sikh religious names, not surnames.
- **East Malaysia:** many Iban names follow the same pattern with "anak" (child of) before the father's name. Do not treat the last word as a surname. Married women generally keep their own names; never assume "Mrs + husband's name". Name fields should not require a surname.
- **Honorifics:** Tun > Tan Sri > Datuk Seri / Dato' Sri > Datuk / Dato'. Datuk and Tan Sri are federal; Dato' is from a state Ruler. Copy the person's exact spelling. Wives: Toh Puan (Tun), Puan Sri (Tan Sri), Datin (Datuk/Dato'). Haji / Hajjah mark the pilgrimage; Tunku, Tengku, Raja, Syed and Sharifah are part of the name.
- **Courtesy styles in formal letters:** YBhg. (Yang Berbahagia) before Tan Sri, Datuk, Dato'; YABhg. for Tun; YB for ministers and elected members; YAB for the Prime Minister and chief ministers. Confirm with the person's office for anything formal.
- English: "Dear Datuk Lee", "Dear Puan Aisyah", "Dear Mr Karthik". Malay letters: "Tuan/Puan," as salutation ("Tuan" alone to a government office). Sign-offs: "Best regards", "Thank you"; Malay "Sekian, terima kasih." then "Yang benar," or the name.
- Inclusive greetings: "Salam sejahtera" (peaceful greetings, for all faiths); "Assalamualaikum" only toward Muslim readers who use it.

## Structuring a message

1. Subject naming one topic; keep the thread subject when replying.
2. Salutation with the right title, senior people first, and one warm line ("Hope you are well", "Terima kasih atas pertemuan semalam").
3. A sentence of context, then the request, phrased as a question.
4. Details in short paragraphs or bullets; formal Malay letters number the middle paragraphs and stay "ringkas, tepat dan padat" (brief, precise, dense).
5. Next step with a date, an offer to talk, and a courteous close.

- After calls or meetings, send a short neutral recap of what was agreed, who does what, and by when; verbal agreements are not treated as final.
- Do not ask for an instant decision. Slow replies usually mean internal or senior approval, not lack of interest; asking about the approval steps is fine. WhatsApp is common for business follow-up once contact exists; keep the same courtesy there.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with appreciation, a reason and an alternative: "Thank you for the proposal. The timeline is challenging for us; could we look at a phased start?"
- A flat rejection of someone's proposal is easily taken personally, so recognise the work behind it first.
- Criticism is private. Never correct someone in a group thread or copy their boss to apply pressure. Describe the issue, not who caused it ("The figures in section 3 need another look").
- Check agreement with open questions ("How does your team see this timeline?") rather than "Do you agree?". Juniors rarely contradict seniors in writing; invite concerns privately.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with reliability, long-term partnership, references and value, not urgency or hard closes.
- Advertising code: claims and superlatives must be substantiable; comparisons fair and like for like; no disparaging competitors; no criticism of any government; "guaranteed" only with a real guarantee; testimonials genuine and current; living people need consent.
- Ads are expected to reflect the multiracial society and must not typecast races or sexes. Religion may not be used to sell: no clerics, scripture or religious personalities endorsing ordinary products. Influencer posts need an upfront "Ad" or "Sponsored" label in the same language, inside the video; "#sp" or "#collab" is not enough.
- **Halal:** only JAKIM and the state Islamic authorities certify. Do not use home-made halal badges or phrases like "Muslim-friendly", "Muslim choice" or "pork-free" without certification.
- Festive campaigns (Hari Raya, Chinese New Year, Deepavali, Christmas, Gawai, Kaamatan) are a major genre built on warmth and inter-ethnic harmony. Check details with the community; misused colours, costumes or a lecturing tone cause backlash.
- CV: 1-2 pages, reverse chronological, quantified results in a modest voice. Photos are common locally and optional for MNCs; leave out religion, marital status and expected salary unless asked.
- Job ads: state a job reason for any language requirement ("Mandarin, to handle China accounts"); unexplained language, race, age or gender filters are a public controversy. Since 2023 the labour authorities can rule on employment discrimination complaints.

## Humor and emphasis

- Light humour after rapport; never at someone's expense.
- No jokes on race, religion or royalty, even mild ones. Emphasise with bold or structure, not capitals or "!!". A laughing reply to a problem is often unease, not indifference.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **3R:** race, religion and royalty. The communications regulator acts on content insulting the King or Islam, and sedition law covers contempt for the Rulers, stirring hostility between races, and questioning citizenship, Malay as the national language, the special position of Malays and the natives of Sabah and Sarawak, or the Rulers' sovereignty. Do not question these, even jokingly.
- Ads must not offend religious, racial or political sensitivities or make casual use of religious names and concepts. The national flag, coat of arms and anthem are controlled in commercial use. Kuala Lumpur signboards must give Malay prominence; other councils have similar rules.
- **Personal data:** privacy notices are expected in Malay and English; 2024 amendments raised fines and added breach notification and data protection officers. Treat race, religion and health data as sensitive.
- Pork, alcohol and gambling imagery are out for Muslim audiences; leather can offend Hindus. Gifts stay modest.
- Avoid disputed place names and border issues. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Many Chinese readers avoid 4 and like 8; white suggests mourning, so festive packets and greetings use red or gold. Yellow is associated with royalty.
- Show giving and receiving with the right hand or both hands; no pointing fingers, shoe soles or touching heads.
- Keep mainstream imagery modest; avoid opposite-gender touching. Some Muslim women do not shake hands with men. Casting should show several communities. In Malay, write "orang kurang upaya (OKU)", not "cacat".

## Variation within the culture

- **Malays** (mostly Muslim) bring patronymic names, halal and prayer times, Ramadan and Hari Raya. "Selamat Hari Raya Aidilfitri" is the festive greeting. Malay is expected in government and GLC correspondence.
- **Chinese Malaysians** bring surname-first names, lunar festivals, number and colour symbolism; Mandarin, Cantonese and Hokkien are used in family firms and community media. "Gong Xi Fa Cai" for Chinese New Year.
- **Indian Malaysians** (mainly Tamil) may use patronymic names; many Hindus avoid beef. "Selamat Hari Deepavali" or "Happy Deepavali". Some readers are more openly expressive.
- **Sabah and Sarawak:** many indigenous peoples (Iban, Kadazan-Dusun, Bidayuh and others), many of them Christian; they are Bumiputera but not Malay. Gawai (Sarawak) and Kaamatan (Sabah) are major holidays. Do not present Peninsular Malay culture as all of Malaysia.
- **Language choice:** English reaches urban professionals; broad consumer campaigns need Malay, and often Chinese and Tamil versions. Do not assume ethnicity from a name and pick the language for the reader.
- **Sector and age:** government, GLCs and family firms are formal and rank-conscious; MNCs and start-ups are flatter and faster. Younger readers are more direct online but still use titles with seniors.
- **Calendar:** state weekends, state Rulers' birthdays and many moving religious holidays shift deadlines.

- [ ] Malay copy is Malaysian Malay: no Indonesian words, months, "Anda" capitalisation or 24-hour dot times.
- [ ] One courteous opening line and a line of context precede the request; the request is a clear question.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (direct, informal Western style):

> Hi Osman, we still haven't got the signed contract. We need it by tomorrow or the launch slips. Please sort it out ASAP!!

**After** (for Encik Hakim bin Osman, a manager reporting to Dato' Faridah):

> Dear Encik Hakim,
>
> Thank you for the productive meeting with Dato' Faridah last Tuesday. We are excited to work with your team.
>
> As discussed, our launch is planned for 3 November. To keep that date, we would need the signed contract by Friday, 24 October. Would that be possible within your approval process? If any point in the draft still needs Dato' Faridah's review, we would be glad to go through it with you by phone.
>
> Best regards,
> Daniel Wong

**Why:** The writer no longer calls Hakim "Osman" (a father's name) and gives the senior person her correct title. The ultimatum becomes a dated request linked to a reason, the approval process is respected, and an offer to talk replaces "ASAP!!". The context line before the ask matches what Malaysian readers expect.
