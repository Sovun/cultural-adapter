# Argentina

## In brief

Argentine readers expect warmth, a personal opener and Spanish that sounds local. The local informal pronoun is "vos", not "tú": the national government writes its own web pages in vos, and so do shops and apps, so tú-based copy from Spain, Mexico or "neutral" Spanish reads imported. First business contact still opens with "Estimado/a" and, with senior people, "usted" and a title. Opinions are voiced openly, but criticism stays private and refusals come cushioned. Prices, currency and the Malvinas are sensitive: mark pesos versus dollars, date every quote, and use "Islas Malvinas" in Spanish.

## Voice and tone

- Warm, personal and clear. Short sentences and everyday verbs: the government's own standard asks for active voice, one idea per paragraph and about 15-20 words per sentence.
- Write Rioplatense Spanish. Voseo forms: "vos tenés", "podés", "querés"; imperatives with a stressed final vowel: "completá", "elegí", "consultá", "escribinos", "registrate". Never "tú tienes" or "completa" in Argentine copy.
- Keep one form per text. Mixing vos, tú and usted is the most common localisation error; usted may still appear in formal terms and conditions.
- Companies describe themselves in the first person plural ("Somos un equipo de...") rather than in the third person.
- Lunfardo and "che" signal Buenos Aires informality. A little suits casual consumer copy; heavy porteño slang from a foreign brand sounds forced and may not land in the provinces.
- Some everyday words from Spain or Mexico read as crude or odd ("coger" is vulgar here); have a local speaker check.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s) and usually one surname (the father's); some people use two. Use the surname exactly as given. "Sr./Sra. + surname" is the neutral courtesy form.
- Titles in business: "Lic." (university graduate), "Dr./Dra." (lawyers, physicians), "Ing.", "Cdor./Cdora." (accountant). Use them where the person signs with them.
- Salutations: "Estimado Lic. Pérez:" or "Estimada Carolina:" (first name after "Estimado/a" is common even in formal mail); "Hola, Carolina:" once there is contact. A colon is standard; a comma is common in practice, so do not correct a native writer.
- Usted for older, senior or unknown readers and officials; vos once there is trust or when the reader uses it. Younger people use vos even with strangers.
- Sign-offs: "Atentamente" (most formal), "Saludos cordiales" (standard), "Saludos" (everyday), "Un abrazo" or "Abrazo" (established contacts). "Quedo a disposición" (I remain available) before the sign-off.
- In Spanish say "estadounidense", not "americano": "América" is the whole continent.

## Structuring a message

1. Subject naming the topic.
2. Salutation and a warm line ("Espero que estés muy bien", "Gracias por tu tiempo el martes"). Mention the referring contact or institution in a first email.
3. Purpose, then reasons in short paragraphs.
4. A dated next step and an offer to talk by phone or video.
5. "Quedo a disposición" and the sign-off.

- Written messages feel less personal than a call; settle sensitive points by phone, then confirm by email. Recap emails after meetings are welcome, not distrustful.
- Expect several rounds before a decision. Do not push for an answer "today"; slow replies often reflect approval layers, not disinterest. In incoming Argentine mail the request may come near the end.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Lively debate and strong opinions are normal and not hostile. Public contradiction, blame in group threads and widened CC lists damage trust.
- Refuse with a reason and an alternative: "Por ahora no podemos avanzar con esto, pero podríamos..." Frame problems around the issue, not the person.
- Directness grows with the relationship; a trusted colleague can be very frank, a new or senior contact gets a diplomatic tone.
- Decode hedges: "lo vemos" (we'll see) and "haremos lo posible" (we'll do our best) are not commitments. Ask for a date.
- Customer letters (refusals, debt collection) must stay respectful by law: no shaming or intimidation, and no collection letter dressed up as a court claim.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade through credibility: preparation, references, local presence, after-sales support. Soft selling works better than pressure; everything is negotiable until signed, so leave room.
- Argentine ads are known for wit, irony, emotion and nostalgia. Football, asado, mate and long gatherings with friends are shared everyday codes.
- Financing sells: "cuotas sin interés" (interest-free instalments) is a central device. Credit offers must show the effective annual rate (TEA) and total financial cost (CFT).
- Public offers must state validity and conditions ("Válido del 1/11 al 30/11", "Hasta agotar stock"). Ad claims form part of the contract. Prize draws tied to a purchase are banned, hence "sin obligación de compra".
- Comparative ads are lawful only when objective, verifiable and like-for-like, with no denigration. Consumer documents and labels must be in Spanish; translations may not be more prominent.
- Marketing consent must be express and separate (no pre-ticked boxes), and people can ask to be removed from lists at any time.
- CV: one and a half to two pages, reverse chronological, a tailored "perfil" at the top; a professional head-and-shoulders photo, age or date of birth and nationality are common; grades worth stating if above 7/10; language levels as básico, intermedio, avanzado.
- Job ads: discrimination by sex, age, nationality, religion, politics, union membership or physical characteristics is unlawful, and employers may not ask about political, religious, union or sexual-orientation views. Drop "hasta 35 años", "buena presencia" and sex filters. Pay is quoted monthly, gross ("bruto mensual"); the "aguinaldo" (an extra half-salary in June and December) is legally due.
- Inclusive forms ("todes", "x", "@") are politically charged. Since 2024 the national government has barred them in its own documents (this does not bind private firms; check current status). Collective nouns ("el equipo", "las personas usuarias") are the low-risk choice.

## Humor and emphasis

- Ironic, quick humour is widely enjoyed, but teasing and nicknames belong to close relationships; an outsider imitating them misfires.
- Football is common ground, but never take sides between clubs (Boca and River above all). Opening ¿ and ¡ are required; missing accents look careless.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Malvinas / Falklands:** the islands are administered by the United Kingdom and claimed by Argentina, which wrote the claim into its constitution; the 1982 war is commemorated every 2 April. Argentine readers expect "Islas Malvinas" in Spanish, and a map without them, or labelled as British, provokes strong reactions. The official national map is bicontinental (including the Antarctic sector). Make no jokes and no side comments.
- **Politics:** society is sharply polarised ("la grieta"), around Peronism and its opponents. Any political allusion, including colours or slogans, reads as taking sides.
- **Dictatorship 1976-1983:** human-rights crimes and the "desaparecidos" are a national wound; 24 March is a day of memory. Never trivialise.
- **Economy:** years of high inflation, devaluations and exchange controls touched every household. No jokes about crises or "cheap Argentina"; do not assume prices are stable or that readers think in dollars.
- Do not fold Argentina into a generic "LatAm" audience or compare it to neighbours in copy. Racist or religious hate propaganda is a crime. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Seasons are reversed: Christmas is in summer, the school year starts in March, winter holidays fall in July. Snowy Christmas visuals and "back to school in September" are wrong.
- Light blue and white are national colours; mate, asado and the gaucho are shared everyday icons.
- Cheek kisses between colleagues and hands on shoulders look natural in imagery of people. Origin claims follow labelling rules ("Industria argentina").

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | 17/10/2026 in business; CLDR short 17/10/26. Long: 17 de octubre de 2026 (lowercase month). |
| Time | Write business and schedule times in 24-hour form: 14:30 or 14:30 h ("hs" is common but nonstandard). CLDR's software default is 12-hour (2:30 p. m.), as used in casual speech. |
| Numbers | 1.234.567,89; four-digit numbers grouped (1.234). "Billion" = "mil millones" (a "billón" is 10^12). |
| Currency | $ 1.234,56 for pesos (symbol first, space). Dollars: US$ 1.200 or USD. Never leave "$" ambiguous. |
| Percent | 18,5% (no space). |
| Week starts | Monday (unlike Mexico and Brazil). |
| Phone | +54 11 2345-6789 (Buenos Aires landline); mobiles +54 9 11 2345-6789, locally 011 15-2345-6789. |
| Address | Av. Corrientes 1234, piso 5, depto. B / C1043AAZ CABA. Alphanumeric postcode (CPA) before the city; older four-digit codes still widely used. |
| Other | UTC-3, no daylight saving. VAT 21% general; consumer receipts show the VAT contained. IDs: DNI (person), CUIT/CUIL (tax and labour). Metric. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Buenos Aires and the interior:** Greater Buenos Aires concentrates business and is faster, more informal and more cosmopolitan. Provincial readers (Córdoba, the northwest, Patagonia) often expect more formality, use usted more and can resent porteño tone; neutral Rioplatense with vos travels best nationally.
- **Generations:** younger readers use vos with everyone; many older readers and some interior regions expect usted at first contact.
- **Sectors:** tech, startups and agencies are flat, first-name and quick; banking, law, government and family-owned industrial firms keep titles and usted longer. Multinationals keep tighter schedules.
- **Heritage:** large Italian and Spanish immigrant heritage shapes surnames and food culture; others identify with the gaucho and rural interior traditions; imagery choices signal which identity a brand speaks to.
- **Language:** many professionals speak good English, but contracts, labels and consumer texts must be in Spanish, and foreign documents for legal use need a sworn translation.
- **Calendar:** January-February is summer holiday season and business slows sharply; the business day starts and ends late, lunches 13:00-15:00, dinners from 21:00.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Informal and consumer copy uses voseo ("tenés", "completá", "escribinos"); no tú forms.
- [ ] One form (vos or usted) throughout; usted only for first contact with seniors, officials or formal legal text.
- [ ] First contact opens with "Estimado/a" and a correct title where the reader uses one; a warm line precedes the ask.
- [ ] Refusals give a reason and an alternative; no criticism in group threads.
- [ ] Agreements and dates are confirmed in writing with a follow-up step.
- [ ] No pressure for an immediate decision; a call is offered.
- [ ] Every price shows its currency ($ for pesos, US$ for dollars) and a validity date.
- [ ] Offers state validity and conditions; credit offers show TEA and CFT; no purchase-linked prize draws.
- [ ] Comparative claims are verifiable and do not denigrate; no unbacked superlatives.
- [ ] Spanish versions are present and at least as prominent as any other language.
- [ ] Marketing consent is opt-in and separate; an opt-out is offered.
- [ ] "Islas Malvinas" in Spanish; maps include the islands; no political, dictatorship or economic-crisis jokes.
- [ ] Southern Hemisphere seasons and the January-February slowdown are respected.
- [ ] Times in 24-hour form; numbers 1.234,56; "billion" rendered as "mil millones".
- [ ] Job ads have no age, sex or appearance filters; pay is quoted monthly gross.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (neutral Latin American Spanish, tú, generic):

> ¡Hola! Tu suscripción vence pronto. Renueva ahora por solo $12.99 al mes. Si tienes dudas, contáctanos.

**After** (for Argentine customers, in Spanish with gloss):

> Hola, Martín:
>
> Tu plan vence el 30 de noviembre. Si querés seguir usándolo, renovalo antes de esa fecha por US$ 12,99 por mes (precio válido hasta el 30/11/2026). Podés cancelarlo cuando quieras desde tu cuenta, igual que lo contrataste. ¿Tenés alguna duda? Escribinos y te ayudamos.
>
> Un saludo,
> El equipo de Nubo

*(Gloss: "Hi Martín, your plan expires on 30 November. If you want to keep using it, renew it before that date for US$12.99 a month (price valid until 30/11/2026). You can cancel any time from your account, the same way you signed up. Any questions? Write to us and we'll help. Regards, the Nubo team")*

**Why:** Tú forms become voseo ("querés", "renovalo", "podés", "escribinos"). The ambiguous "$" becomes US$ with a decimal comma and a validity date, and vague "pronto" becomes a date. The cancellation line reflects the rule that online subscriptions must be cancellable online. The tone stays warm without hard-sell pressure.
