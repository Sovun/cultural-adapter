# Pakistan

## In brief

Business writing in Pakistan is mostly in English, with British spelling and a courteous, formal register; Urdu is the national language and the main medium for mass-market and government-facing content. Readers expect respect for seniority, a greeting and a wellbeing line before business, and refusals or criticism that leave the other person's dignity intact. Religion shapes public life and law: blasphemy offences carry the heaviest penalties in the Penal Code, so religious references, Arabic script and satire need real care. Pakistan has its own language, script, politics and sensitivities; do not adapt Indian content by swapping a few words, and keep Kashmir wording and maps in line with what Pakistani readers expect.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous and respectful. Local writers use "Kindly...", "I would be grateful if...", "With due respect" and "Thanking you in anticipation"; these read as polite, not servile, so do not strip them from a local text meant for local readers.
- British spelling (colour, programme, organise). Pakistani English words such as "timings" (working hours), "prepone" (bring forward), "in-charge" (head of a unit) and "godown" (warehouse) are fine for local readers but should be replaced for international ones.
- Religious phrases are everyday language for many Muslim readers: InshaAllah (God willing), Alhamdulillah (praise be to God, often the answer to "how are you"), MashaAllah (said in admiration). Foreigners need not use them; never treat them ironically.
- Urdu is written right to left in Nastaliq script, which needs proper fonts and RTL layout; English documents stay LTR. Much consumer copy uses English or Roman Urdu (Urdu in Latin letters, e.g. "Shukriya" for thank you); pick one Roman Urdu spelling and keep it consistent.
- Modern Urdu product and marketing text should be plain and conversational; ornate classical phrasing belongs in formal letters and official notices.

## Addressing people

- First contact: title + surname or title + full name ("Dear Mr Siddiqui", "Dear Ms Farah Malik"). Keep every title the person uses: Dr, Engr., Prof. Dr., Barrister, and military ranks (retired officers often write "Col. (R)"). Dropping a title can read as a slight.
- "Sir" and "Madam" are the standard address for superiors and officials. "Respected Sir/Madam" opens traditional and government letters; it looks dated to Western readers but is normal deference here.
- Urdu honorifics in mixed writing: "[Name] Sahib" for a man (Ahmed Sahib), "Sahiba" for a woman (less common; "Madam" is safer), "ji" after a first name for warm respect (Sana ji), "Janab" (sir) and "Mohtarma" (madam) in official Urdu. "Bhai" (brother) and "Apa/Baji" (elder sister) are friendly, not for first contact. Ask women how they prefer to be addressed.
- Names: the last word is often not a family name. Tribal or lineage names (Khan, Syed, Shah), honorific elements (Haji, Chaudhry, Mian) and father's names all occur, and many married women keep their own name. Follow the person's own signature.
- "Assalam-o-Alaikum" (peace be upon you; reply "Wa Alaikum Assalam") is the common greeting between Muslims, also in email. It is optional for a foreign writer and not a default for Christian, Hindu or other minority readers. Never use "namaste" as a generic Pakistani greeting.
- Asking after "your family" is welcome; asking a man about his wife or daughters by name is not.
- Sign-offs: "Regards", "Best regards", "Warm regards". "Allah Hafiz" or "Khuda Hafiz" (God protect you) appear in Urdu and informal notes; both are fine, and the choice should not be commented on.

## Structuring a message

1. Salutation with title, then a greeting line ("I hope you are well").
2. Brief context or reference to the last contact, then the request.
3. The request phrased politely, with dates and owners stated.
4. A closing with good wishes and an offer to discuss by phone or meeting.

- Traditional official letters open with "It is respectfully submitted that..." and give background before the request; private-sector email is shorter but keeps the greeting.
- Do not expect a decision from the first message. Polite, repeated follow-up is normal and not pushy; a written recap of agreed actions is appreciated.
- Plan around Friday midday prayers (Jumu'ah), shortened hours in Ramadan and the Eid holidays; avoid deadlines in the days before Eid.
- Senior readers should be named first in a multi-recipient message; do not copy their superiors without reason.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with a reason and an alternative: "At this stage we are unable to..., but we could...". Avoid "No, this is not possible."
- Criticism goes in private, never in a group thread. Pair it with genuine appreciation and frame it around the process, not the person; a message that forces someone to admit fault in writing usually backfires.
- Soft signals: "I'll see what I can do", "it may be difficult" and silence from a senior can mean no. Ask open questions ("Which date suits you?") rather than strings of yes/no questions.
- Never accuse anyone of disloyalty or of being "anti-Islam" or "anti-Pakistan", even in a heated dispute. Criminal and civil defamation laws cover online posts, so public "naming and shaming" of a partner or official carries legal risk.
- When adapting Pakistani text for direct cultures, cut stacked courtesy formulas and turn "we will try our best" into a dated commitment.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Family benefit, trust, value, modesty and community belonging persuade; celebrity and cricket endorsements work well. Overt sexual appeals cause backlash.
- Deceptive marketing is banned by competition law. Regulator guidelines require independent proof before "No. 1", "best" or comparative claims, judge the overall impression, and treat hidden conditions, "T&Cs apply" without terms, and fine print as deceptive. Disclosures must be prominent and in the ad's language. Offers must state duration and limits. Health and skin-whitening claims need scientific support; paid endorsements must be disclosed.
- Broadcast rules bar using religious symbols, national symbols or the anthem to sell, ads for alcohol, tobacco and gambling, and urging children to buy. Indecent advertising is a criminal offence in any medium.
- Show women modestly and avoid physical intimacy between unrelated men and women; urban upmarket brands push further but draw complaints.
- CVs: in English, one page for graduates, two for experienced staff. Professional qualifications (ACCA, CA, CFA) go near the top. Traditional CVs may include a photo, father's name, national ID (CNIC), date of birth, religion and marital status; reviewers abroad should not treat these as errors, while CVs for multinationals usually leave them out.
- Job ads: anti-discrimination rules in the Constitution bind state employment, and public-sector ads state quotas, age limits and domicile. Private ads still often say "male only" or give age ranges; a foreign employer should write neutral ads and never tie a role to a religion. Larger employers mention a harassment-free workplace policy.

## Humor and emphasis

- Keep humour out of first-contact business text. Light warmth, compliments on hospitality and cricket talk are safe once there is rapport.
- No jokes, memes or wordplay touching religion, religious figures, the founding fathers (Jinnah, Iqbal), the armed forces or the judiciary. No jokes about ethnic or sectarian groups (Punjabi, Pashtun, Sindhi, Baloch, Muhajir; Sunni, Shia).
- Emphasis through polite intensifiers ("highly appreciated") is normal; capitals and "!!" are not.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Blasphemy:** the Penal Code punishes insulting any religion (s.295-A, up to 10 years), defiling the Quran (s.295-B, life imprisonment) and defiling the name of the Prophet Muhammad (s.295-C, death penalty), including by innuendo or images; s.298-A covers the Prophet's family and companions. Accusations alone have led to mob violence. Avoid any satirical, decorative or casual religious content.
- **Arabic script and the Quran:** never use Quranic verses or Arabic calligraphy as decoration, on products, clothing, footwear, floors or disposables. Many readers cannot read Arabic and may assume any Arabic text is religious.
- **Ahmadis:** the Constitution classes Ahmadis as non-Muslims and the Penal Code bars them from Islamic terms. Do not categorise anyone's faith; if needed, write "the Ahmadi community".
- **Kashmir:** Pakistani readers expect maps showing all of Jammu and Kashmir as disputed, and the names "Azad Jammu and Kashmir" and "Gilgit-Baltistan"; Indian-administered Kashmir is called "Indian Illegally Occupied Jammu and Kashmir" officially or "Indian-held Kashmir" in media. "Pakistan-occupied Kashmir" is Indian usage and offends. For mixed audiences, avoid maps or write "Pakistan-administered" and "Indian-administered Kashmir". Keep promotions away from 5 February and 5 August, both Kashmir observance days.
- **India, the military, courts, state:** do not raise India relations, terrorism or domestic politics unless the reader does. Online speech law (2016, amended 2025) criminalises spreading false information that may cause fear or unrest and allows blocking content against the state, armed forces, judiciary or "glory of Islam".
- Alcohol, pork, dating, LGBTQ themes and eating in public during Ramadan daylight are high-risk in content. Muharram, especially Ashura, is a mourning period for Shia readers: no festive promotions.
- Gender-rights slogans are contested; expect praise and backlash. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Green and white, the crescent and star, and images of Jinnah and Iqbal are treated with reverence; do not alter the flag. Independence Day (14 August) campaigns are popular when celebratory.
- The left hand is unclean: no images of giving or eating with it. Modest dress in visuals; a dupatta or headscarf suits traditional settings.
- Do not use Indian imagery, Indian celebrities, Hindi phrasing or Devanagari script as stand-ins for Pakistan; Urdu uses Perso-Arabic script.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | Day first. English: 18/10/2026, 18-Oct-2026, 18 October 2026. Urdu: 18 اکتوبر، 2026. Never month-first. Hijri dates appear for religious events. |
| Time | 12-hour: 2:30 PM. PKT (UTC+5), no daylight saving. |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89. Lakh (1,00,000) and crore (1,00,00,000) are common alongside million; give both for international readers. Official Urdu may use ۱۲۳ digits. |
| Currency | Rs1,500 or Rs 1,500 (rupee); PKR 1,500 in international documents. |
| Week starts | Sunday in calendars. Sunday is the universal day off; many offices, banks and MNCs also close Saturday, some firms work it. Friday is a working day with a long midday prayer break. |
| Phone | Mobile 0300-1234567, +92 300 1234567; Karachi landline (021) 3456 7890. |
| Address | House 12, Street 5, F-7/2, Islamabad 44000. Five-digit postcode after the city. |
| Script | Urdu: right to left, Nastaliq; full stop ۔ and comma ، . English: left to right, British spelling. |
| Other | Metric. National ID (CNIC) appears on official forms; do not request it in marketing forms. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Languages:** Urdu is the mother tongue of a minority. Punjabi, Sindhi (official use in Sindh), Pashto, Balochi, Saraiki and others are spoken; do not assume Urdu is everyone's first language. English and Roman Urdu reach urban middle and upper classes; Urdu script reaches wider and rural audiences.
- **Regions:** Karachi is commercial and fast-paced; Lahore and Islamabad mix government and corporate styles; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan are more conservative, with strong hospitality norms among Pashtuns.
- **Sectors:** banks, MNCs, telecoms and tech use first names sooner and shorter emails; government, family firms and older readers expect Sir/Madam and full formulas.
- **Religion:** a Sunni majority, a significant Shia minority, and Christian, Hindu, Sikh and Ahmadi communities with different sensitivities. Some prefer "Ramadan" and "Allah Hafiz", others "Ramzan" and "Khuda Hafiz".
- **Generations:** younger urban readers accept global brand themes and informal chat; conservative and older readers respond to modesty and tradition.
- **Diaspora:** large communities in the Gulf, UK and North America mix local and host norms.

- [ ] Refusals give a reason and an alternative; no criticism in group threads or with superiors copied.
- [ ] Dates, owners and next steps are explicit; "InshaAllah" or "we will try" is not taken as confirmation.
- [ ] British spelling; Pakistani English idioms replaced if the audience is international.
- [ ] No religious jokes, satire or imagery; no Quranic verses or Arabic calligraphy as decoration.
- [ ] No content on Ahmadi faith status, sectarian differences or ethnic stereotypes.

- [ ] No Indian imagery, Hindi phrasing or Devanagari presented as Pakistani.
- [ ] Superlatives and comparisons are backed by independent evidence; offers state conditions and duration; disclosures in the ad's language.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt project email from a Northern European manager):

> Hi Bilal, the test report you sent is incomplete. Resend it by Thursday with all results or we will escalate to your director.

**After** (for a senior engineer at a Lahore partner firm):

> Dear Engr. Bilal Sahib,
>
> I hope you are well, and thank you for sending the test report so promptly. While reviewing it, our team noticed that the results for the second and third batches are not yet included. Could your team kindly add them? Having the complete report by Thursday, 22 October would let us keep the client review on schedule. If anything is holding the results up, I would be glad to discuss it on a call this week.
>
> Best regards,

**Why:** The gap is still named precisely, but it is framed around the report, not the reader, and comes after a greeting and thanks. The title and "Sahib" show respect, the threat to go over his head is replaced by a reason tied to the client, and the exact date plus an offer to talk replace pressure with a clear, face-saving path.
