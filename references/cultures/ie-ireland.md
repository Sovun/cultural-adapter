# Ireland

## In brief

Irish readers expect friendly, plain and unpretentious writing: a warm line, the point, and a clear next step. First names come almost at once and stiff, corporate wording creates distance. Refusals and criticism are softened, so mild phrases often carry more weight than they seem to, and hype or self-praise is quietly discounted. Ireland is not part of the UK: it uses the euro, has its own laws and its own official language, Irish, and labelling Irish readers "British" is one of the worst mistakes a text can make.

## Voice and tone

- Friendly, plain, a little informal. Short sentences, active voice, "you" for the reader and "we" for the organisation. Plain English is the public-sector norm, and many adults find dense text hard to read, so consumer copy should be simple.
- Conditional, softened requests are standard: "Would you be able to...", "Could you just send...", "I'd appreciate it if...". A bare imperative to a stranger reads as rude.
- Understatement is the default. "Not bad" can be high praise, and "a bit of an issue" may be a serious problem.
- Use Irish/British spelling (organise, colour, programme, CV) and avoid US idioms that read as imported.
- Do not imitate Irish slang, banter or swearing to sound local; from an outsider it lands badly.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, surname. Irish names keep the fada (síneadh fada, the acute accent): Seán, Siobhán, Róisín, Ó Briain. Dropping it is a visible error, and public bodies are legally required to record names with fadas correctly. Keep prefixes as the person writes them (Ó, Ní, Mac, Nic, O', Mc); "Ní" and "Nic" mark women's surnames.
- Titles without full stops: Mr, Ms, Dr. "Ms" is the default for women. Mirror the reader's sign-off; most move to first names immediately.
- Salutations: "Dear Aoife," or "Hi Aoife," in normal email; "Dear Ms Byrne," for formal letters; "Dear Sir or Madam" only when no name can be found. "Hey" is too casual for a first message. "Hi all" or "Hi everyone" for groups ("lads" is casual speech).
- Sign-offs: "Kind regards" (standard), "Best wishes" or "Thanks" (familiar), "Yours sincerely" (formal letters).
- Irish-language forms appear in public-sector and bilingual mail: "A chara" (Dear Sir/Madam, literally "friend"), "Le meas" (Regards), "Is mise le meas" (Yours sincerely), "Go raibh maith agat" (thank you). With a name the vocative changes it ("A Sheáin, a chara" for Seán); if unsure, use English rather than guess.
- Never assume the national identity of someone from Northern Ireland; people there may identify as Irish, British or both.

## Structuring a message

1. Specific subject line.
2. Greeting and one warm line (weather, the weekend, "Hope you're well"). Skipping it can feel abrupt; a paragraph of small talk is not needed.
3. Purpose in the first or second sentence, one topic per short paragraph.
4. A concrete next step: a date, a choice of two times, a named owner. "Let's catch up sometime" is read as politeness, not a plan.
5. A friendly close ("Any questions, just let me know").

- Confirm agreements in writing after a call, since a spoken "grand" may only mean "I heard you". Answer quotes and orders promptly.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Soften a "no" with thanks, a short reason and an alternative: "Thanks for this. It doesn't suit us this quarter, but we'd be glad to look again in January."
- Criticism goes one to one, framed as a suggestion and kept specific. Never criticise on a wide CC list.
- Decode incoming signals: "we'll see", "maybe", "I'll come back to you" often mean no; "it should be fine" can be a quiet warning; "whatever you think yourself" can signal disagreement. If you need a commitment, ask for a specific date or action.
- Silence or no objection in a thread is not consent; doubts may surface later and in private.
- Hard-sell pressure and ultimatums backfire; fair give-and-take works.
- An offer may be declined once out of courtesy; make it easy to accept on a second mention.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Evidence, fair dealing and a likeable, self-aware voice beat superlatives. Dry humour and specific local references outperform imported global campaigns.
- Advertising standards: marketing must be legal, decent, honest and truthful. "Number one", "leading" or unqualified "best" need proof; comparisons must be fair and not denigrate rivals; "free" means nothing to pay beyond unavoidable costs; absolute green claims ("eco-friendly") need proof. Paid influencer posts open with "#Ad" (or Irish "#Fógra").
- A "was/now" price must use the lowest price charged in the previous 30 days. Consumer prices are in euro.
- Marketing email and SMS to individuals generally need prior consent (with a narrow exception for similar products to existing customers), and every message needs a working opt-out.
- Alcohol and gambling advertising are restricted by statute, with rules being phased in; check the current position before writing such campaigns.
- CV: at most two pages, reverse chronological, plain single-column layout, a short profile, achievement bullets with numbers, no "I". No photo, date of birth, marital status or nationality. Irish terms: Leaving Certificate, 2:1 or First, two referees. A tailored one-page cover letter is standard.
- Job ads must not indicate discrimination on the nine protected grounds: gender, civil status, family status, sexual orientation, religion, age, disability, race and membership of the Traveller community. Avoid "young", "energetic", "recent graduate", "native English speaker" and gendered titles ("salesman"). EU pay-transparency rules (salary ranges in ads) were not yet transposed in 2026; check current status.

## Humor and emphasis

- Irony, understatement and self-deprecation are everyday tools, and a light remark in an Irish reply can carry a serious message.
- Slagging (teasing as a sign of acceptance) is for people who know each other; do not copy it in writing to strangers.
- Emphasis through plain wording and bold, not capitals, stacked exclamation marks or "amazing".

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Ireland is not the UK.** Do not list Ireland under "UK", price in sterling, or call Irish people British. Avoid "British Isles"; write "Britain and Ireland" or "the UK and Ireland". The state's name is Ireland ("Republic of Ireland" is a description).
- **Northern Ireland and the Troubles:** identity and politics remain sensitive. Take no side, make no jokes, avoid green-versus-orange framing, and keep Troubles-linked drink names and puns out.
- **Travellers:** a protected group under equality law; "tinker" and similar terms are slurs. Advertising rules also protect dignity on all nine grounds and bar gender stereotyping and ridicule of religion.
- **Religion:** private; do not assume Catholic identity or use religious framing.
- **Irish language:** public bodies must place at least 20% of their advertising in Irish and use Irish-language media. Get any Irish text checked by a competent speaker.
- **Finance:** banks still face distrust after the 2008 crash; triumphant tone from financial brands can backfire.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Leprechauns, shamrock overload, pints and fiddles read as tourist clichés. Do not assume everyone drinks.
- St Patrick's Day (17 March) is a national holiday; the short form is "Paddy's Day", never "Patty's Day". Green beer is an American import.
- Green and orange set against each other can read as sectarian.

## Variation within the culture

- **Irish (Gaeilge):** first official language and widely studied, but English is the language of business; daily speakers are concentrated in Gaeltacht areas, mainly along the west coast. A bilingual touch reads as courtesy; badly translated Irish draws public criticism.
- **Public sector:** statutory language duties, bilingual branding and Irish salutations; very plain-English oriented.
- **Dublin multinationals and tech:** plainer, more direct, idiom-free English and US-influenced habits; traditional sectors (law, banking) keep more formality.
- **Regions:** rural and small-town readers often value personal connection and local reference even more; avoid regionally marked slang in national copy.
- **Northern Ireland** is a separate UK jurisdiction with sterling and UK law; do not apply this profile there unchanged.
- **Generations:** younger readers are more casual; religious greetings and formal titles are fading.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Ireland is never described as part of the UK or "British Isles"; prices are in euro.
- [ ] Fadas and name prefixes are spelled exactly (Seán, Ní Bhriain, Ó Súilleabháin).
- [ ] Irish/British spelling and DD/MM/YYYY dates; no US date order.
- [ ] One warm opening line, then the purpose within the first two sentences.
- [ ] Requests are softened questions, not bare imperatives.
- [ ] Refusals include thanks, a reason and an alternative; criticism is private and specific.
- [ ] The message ends with a dated, concrete next step.
- [ ] No superlatives or self-praise without numbers or proof; no hype words or "!!".
- [ ] No leprechaun, shamrock or drinking clichés; no Troubles references or jokes.

- [ ] Job ads avoid age-coded, gendered and "native speaker" wording and cover all nine grounds.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt US-style sales follow-up):

> Hey Sean! Our platform is the #1 solution on the market and your team will LOVE it. I need a decision by Friday, so let me know ASAP!!

**After** (tailored for an Irish contact):

> Hi Seán,
>
> Hope you had a good weekend. Thanks again for your time on Tuesday. As promised, I've attached a short summary: the two firms in Cork using it cut their month-end reporting from four days to two.
>
> Would a 20-minute call on Thursday morning or Friday afternoon suit to go through any questions? If the timing doesn't work for you, no bother at all.
>
> Kind regards,
> Anna

**Why:** The fada is restored, the hype and pressure are replaced by one modest, local result, and the deadline becomes an easy choice of two times with a graceful way out. The warm opener and "no bother" keep it friendly without imitating slang.
