# Norway

## In brief

Norwegian readers want short, plain, honest text that gets to the matter in the first lines. Everyone is "du" and first names are normal from the first email, including with managers and customers; titles and the old polite "De" sound dated. Hype, self-praise and pressure to decide fast lower trust, because decisions are made by broad agreement and credibility comes from keeping promises. Marketing law is strict and specific: claims must be documented, retouched bodies must be labelled, paid posts must say "reklame", and alcohol ads are banned. Norwegian has two written standards (Bokmål and Nynorsk), and Sámi languages have equal status in law.

## Voice and tone

- Plain, everyday words over bureaucratic ones: "bruke" not "benytte", "prøve" not "forsøke". Clear writing is treated as competence; talking round the point ("gå rundt grøten") is not.
- Helpful, polite and factual. Friendly filler without a real answer reads hollow, most of all in refusals, complaints and decisions.
- Translated English is easy to spot. Rewrite constructions like "gir deg muligheten til" ("gives you the opportunity to") as "med X kan du". Put possessives after the noun ("kontoen din", not "din konto") or drop them.
- Address the reader as "du" rather than impersonal "man". Use gender-neutral role nouns ("leder", "talsperson") and greet groups with "alle sammen" or "kolleger", not "damer og herrer".
- Plain directness is not rudeness. Do not answer a short Norwegian email with heavy cushioning; it can look evasive.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s), then surname. First names from the first message; full name at first mention in formal or public settings, where some readers find first-name-only address too familiar.
- "Hei Ola," is the neutral default. "Kjære Ola," is warmer and can suit a first email to a senior person, though some find it intimate. "God dag" is rare and stiff.
- "Du" (one person) and "dere" (several) are correct in every situation. "De/Dem/Deres" survives mainly for the royal family and ceremony and sounds outdated in business. Never capitalise "du" mid-sentence.
- Job titles are lowercase, also in signatures: "Kari Nordmann, daglig leder".
- Sign-offs: "Med vennlig hilsen" (standard), "Vennlig hilsen" (a little lighter), "Beste hilsen" (friendly), "Mvh" (internal; a few readers find it curt).
- In English, "Hi Ola," is fine. "Dear Mr Hansen" will not offend, but reads distant.

## Structuring a message

1. Subject line that names the matter: "Tilbud 2026-31: ny leveringsdato".
2. "Hei" + name, then at most one context line ("Takk for møtet i går", "Som avtalt sender jeg...").
3. The point, then short paragraphs or a list with only what the reader needs for the decision.
4. Action items with owner and date.
5. "Ta gjerne kontakt hvis du har spørsmål." and the sign-off.

- Headings in sentence case; Title Case looks translated. Semicolons are rare; split long sentences. Defined terms in contracts are not capitalised.
- Meetings get an agenda in advance and end on time. A reply, even a holding one, within about a working day is appreciated.
- Avoid evening and weekend email unless it is urgent; time off is protected.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- "Jeg er ikke enig" (I don't agree) is acceptable between colleagues if it stays on facts. "Jeg er bare delvis enig" is softer.
- A refusal thanks the sender, says clearly that the answer is no, and, where possible, says what you can do instead.
- Criticism is specific and aimed at improving the work. Norwegians often soften it with "kanskje" (perhaps), "kunne" (could) or "Hva synes du?" (what do you think?). Read these as real requests.
- "Ja" means yes and "nei" means no. Silence is not agreement; ask for an explicit answer.
- Decisions come from broad agreement. Pushing for a quick written decision, or deciding over people's heads, creates resistance. Propose, explain, invite comments, set a date.
- Own mistakes plainly, with a fix and a date. Never overpromise.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with facts, quality, reliability, practical benefit, sustainability and fair terms. Status, luxury and "beat the competition" appeals fall flat; equality and quality of life land better.
- Every factual claim in an ad (price, performance, "best", "fastest") must be documented when the ad runs. Misleading statements and omissions are banned, and ads must not conflict with gender equality or exploit bodies.
- Ads with bodies or skin retouched or filtered must carry the standard retouching label with the word "REKLAME". Paid social posts must start with "reklame" or "annonse" ("advertisement" in English); "sponset" or "i samarbeid med" alone is not enough.
- General green claims ("miljøvennlig", "grønn") face strict rules; green imagery counts as a claim too. Email and SMS marketing need prior consent. Buy-now appeals to children are banned. Alcohol advertising is banned in almost all forms.
- CV: up to two pages, clean layout, often a short key-qualifications summary; duties then results, factual tone, "we" for team work. Photo optional, rarer in the public sector and academia. A tailored application letter ("søknad") is expected.
- Job ads must not signal a preference by sex, pregnancy, family situation, ethnicity, language, religion, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity or age; avoid "ung og dynamisk". Do not ask about these or about union or political membership. Inviting the under-represented sex to apply is common, and public jobs usually state pay. EU pay-transparency rules are not yet in Norwegian law; check current status.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour tends to be dry, understated and self-deprecating; keep it out of first contacts and formal mail, and do not tease the reader.
- Emphasis comes from facts and bold, not capitals or "!!". The exclamation mark is normal only in set labels: "Obs!", "Viktig!", "Tips!".
- Rewrite English idioms; literal versions sound comic.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Language:** Bokmål and Nynorsk have equal legal status. Public bodies must use both and reply in the standard a person uses; private firms may choose. Sámi languages have equal status with Norwegian, with extra rights in a defined Sámi administrative area; Kven, Romani and Romanes are minority languages.
- **Sámi:** many readers object to Sámi dress or symbols used as decoration or costume in tourism and ads; involve Sámi partners if Sámi culture is shown.
- Income and wealth are not hooks for copy. The oil industry is often a debated topic; avoid casual takes.
- Region, culture, age or gender stereotypes, even flattering ones, are out. Treat the Nynorsk-Bokmål question neutrally.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Nature, outdoor life ("friluftsliv"), skiing and the cabin are authentic positive imagery and good rapport topics.
- Show diverse people of different ages and genders in active roles. Expect to relabel or replace polished retouched stock images.
- Holidays and the national day are lowercase: jul, påske, 17. mai. Gifts in business are modest or absent.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | 17.10.2026; short 17.10.26. Long: 17. oktober 2026, fredag 17. oktober 2026 (lowercase). No slashes; without a year "17.10.". ISO dates only in technical text. |
| Time | 24-hour with "kl.": kl. 14.30 or kl. 14:30. Ranges: kl. 13-15. No am/pm. |
| Numbers | 1 234 567,89 (non-breaking space, decimal comma). Four digits may be unspaced (2500). Ordinals take a full stop: 3. plass. |
| Currency | 1 234,56 kr (code NOK). "kr 1 234" and retail "kr 299,-" are also common; pick one style. "kr" has no full stop. |
| Percent | 25 % (space before the sign). |
| Week starts | Monday; week numbers ("uke 42") widely used for planning. |
| Phone | +47, eight digits: 22 12 34 56. Mobiles start with 4 or 9, often written 912 34 567. |
| Address | Storgata 12 / 0155 Oslo. Street before number; four-digit postcode before the town. |
| Other | Alphabet ends æ, ø, å; forms and URLs must handle them. Abbreviations: f.eks., ca., kl.; write "og", not "&". Metric. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Bokmål and Nynorsk:** most businesses write Bokmål. Nynorsk is the main written standard in many western Norwegian municipalities; copy for public clients or local campaigns there may need Nynorsk. State bodies publish in both.
- **Sámi:** in the north (Finnmark, Troms, parts of Nordland and Trøndelag) public services and some local campaigns use North, Lule or South Sámi alongside Norwegian. A Sámi version of public-facing material is a sign of respect.
- **Neighbour languages:** Danish and Swedish are read without trouble, but consumer copy still needs Norwegian. English is fine for B2B email and in tech.
- **Generations and roles:** some older readers still associate "De" and full names with politeness; younger urban professionals are the most informal. The modesty norm is debated and weaker in startups and sales.
- **Sectors:** finance, law and energy are more formal in dress and tone; public sector and academia are plain and egalitarian.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The matter is in the subject line and the first sentence; at most one context line before it.
- [ ] Norwegian text uses "du"/"dere", never "De", and "du" is lowercase mid-sentence.
- [ ] No titles in address; titles in signatures are lowercase.
- [ ] Hype, superlatives and urgency words are removed; every factual claim can be documented.
- [ ] Requests are concrete (what, who, by when) and do not push for an instant decision.
- [ ] Refusals say no clearly, with a reason and an alternative where possible.
- [ ] No emoji or exclamation marks in professional email; "!" only in labels like "Obs!".
- [ ] Headings in sentence case; months, weekdays, holidays and job titles lowercase.
- [ ] No English calques ("gir deg muligheten til", "din konto"); plain everyday words.
- [ ] Dates, times and numbers use 17.10.2026, kl. 14.30 and 1 234,50; prices use one consistent "kr" style.
- [ ] Retouched images carry the REKLAME label; paid posts start with "reklame"/"annonse"; no alcohol ads.
- [ ] Job ads have no age-coded wording, gendered titles or requests for photo, age or family details.
- [ ] Deadlines and launches avoid July, Easter week and the Christmas-New Year week.
- [ ] Nynorsk or Sámi versions are considered for public clients in western or northern Norway.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (upbeat US-style sales follow-up):

> Hi Ingrid! Huge thanks for your time yesterday - it was AMAZING to meet your team!! As promised, here's our world-class onboarding package. The 15% launch discount ends Friday, so let's lock this in ASAP!

**After** (for a Norwegian buyer, in Norwegian with gloss):

> Hei Ingrid,
>
> Takk for møtet i går. Som avtalt sender jeg tilbudet på opplæringspakken. Prisen er 84 000 kr eks. mva. for 40 brukere, og den gjelder ut november. To kommuner i Rogaland bruker løsningen i dag, og jeg setter dere gjerne i kontakt med dem.
>
> Kan vi ta en kort prat neste uke, etter at dere har sett på det internt?
>
> Vennlig hilsen

*(Gloss: "Hi Ingrid, thanks for the meeting yesterday. As agreed, I am sending the offer for the training package. The price is NOK 84,000 excl. VAT for 40 users and is valid until the end of November. Two municipalities in Rogaland use the solution today, and I am happy to put you in touch with them. Can we have a short talk next week, after you have looked at it internally? Kind regards")*

**Why:** The hype, capitals and "!!" go; facts, a reference customer and a clear price do the persuading. The artificial deadline becomes a validity period that leaves room for the internal agreement Norwegian buyers need, and the call is offered after that, not before.
