# Germany

## In brief

German professional readers want precise, complete, factual text. Clarity counts as respect, and vagueness looks evasive or incompetent. Business is more formal than in English-speaking countries: surnames, titles and "Sie" are the default. Hype and offers that sound too good make readers suspicious, and German law backs this up with strict rules on advertising claims, marketing email and job ads. Data protection and anything touching the Nazi era are areas where mistakes are costly.

## Voice and tone

- Factual (sachlich), precise and complete. Long, detailed documents are fine if well structured.
- Courtesy shows through correct forms of address and accuracy, not warm adjectives.
- Avoid filler, buzzwords and heavy anglicized marketing jargon in German text. Some anglicisms are normal in tech and startups.
- Use active voice and plain statements; Konjunktiv hedging ("ich würde", "könnte") reads as weak, especially in applications.
- Do not ask "How are you?" as a mere formula; it may be answered literally.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, surname. Only the call name is used; further given names appear on legal documents.
- Formal: "Sehr geehrte Frau Schmidt," / "Sehr geehrter Herr Schmidt,". With a doctorate: "Sehr geehrte Frau Dr. Schmidt,". Professors: "Sehr geehrter Herr Prof. Dr. Schmidt," or "Herr Professor Schmidt".
- Unknown reader: "Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren,". Gender unknown: "Guten Tag Alex Schmidt,". Groups: "Liebes Team," or "Guten Tag,".
- "Liebe Frau Schmidt," is warmer but still on "Sie". "Hallo Frau Schmidt," is semi-formal; "Hallo Anna," only on a du basis. Never "Fräulein".
- Start formal: moving from "Sie" to "du" later is easy, moving back is awkward.
- The text after the salutation comma starts in lowercase unless the word is a noun.
- Sign-offs, no comma after them: "Mit freundlichen Grüßen" (formal), "Beste Grüße" (known contacts), "Viele Grüße" (casual), "Liebe Grüße"/"LG" (close contacts only).
- In English email to Germans, "Dear Ms Schmidt" (with title) is safer than first names until invited.
- External business email from registered companies must carry company details in the signature: name with legal form, registered office, register court and number, managing directors (§35a GmbHG, §37a HGB).

## Structuring a message

- Subject line: specific and factual, without "Betreff:" in front ("Angebot Nr. 2026-114: Rückfrage zu Lieferzeit").
- A very short courtesy opener or none; purpose in the first paragraph.
- Full context, numbered points, conditions. Name attachments in the body ("Im Anhang finden Sie ..."). Readers check details.
- End with a clear next step and deadline. "Für Rückfragen stehe ich gerne zur Verfügung." is a common closing line.
- Confirm agreements in writing after calls, with owners and dates; reopening settled points is unwelcome.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement is explicit and not personal: "Das sehe ich anders, weil ..." is normal.
- Criticism is concrete and about the issue. Praise is sparing; "passt" or "gut" is already positive. Younger staff increasingly expect more praise.
- Refusals are clear: "Das ist leider nicht möglich." Pressure or repetition does not replace a new argument.
- Decoding: an employment reference (Arbeitszeugnis) must by law be clear (§109 GewO), yet a coded grading scale persists. "Stets zu unserer vollsten Zufriedenheit" is top; faint praise means criticism.
- Rejection letters to applicants should be short and neutral; wording can become evidence in a discrimination claim.
- When adapting German source text for indirect cultures, soften: German directness can read as rude in English, Japanese or Arabic contexts.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with specifications, test results, certifications (TÜV, DIN, ISO), guarantees, clear prices and visible data protection. Calm design beats countdowns and urgency tactics.
- Misleading claims are actionable under competition law (UWG), usually via a competitor's warning letter. Sole-position claims ("Nr. 1") must be provable. Comparative advertising is legal only if objective, verifiable and not disparaging (§6 UWG).
- Green claims: under the UWG amendment applying from 27 September 2026, generic claims ("umweltfreundlich", "nachhaltig") without proof, offset-based "klimaneutral" and self-made eco seals are banned; future targets ("klimaneutral bis 2030") need a verified plan.
- Label paid content and influencer posts as "Werbung" or "Anzeige" (§5a UWG). Consumer prices must show the total price including VAT (PAngV).
- Job ads must be neutral on gender, origin, religion, disability, age and sexual identity (§11 AGG). Use "(m/w/d)" or neutral nouns, and keep the body neutral too. Avoid "junges Team", age limits and "Deutsch als Muttersprache"; write "sehr gute Deutschkenntnisse" with a reason. Rejected applicants can claim compensation within two months.
- Pay transparency: the EU directive was due by June 2026; German transposition was still pending at last check (check current status). Salary ranges are increasingly expected.
- CV (Lebenslauf): tabular, reverse chronological, at most two A4 pages. A professional photo and date of birth are common but optional. The cover letter (Anschreiben) fits one page and ends with an interview request; send everything, with certificates and Zeugnisse, as one PDF.
- Gender-inclusive German (Mitarbeiter*innen, colon forms) is polarized. Bavaria and Saxony restrict special characters in official use. Follow the client; neutral forms ("Mitarbeitende", "Team") avoid the debate.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor exists (irony, wordplay) but stays out of serious business text. A joke in the wrong place reads as unprofessional.
- Self-deprecating humor can be taken literally as lack of competence.
- Exclamation marks and capitals for emphasis look unprofessional. Bold for key facts is fine.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Nazi era: symbols, slogans, salutes and lookalikes of banned organisations are illegal, with narrow historical or educational exceptions (§86a StGB). No Nazi comparisons, Holocaust references or WWII jokes. The first stanza of the old anthem is linked to the far right.
- Do not address Germans as collectively responsible for WWII ("you Germans...").
- Marketing email needs prior express consent (§7 UWG), proven by double opt-in. The existing-customer exception is narrow and needs an opt-out notice in every mail. Every newsletter needs an unsubscribe link and sender details. US-style opt-out lists do not work; use Bcc for group mails.
- Websites and newsletters need an Impressum under §5 DDG (the TMG is repealed) plus a GDPR privacy policy. Cookie consent must be genuine opt-in.
- Privacy is a cultural value as well as law, with strong works-council rights on employee monitoring. Copy that celebrates tracking lands badly.
- Migration, East/West differences and the far right are politically charged.
- Insults can be a criminal offence; aggressive or mocking copy about real people is risky. Using "Dr." without holding it is also an offence; never add a title the person does not hold.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Avoid imagery that evokes Nazi aesthetics: Fraktur in political contexts, militaristic eagles, neo-Nazi number codes (18, 88), also in product numbers and prices.
- Thumb-first counting: a raised thumb means "one". Germans "press thumbs" (Daumen drücken) rather than cross fingers.
- Black, red and gold appear mainly around football; nationalistic imagery is otherwise avoided.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | DD.MM.YYYY (07.03.2026). Long form: 7. März 2026. |
| Time | 24-hour clock: 14:30 Uhr. |
| Numbers | 1.234.567,89; "15 %" with a space. Mio., Mrd.: English "billion" is "Milliarde"; German "Billion" is 10^12. |
| Currency | 1.234,50 € (symbol after amount, with a space). |
| Week starts | Monday. Calendar weeks (KW 10) are widely used for scheduling. |
| Units | Metric. |
| Phone | +49 30 1234567; extensions after a hyphen (030 1234567-12). |
| Address | Street then number (Hauptstraße 5), five-digit postcode before city (10115 Berlin). |
| Other | „German quotation marks“. Letters follow DIN 5008. School and some public holidays differ by federal state. |

## Variation within the culture

- Startups, tech and creative agencies use "du" and English freely; banks, law firms, administration and Mittelstand industrial firms are more formal. Some consumer brands use "du"; premium, financial and official contexts stay with "Sie".
- Strong regional identities (Bavaria, Rhineland, Hamburg, Berlin). Avoid Bavarian stereotypes as symbols of all Germany.
- Former East Germany is less religious and has its own historical sensitivities (Stasi, reunification).
- Austria and Switzerland differ in vocabulary, formats and tone: Austria is more title-conscious, Switzerland more indirect and drops "ß". Do not reuse German copy unchanged.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Every request specifies what, who, by when and under what conditions.
- [ ] Vague timing ("soon", "in a few days") is replaced with dates.
- [ ] Hype words, superlatives and unverifiable or generic green claims are removed or substantiated.
- [ ] "Sie" unless the brand or relationship clearly uses "du", applied consistently.
- [ ] Salutation includes correct title (Dr., Prof.) and uses "Frau", never "Fräulein".
- [ ] No "Betreff:" before the subject; no comma after the closing formula.
- [ ] Dates, numbers and currency use German formats; "billion" rendered as "Milliarde".
- [ ] No Nazi-era references, comparisons, symbols or jokes.
- [ ] Marketing email has provable opt-in consent and an unsubscribe link.
- [ ] Job ads use (m/w/d) or neutral nouns, with no age, origin or "Muttersprache" wording.
- [ ] Paid content labelled "Werbung"/"Anzeige"; consumer prices include VAT.
- [ ] Web copy links an Impressum (§5 DDG) and privacy notice.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (relationship-first, vague):

> Hi Thomas! Hope you're doing great! Just wanted to touch base about the project. We're running a little behind but should be done pretty soon. Let me know your thoughts!

**After** (tailored for German readers, in English):

> Dear Mr Becker,
>
> The data migration (work package 3) will be completed on 24 March instead of 17 March. Reason: the client's test environment was available one week later than planned. Work packages 4 and 5 are not affected.
>
> Please confirm by 12 March whether the revised date works for your acceptance test.
>
> Kind regards,

**Why:** German readers want the facts, cause, impact and a dated decision request. Exclamation marks and vague timing undermine credibility.
