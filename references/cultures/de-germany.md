# Germany

## In brief

German professional readers want precise, complete, factual text. Directness is a form of respect, and vagueness looks evasive or incompetent. Formality is higher than in English-speaking countries: surnames, titles and the formal "Sie" are the default in business. Hype and offers that seem unrealistically good make readers suspicious. Data protection, accuracy of claims and anything touching the Nazi era are areas where mistakes are costly.

## Voice and tone

- Factual (sachlich), precise and complete. Long, detailed documents are acceptable if well structured.
- Courtesy is shown through correct forms of address and accuracy, not through warm adjectives.
- Avoid filler phrases, buzzwords and anglicized marketing jargon in German-language text. Some anglicisms are normal in tech and startups, but heavy use reads as trendy and unclear.
- Do not ask "How are you?" as a mere formula; Germans may treat it as a real question.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name(s), surname. Multiple given names are one full first name, not a middle name.
- Formal salutation: "Sehr geehrte Frau Schmidt," / "Sehr geehrter Herr Schmidt,". With a doctorate: "Sehr geehrte Frau Dr. Schmidt,". Professors: "Sehr geehrter Herr Professor Schmidt,".
- Gender-neutral salutation when gender is unknown: "Guten Tag Alex Schmidt," or "Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren," for organizations.
- "Fräulein" is outdated and can be offensive. Use "Frau" for all women.
- Semi-formal: "Hallo Frau Schmidt,". Informal (du-basis): "Hallo Anna,".
- Sign-off: "Mit freundlichen Grüßen" (formal), "Viele Grüße" or "Beste Grüße" (neutral), "LG" (Liebe Grüße) only for close contacts.
- In English-language email to Germans, "Dear Ms Schmidt" with a title is safer than first names until invited.
- Business email must legally include company details in the signature (company name, legal form, registered office, register court and number, managing directors).

## Structuring a message

- Subject line: specific and factual ("Angebot Nr. 2026-114: Rückfrage zu Lieferzeit").
- Very short courtesy opener or none; purpose in the first paragraph.
- Provide full context, numbered points, conditions and attachments. Readers will check details.
- End with a clear next step and deadline. "Für Rückfragen stehe ich gerne zur Verfügung." is the standard closing line.
- Agreements are confirmed in writing; changes later are unwelcome.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement is expected to be explicit and is not personal. "Das sehe ich anders, weil..." is normal.
- Criticism focuses on the issue and is concrete. Praise is sparing and specific; "Passt" or "gut" is already positive feedback.
- Refusals are clear: "Das ist leider nicht möglich." Do not keep pushing after a refusal.
- When adapting German source text for indirect cultures, soften: German directness can read as rude in English, Japanese or Arabic contexts.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with specifications, test results, certifications (TÜV, DIN, ISO), guarantees and clear prices.
- German competition law (UWG) prohibits misleading claims; sole-position claims ("Germany's best", "No. 1") must be provably true. Comparative advertising is allowed only under strict conditions.
- Environmental claims ("klimaneutral", "nachhaltig") face strict court scrutiny and EU greenwashing rules.
- Websites need an Impressum (legal notice) and a GDPR-compliant privacy policy. Cookie consent must be genuine opt-in.
- Job adverts: by convention under the General Equal Treatment Act (AGG), job titles carry "(m/w/d)" (male/female/diverse). Avoid age-coded wording ("junges Team").
- CV (Lebenslauf): tabular, reverse chronological, one to three pages. A photo and date of birth are still common but optional; many now omit them. A cover letter (Anschreiben) is formal and precise. Certificates (Zeugnisse) and references from employers carry weight.
- Gender-inclusive German (Gendersternchen "Mitarbeiter*innen", colon or participle forms) is a polarized topic. Some state governments ban the asterisk in official documents. Choose the convention the client uses; neutral forms ("Mitarbeitende", "Team") avoid the debate.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor exists (irony, wordplay) but is kept out of serious business communication. A joke in the wrong place reads as unprofessional.
- Self-deprecating humor can be taken literally as lack of competence.
- Exclamation marks and capital letters for emphasis look unprofessional. Bold for key facts is fine.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Nazi era: Nazi symbols, slogans and salutes are illegal (with narrow historical or educational exceptions). Never use Nazi comparisons, Holocaust references or WWII jokes in marketing or casual text. The first stanza of the old anthem is associated with the far right.
- Do not address Germans as collectively responsible for WWII ("you Germans...").
- Data privacy is a cultural value and a legal requirement (GDPR, plus strong works-council rules on employee monitoring). Copy that celebrates tracking, data collection or surveillance features lands badly.
- Migration and refugees, East/West differences, and the far right are politically charged.
- Insulting people can be a criminal offence. Aggressive or mocking copy about real people is risky.
- Unauthorized use of "Dr." is a criminal offence; never add a title the person does not hold.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Avoid imagery that evokes Nazi aesthetics: certain fonts (Fraktur in political context), eagles with militaristic styling, specific number codes used by neo-Nazis (18, 88).
- Thumb-first counting: a raised thumb means "one".
- Germans "press thumbs" (Daumen drücken) rather than cross fingers for luck.
- Black, red and gold national colors are used mainly around football; nationalistic imagery is otherwise avoided.

## Variation within the culture

- Startups, tech and creative agencies use "du" and English freely; banks, law firms, public administration and Mittelstand industrial firms are much more formal.
- Strong regional identities (Bavaria, Rhineland, Hamburg, Berlin). Avoid Bavarian stereotypes (lederhosen, beer) as symbols of all Germany.
- Former East Germany is less religious and has different historical sensitivities (Stasi, reunification).
- Austria and German-speaking Switzerland differ (vocabulary, "ß" not used in Switzerland, more indirect communication in Switzerland). Do not reuse German copy unchanged.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Every request specifies what, who, by when and under what conditions.
- [ ] Vague timing ("soon", "in a few days") is replaced with dates.
- [ ] Hype words, superlatives and unverifiable claims are removed or substantiated.
- [ ] German-language text uses "Sie" unless the brand or relationship clearly uses "du", and is consistent.
- [ ] Salutation includes correct title (Dr., Prof.) and uses "Frau", never "Fräulein".
- [ ] Dates, numbers and currency use German formats.
- [ ] No Nazi-era references, comparisons, symbols or jokes.
- [ ] Data collection features are described with privacy and consent in mind.
- [ ] Job ads include (m/w/d) and avoid age-coded language.
- [ ] Web copy has Impressum and privacy notice linked where relevant.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (relationship-first, vague):

> Hi Thomas! Hope you're doing great! Just wanted to touch base about the project. We're running a little behind but should be done pretty soon. Let me know your thoughts!

**After** (tailored for German readers, in English):

> Dear Mr Becker,
>
> The data migration (work package 3) will be completed on 24 March instead of 17 March. Reason: the client's test environment was available one week later than planned. Work packages 4 and 5 are not affected.
>
> Please confirm by 12 March whether the revised date works for your acceptance test.
>
> Kind regards,

**Why:** German readers want the facts, cause, impact and a dated decision request. Exclamation marks and vague timing undermine credibility.
