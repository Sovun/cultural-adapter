# France

## In brief

French readers value elegant, well-reasoned writing, correct etiquette and respect for the French language. Arguments are expected to be built logically, often from principle to conclusion, and debate is a sign of engagement, not hostility. Formality is high in first contact: "vous", titles and courteous formulas matter. By law, many commercial and employment texts must be in French, and secularism (laïcité) makes religion a sensitive topic in public communication.

## Voice and tone

- Polished, precise, slightly formal. Good French style is itself a credibility signal; clumsy or machine-like French hurts the brand.
- Intellectual framing (concepts, reasoning, cultural references) is welcomed in B2B and editorial content.
- Avoid American-style cheerleading ("You're going to love this!") and excessive emoji in professional text.
- Anglicisms are common in tech and marketing, but a French equivalent is preferred in official and public-sector text.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name(s), surname. Compound given names (Jean-Marc) are one name.
- Salutation in formal letters: "Madame, Monsieur," or "Madame," / "Monsieur," without the surname. In email: "Bonjour Madame Dupont," or "Bonjour Monsieur Martin," is now standard.
- "Mademoiselle" is no longer used in administrative or professional contexts. Use "Madame" for all women.
- Abbreviations: M. (Monsieur), Mme (Madame). Titles: "Maître" for lawyers and notaries, "Docteur" for physicians.
- Use "vous" in all professional communication unless the company culture clearly uses "tu" (some startups and brands for young audiences do).
- Sign-offs in email: "Cordialement", "Bien cordialement", "Bien à vous". Formal letters end with a full courtesy formula ("Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.").
- Always start a contact with "Bonjour" (or "Bonsoir"). Skipping the greeting is rude in speech and abrupt in writing.

## Structuring a message

- Greeting and a courteous opening line, then context and reasoning, then the request.
- Longer, well-articulated paragraphs are acceptable. Bullet-point-only messages can seem curt in formal correspondence.
- Show that you have thought through the issue: anticipate objections and explain the logic.
- Close politely with an offer to discuss further.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Intellectual disagreement is normal and can be forceful. Being contradicted is not a personal attack.
- Critique tends to be explicit; praise is rarer and more measured. "Pas mal" can be a real compliment.
- High-pressure sales tactics and confrontational persuasion backfire; logical, low-key presentation works.
- When adapting French source text for indirect cultures (Japan, Thailand, UK), soften critical statements.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- **Loi Toubon (1994)**: advertising, product labels and instructions, user manuals, contracts, job offers, employment documents and workplace documents in France must be in French. Foreign-language slogans need a French translation of equal legibility. Treat English-only copy for French consumers or employees as a legal risk.
- Comparative advertising is allowed only if objective and verifiable. Environmental claims are tightly regulated (Climate and Resilience Law 2021 restricts "carbon neutral" claims).
- Quality, craftsmanship, heritage, aesthetics and savoir-faire are strong persuasion levers. Price-only messaging can cheapen a brand.
- CV: typically one page (two for senior roles), with a short profile. A photo was traditional and is now optional; employers may not discriminate on it. Education and diplomas are prominent. A formal cover letter (lettre de motivation) is expected for many roles.
- Salary is rarely discussed openly; avoid asking about personal finances.

## Humor and emphasis

- Wit, irony, wordplay and satire are appreciated. Punchline jokes and slapstick less so.
- Humor in professional text is acceptable if clever and understated. Avoid humor that mocks French culture or language from outside.
- Exclamation marks are used more in French than in English, but in business text one is enough.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Laïcité**: religion is considered private. Avoid religious references in general marketing and workplace communication. Religious symbols are banned for public servants at work and in public schools; the full face veil is banned in public.
- Immigration, integration, the status of minorities and the colonial past (especially Algeria) are highly sensitive.
- France does not collect official statistics on ethnicity or religion. Copy that categorizes people by ethnicity sits uncomfortably with the republican ideal of an indivisible people.
- Politics: asking or presuming whom someone votes for is rude.
- Money and wealth: avoid ostentation and salary talk.
- Criticism of France or the French language by outsiders is resented even though the French criticize themselves freely.
- Do not equate France with Paris. Regions such as Brittany, Corsica, Alsace and the Basque Country have strong identities.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Chrysanthemums are associated with funerals and graves.
- The thumbs-up and the OK sign both mean "fine" (the OK circle can also mean "zero"/worthless in some regions).
- Imagery that clichés France (berets, baguettes, Eiffel Tower everywhere) reads as touristy rather than local.

## Variation within the culture

- Paris is faster, more international and more English-friendly than the provinces.
- Startups and tech firms use more English and "tu"; public sector, luxury, law and finance are traditional.
- Belgian, Swiss, Quebec and African French differ in vocabulary, formality and some formats (Quebec uses different typography and many distinct terms). Do not reuse France-specific copy unchanged.
- Younger professionals are more open to direct, informal digital communication.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Text addressed to French consumers or employees is in French (Loi Toubon), or a French version exists.
- [ ] "Vous" is used consistently unless the brand deliberately uses "tu".
- [ ] Salutation and sign-off use proper French formulas; no "Mademoiselle".
- [ ] The argument shows reasoning and context, not just a list of benefits.
- [ ] Hard-sell language, urgency tricks and hype are toned down.
- [ ] No religious references in general business or HR communication.
- [ ] No talk of salary or personal wealth unless the context requires it.
- [ ] French typography is correct (spaces before ; : ! ?, guillemets, lowercase months).
- [ ] Dates, numbers and currency use French formats.
- [ ] France is not reduced to Paris or tourist clichés.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style sales email):

> Hi Claire! Our tool will 10x your team's productivity. Book a demo today, spots are filling up fast!

**After** (tailored for French readers, translated back into English for illustration):

> Dear Ms Laurent,
>
> Many finance teams lose several days each month consolidating reports by hand. Our approach automates this consolidation while keeping full traceability of each figure, which your auditors will require. At Groupe Martin, closing time fell from eight to five days.
>
> If you think this is relevant to your situation, I would be happy to present the method to you in a 30-minute meeting at your convenience.
>
> Kind regards,

**Why:** French readers expect a courteous form of address, a reasoned problem statement before the offer and measured claims. Pressure tactics and hype undermine credibility.
