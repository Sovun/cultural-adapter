# France

## In brief

French readers value elegant, well-reasoned writing, correct etiquette and respect for the French language. Proposals are expected to show their reasoning before the recommendation, and debate is a sign of engagement, not hostility. Formality is high in first contact: "vous", "Madame/Monsieur" and fixed courtesy formulas matter, and errors in them are noticed. By law, advertising, consumer information, job ads and employment documents must be in French, and marketing copy faces strict rules on consent, labelling, price claims and green claims. Secularism (laïcité) keeps religion out of public and business communication.

## Voice and tone

- Polished, precise, slightly formal. Good French style is itself a credibility signal; literal or machine-translated French is spotted at once and hurts trust.
- Logical framing persuades in B2B and editorial content; emotional pitches carry little weight. Lifestyle consumer marketing allows more narrative.
- Avoid American-style cheerleading ("You're going to love this!") and emoji in professional text.
- Anglicisms are common in tech and marketing, but gratuitous ones ("feedback" for "retour") are noticed, and official and public-sector text prefers French terms.
- Plan for French text running about 15-30% longer than English in layouts and UI.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name(s), surname. Forms and introductions often put the surname first, in capitals (DUPONT Marie). Compound given names (Jean-Marc) are one name.
- Salutation: "Madame, Monsieur," for an unknown reader; "Madame," / "Monsieur," alone in formal letters. In email "Bonjour Madame," is the safe form; "Bonjour Madame Dupont," is common but some readers find the surname after "Madame" incorrect. "Bonjour Marie," for colleagues and established contacts.
- "Cher/Chère" with a stranger sounds presumptuous; keep it for people you know.
- Function titles in formal salutations: "Madame la Directrice", "Monsieur le Président"; "Maître" for lawyers and notaries, "Docteur" for physicians.
- "Mademoiselle" was removed from administrative forms in 2012 and reads dated or patronising in business. Use "Madame" for all women. Abbreviations: M. (not "Mr") and Mme.
- Use "vous" in all professional communication; switching to "tu" is proposed explicitly ("On peut se tutoyer ?"), usually by the other side. Keep "vous" with senior people even if they are friendly.
- Sign-offs in email: "Bien cordialement" (neutral and safe), "Cordialement" (cooler), "Bien à vous" (slightly warmer). Never "Cdt" or other abbreviations.
- Formal letters end with a full formula that repeats the exact salutation: "Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées." Avoid pompous variants and grammar slips ("En espérant..., veuillez agréer").

## Structuring a message

- Email form: a short noun-phrase subject ("Demande de devis"), salutation, body in full sentences, closing formula, signature with title, company and phone. Ending on just "Merci" or a name looks abrupt.
- Routine emails: a short contextual opener ("Suite à notre échange...", "Je me permets de vous contacter..."), then the purpose. No chatty small talk.
- Proposals and first pitches: context and reasoning, then the recommendation. Anticipate objections and explain the logic.
- Longer, well-articulated paragraphs are acceptable; bullet-only messages can seem curt in formal correspondence.
- Readers expect an agenda and documents before meetings. Meetings analyse and debate; decisions often come later, so follow-up must not assume agreement.
- Soften requests with the conditional: "Pourriez-vous...", "Serait-il possible de...". Chase with "Je me permets de revenir vers vous."

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Intellectual disagreement is normal and can be forceful. Being contradicted is not a personal attack, but an objection needs a reasoned counterargument.
- Critique tends to be explicit; praise is rarer and more measured. "Pas mal" can be a real compliment.
- Debate is welcome, personal confrontation and pressure are not. High-pressure sales tactics backfire; a logical, low-key presentation works.
- A friendly meeting or a "we will see" is not a yes. Do not push for a quick close.
- When adapting French source text for indirect cultures (Japan, Thailand, UK), soften critical statements.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- **Loi Toubon (1994)**: advertising, product descriptions, labels, user manuals, warranties, invoices and consumer contracts aimed at France must be in French. Foreign-language slogans need a French translation that is as legible. Employment contracts, internal rules and documents employees need for their work must also be in French; courts have refused to enforce pay objectives written only in English.
- **Job ads** (Code du travail): written in French even if another language is required; gender-neutral titles with "H/F"; no age limit and no "junior/senior" or age brackets ("débutant", "expérimenté" instead); no mention of sex, family situation, health or other protected grounds; dated and truthful. Employers may not require a photo. Ads are expected to state the contract type (CDI/CDD) and pay or a range; EU pay-transparency rules were due in June 2026, check current status.
- **Influencers and ads**: commercial content must carry "Publicité" or "Collaboration commerciale" throughout; retouched or AI-generated bodies need "Images retouchées" or "Images virtuelles" (law of June 2023). The ARPP self-regulator bans degrading or sexist portrayals.
- **Prices**: an announced reduction must use as reference the lowest price charged in the previous 30 days (at last check; a loosening was reported, check current status). "Soldes" may be advertised only in the legal winter and summer sales periods.
- **Green claims**: the 2021 Climate and Resilience Law limits "neutre en carbone" claims; EU Directive 2024/825 applies from 27 September 2026 and bans offset-based neutrality claims and vague claims ("écologique", "respectueux de l'environnement") without proof. French transposition was still pending at last check; treat the directive's rules as the standard.
- Superlatives ("le meilleur", "n° 1") need substantiation; comparative advertising must be objective and verifiable.
- Quality, craftsmanship, heritage and savoir-faire are strong levers; recognised labels (Label Rouge, AB), French reviews and French reference customers build trust more than foreign examples.
- CV: one page (two for senior roles), education prominent, reverse chronological, no exaggeration. A photo is traditional but optional. A tailored cover letter (lettre de motivation) is expected; references "sur demande".

## Humor and emphasis

- Wit, irony, wordplay and satire are appreciated more than punchline jokes and slapstick.
- Irony with someone you do not know is risky; keep humour for established relationships and consumer copy. Avoid humour that mocks French culture or language from outside.
- Do not carry English exclamation marks into French; strength comes from word choice. In business text, use none.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Laïcité**: religion is private. Avoid religious references in marketing and workplace communication. Religious symbols are banned for public servants at work and in public schools; the full face veil is banned in public.
- **Email marketing (CNIL, GDPR)**: B2C email and SMS need prior opt-in; pre-ticked boxes and consent hidden in terms do not count. Existing customers may get offers for similar products if they could object. B2B prospecting may be opt-out when the message concerns the recipient's job. Every message names the sender and offers a free unsubscribe.
- **Alcohol (Loi Evin)**: ads are limited to authorised media and to objective information (origin, composition, production) with a health message; no association with success, seduction, sport or parties. Influencer posts are litigated. Send alcohol copy to legal review.
- **Inclusive writing**: the point médian ("candidat·e·s") is politically marked and banned in official texts (2017 circular) and schools (2021). Doublets ("candidates et candidats"), neutral nouns ("le personnel") and feminised titles ("la directrice", accepted by the Académie française in 2019) are safe.
- Immigration, integration, minorities and the colonial past (especially Algeria) are highly sensitive. France keeps no official ethnic or religious statistics, and copy that sorts people by ethnicity clashes with the republican ideal.
- Politics: asking or presuming whom someone votes for is rude. Avoid ostentation and salary talk.
- National and linguistic identity is strong: criticism of France by outsiders and English-only openings read as disrespect.
- Do not equate France with Paris. Brittany, Corsica, Alsace and the Basque Country have strong identities.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Chrysanthemums are associated with funerals and graves.
- The thumbs-up means "fine", but the OK circle often means "zero" or "worthless". Avoid it as an icon.
- Imagery that clichés France (berets, baguettes, Eiffel Tower everywhere) reads as touristy rather than local.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | DD/MM/YYYY (07/03/2026). Long form: 7 mars 2026, lundi 7 mars 2026 (lowercase). |
| Time | 24-hour clock: 14:30, or 14 h 30 in prose. No AM/PM. |
| Numbers | 1 234 567,89 (narrow non-breaking space for thousands, comma for decimals). Percent: 25 %. |
| Currency | 1 234,50 € (symbol after amount, with a non-breaking space). |
| Week starts | Monday; ISO week numbers. Spreadsheet list separator is a semicolon. |
| Punctuation | Non-breaking space before : ; ! ? and inside « guillemets ». Curly apostrophe. |
| Capitalization | Few capitals: no English Title Case in headings or buttons. Days, months, languages and adjectival nationalities lowercase. Capitals keep accents (À, É). |
| Phone | 01 23 45 67 89; international +33 1 23 45 67 89 (pairs of digits). |
| Address | Number before street; 5-digit postcode before city (75008 Paris). |

## Variation within the culture

- Paris is faster, more international and more English-friendly than the provinces.
- Start-ups, media and tech use more English and "tu"; banks, insurance, crafts, the public sector and luxury stay traditional.
- Belgian, Swiss, Quebec and African French differ in vocabulary, formality, closing formulas and some formats (Quebec has no space before ; ! ?; fr-CH writes money as 1'234.50 CHF; Belgium and Switzerland say "septante", "nonante"). Do not reuse France-specific copy unchanged.
- Younger professionals often move to "tu" and informal digital channels faster, but surveys conflict; follow the reader's lead.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style sales email):

> Hi Claire! Our tool will 10x your team's productivity. Book a demo today, spots are filling up fast!

**After** (tailored for French readers, translated back into English for illustration):

> Madam,
>
> Many finance teams lose several days each month consolidating reports by hand. Our approach automates this consolidation while keeping full traceability of each figure, which your auditors will require. At Groupe Martin, closing time fell from eight to five days.
>
> If you think this is relevant to your situation, I would be happy to present the method to you in a 30-minute meeting at your convenience.
>
> Kind regards,

**Why:** French readers expect a courteous form of address ("Madame," or "Bonjour Madame,"), a reasoned problem statement before the offer, a measured and checkable claim and a proper closing ("Bien cordialement"). Pressure tactics, hype and exclamation marks undermine credibility.
