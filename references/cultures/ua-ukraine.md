# Ukraine

## In brief

Ukrainian readers expect respectful, sincere and substantive writing. Formality is high at the start ("Ви", name plus patronymic or "пане/пані"), and trust is personal: reliability and follow-through matter more than polished slogans. Hyperbolic American-style enthusiasm reads as insincere. Since 2014, and especially since the full-scale Russian invasion in 2022, language and naming choices carry heavy political weight: Ukrainian forms of names, no association with Russia, and no trivializing of the war.

## Voice and tone

- Respectful, sincere, substantive. Avoid sales-speak and exaggeration.
- Explain the purpose behind requests; bare instructions without rationale meet resistance.
- Visual aids (charts, diagrams) are appreciated in proposals.
- In the current context, resilience and solidarity themes resonate, but exploitative or performative war references are deeply resented.

## Addressing people

- Full name: given name, patronymic, surname (Oleksandr Petrovych Shevchenko). Feminine surnames often end in -a or -ka; some surnames do not change by gender.
- Formal address in Ukrainian uses the **vocative case**: "Шановний пане Олександре" (Dear Mr Oleksandr), "Шановна пані Олено" (Dear Ms Olena). Name plus patronymic is also formal and common in government, education, healthcare and with older people ("Олександре Петровичу").
- Younger professionals and IT/startup companies quickly move to first names ("Олександре, добрий день!"), often in English.
- Use "Ви" (capitalized in letters to one person as a sign of respect) until invited to switch to "ти".
- Sign-offs: "З повагою," (standard), "З найкращими побажаннями," (warm), "Гарного дня!" (friendly).
- In English: "Dear Mr Shevchenko" in first contact; first names once the other side uses them.
- Transliterate names with the official Ukrainian national system (Cabinet of Ministers 2010): Kyiv, Kharkiv, Odesa, Oleksandr, Mykola, Volodymyr. Never Russian forms (Kiev, Kharkov, Odessa, Aleksandr, Nikolai, Vladimir) when referring to Ukrainian people or places.

## Structuring a message

- Polite greeting and a short courteous line; for ongoing relationships, a personal word is welcome.
- Give the bigger aim first, then specifics and the request.
- State exactly what kind of answer or document you need, otherwise you may receive more detail than expected.
- After calls and meetings, send a written recap of agreements; verbal promises do not always become actions.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- A direct "no" may be avoided to spare feelings; listen for hesitation or silence.
- Initial skepticism or disagreement can shift after a clear explanation and detailed plan.
- Feedback on mistakes: private, collaborative, solution-oriented and acknowledging the person's expertise. Public blame causes loss of face.
- Negotiating price directly is uncomfortable for some; discussing scope or implementation changes is an easier route to cost reduction.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with substance: concrete benefits, reliability, guarantees and evidence. Long-term commitment and dependability are strong values.
- Ukrainian is the state language. The 2019 Law on the State Language requires Ukrainian for consumer services, advertising, product information, websites of businesses serving Ukrainian consumers and official communication (with limited exceptions for other languages alongside Ukrainian). Russian-only consumer content is both a legal risk and deeply alienating.
- Patriotic and solidarity messaging works when authentic and supported by real action (donations, support for veterans, local production); opportunistic use backfires.
- CVs: one to two pages, often with a photo. IT and international companies expect English CVs in US/European style with achievements and metrics.
- Ukrainian IT professionals are used to international norms and fairly direct, task-focused English communication.

## Humor and emphasis

- Wordplay, irony and dark humor are common among Ukrainians, including about the war, but outsiders should not joke about war, losses, mobilization or air raids.
- Avoid casual military and violent metaphors in marketing ("bomb", "killer feature", "blitz", "attack the market", "shoot") where they may evoke the war.
- Exclamation marks in business text: sparingly.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Never** say or imply that Ukraine is part of Russia, that Ukrainians and Russians are "one people", or that Ukrainian is a dialect of Russian.
- Write "Ukraine", never "the Ukraine". Use "Kyiv", not "Kiev".
- Do not use Russian place names for Ukrainian cities. Refer to occupied territories (Crimea, parts of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson regions) as temporarily occupied territories of Ukraine; maps must show Ukraine's internationally recognized borders.
- Do not use the term "Great Patriotic War"; in Ukraine it is "the Second World War" (official Ukrainian usage).
- Holodomor is recognized in Ukraine as a genocide. Treat it with gravity.
- Avoid praising Russian culture or drawing parallels between Ukrainian and Russian culture.
- Soviet and Nazi symbols are banned in Ukraine (decommunization laws). The "Z" and "V" symbols used by the Russian army are illegal to promote and deeply offensive.
- Domestic politics and specific politicians are contentious.
- Historical figures such as Stepan Bandera are viewed very differently in Ukraine than in Poland or Russia. Avoid taking a position in content aimed at mixed Ukrainian-Polish audiences.
- LGBT topics: public attitudes are improving, especially among younger people and in cities, but still mixed.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Blue and yellow are national colors; the tryzub (trident) is the coat of arms. Use them respectfully.
- Sunflowers, vyshyvanka (embroidered shirt) and wheat fields are positive national imagery; avoid kitsch.
- Even-numbered flowers are for funerals; give odd numbers.
- The "OK" hand sign and the "fig" gesture can be rude.
- Imagery of destruction, the military or the wounded must not be used for commercial purposes.

## Variation within the culture

- Western Ukraine (Lviv) is predominantly Ukrainian-speaking and more traditional and religious (Greek Catholic); "Пане/Пані" forms are especially common there.
- Kyiv, Dnipro, Kharkiv and Odesa were historically more Russian-speaking; many people have switched to Ukrainian since 2022, which is a personal and sensitive choice.
- Large internally displaced and diaspora populations (Poland, Germany, Czechia, UK, Canada, US). Diaspora content may need sensitivity to the host country.
- IT and startup sectors are informal, English-friendly and globally oriented.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style, with risky wording):

> Our killer new feature will blow up your sales in Kiev and the rest of the CIS market!

**After** (tailored for Ukrainian readers):

> The new feature helps sales teams in Kyiv and across Ukraine process orders 25% faster. We've tested it with three Ukrainian retailers over the last six months.

**Why:** Violent metaphors evoke the war, "Kiev" and "CIS" signal a Russian-centric frame, and hype reads as insincere. Ukrainian readers respond to concrete, locally proven results.
