# Ukraine

## In brief

Ukrainian readers expect respectful, sincere and substantive writing. Formality is high at the start ("Ви", vocative case, name plus patronymic or "пане/пані"), and trust is personal: reliability and follow-through matter more than polished slogans. Hyperbolic American-style enthusiasm reads as insincere. Since 2014, and especially since Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, language and naming carry heavy weight: Ukrainian forms of names, current decolonised place names, no association with Russia and no trivialising of the war. Consumer-facing text must be in Ukrainian by law, and advertising, marketing consent and job ads have concrete legal rules.

## Voice and tone

- Respectful, sincere, substantive. Avoid sales-speak, exaggeration and overpromising; a serious, matter-of-fact tone is not hostility.
- Explain the purpose behind requests; bare instructions without rationale meet resistance. Fewer "thanks" and "sorry" than in English is normal.
- Wartime context: cheerful "business as usual" copy can grate. Resilience and solidarity themes resonate, but exploitative or performative war references are deeply resented.
- Product and UI copy: warm, plain and conversational; business and technical text stays formal. Do not calque English: drop "будь ласка" from every instruction ("Спробуйте ще раз."), avoid "ваш" everywhere, prefer verbs to noun chains ("Не вдалося видалити файл", not "здійснити видалення").

## Addressing people

- Full name: given name, patronymic, surname (Oleksandr Petrovych Shevchenko). Many common surnames (-enko, -uk, -chuk) do not change by gender; adjectival ones do (Kovalskyi, Kovalska).
- Use the **vocative case**; the adjective stays nominative: "Шановний пане Олександре!", "Шановна пані Олено!", "Шановний Олександре Івановичу!". "Шановний Андрій" (nominative) is the most common error.
- Name plus patronymic is standard in government, education, healthcare, banking and with older people. "Пане/пані + first name" is a respectful modern option nationwide.
- Most formal: by position ("Шановний пане міністре!"). Groups: "Шановні колеги!", "Шановні панове!". "Дорогий" is not for official letters. "!" after the salutation is traditional; a comma is common in email.
- Younger professionals and IT companies move quickly to first names ("Олександре, добрий день!"), often in English.
- Use "Ви" (capitalised in letters to one person; lowercase in UI) until invited to switch to "ти".
- Sign-offs: "З повагою," (standard), "З найкращими побажаннями," (warm), "Гарного дня!" (friendly).
- Use feminine job titles for a known woman (директорка, авторка); they are part of the 2019 orthography.
- In English: "Dear Mr Shevchenko" in first contact; first names once the other side uses them.
- Transliterate with the official national system (Cabinet of Ministers Resolution No. 55, 2010): Kyiv, Kharkiv, Odesa, Lviv, Oleksandr, Mykola, Volodymyr. Never Russian forms (Kiev, Kharkov, Odessa, Lvov, Aleksandr, Nikolai, Vladimir) for Ukrainian people or places.

## Structuring a message

- Always open with a greeting ("Добрий день!" is the safe choice) and a short courteous line; in ongoing relationships a personal word is welcome, but do not probe losses, displacement or family.
- Give the bigger aim first, then specifics and the request.
- State exactly what kind of answer or document you need, otherwise you may receive more detail than expected.
- After calls and meetings, send a written recap of agreements; verbal promises do not always become actions.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Early in a relationship and upward, a flat "no" may be avoided; listen for hesitation or silence. With established contacts and in IT, people are frank, and Ukrainians writing in English can sound blunter than intended.
- Initial skepticism or disagreement can shift after a clear explanation and detailed plan; detailed questions often signal interest.
- Feedback on mistakes: private, collaborative, solution-oriented and acknowledging the person's expertise. Public blame causes loss of face.
- Price usually comes last; silence may mean it is too high, and early concessions can read as weakness.
- Job rejections: unreasoned refusals are unlawful and written reasons must be given on request (Labour Code Art. 22); give a brief, real reason.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with substance: concrete benefits, reliability, guarantees, evidence and references. Long-term commitment and dependability are strong values.
- Ukrainian is the state language. The Language Law (2019) requires Ukrainian for consumer service, product information, e-commerce information and advertising (trademarks may stay as registered). Business websites and apps serving Ukraine need a Ukrainian version with no less content that loads by default for users in Ukraine. The Language Commissioner issues warnings, then fines. Russian-only content is both a legal risk and deeply alienating.
- Advertising Law: paid or gifted content must be labelled "реклама"; misleading or unsubstantiated superlatives ("найкращий", "№1") and gender stereotypes are banned; prices for goods offered in Ukraine are in hryvnia, never in the aggressor state's currency.
- Marketing email, SMS, messenger and calls need prior consent, a clearly identified sender and an easy opt-out (Advertising Law, E-commerce Law).
- Patriotic and solidarity messaging works when authentic and backed by real action (donations, support for veterans, local production); opportunistic use backfires.
- Job ads may not set requirements on age, sex, appearance, residence, family status or similar unless the job demands it, or address only men or only women (Law on Employment); use neutral or paired titles (менеджер/менеджерка). Do not ask about nationality, origin or registration.
- CVs: reverse chronological, results with numbers; a photo is optional (professional head shot only); desired salary and city are common. In Ukrainian or English, not Russian.
- Ukrainian IT professionals are used to international norms and fairly direct, task-focused English.

## Humor and emphasis

- Wordplay, irony and dark humor are common among Ukrainians, including about the war, but outsiders should not joke about war, losses, mobilization or air raids. Avoid dehumanising slang for the enemy in professional text.
- Avoid casual military and violent metaphors in marketing ("bomb", "killer feature", "blitz", "attack the market") where they may evoke the war.
- Exclamation marks: sparingly in body text (the salutation "!" is normal).

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Never** say or imply that Ukraine is part of Russia, that Ukrainians and Russians are "one people", or that Ukrainian is a dialect of Russian.
- Write "Ukraine", never "the Ukraine"; "Kyiv", not "Kiev".
- Name the war as Russia's war against Ukraine or the full-scale invasion, not a "conflict" or "crisis", and avoid "both sides" framing and propaganda terms ("separatists", self-proclaimed "republics" as if real).
- Occupied areas (Crimea, parts of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson regions) are temporarily occupied territories of Ukraine; maps show internationally recognised borders.
- Use current place names. Law No. 3005-IX (2023) bans Russian imperial propaganda and drives renaming; hundreds of settlements and streets have new names (327 settlements in 2024 alone). Check current names in addresses and copy.
- Do not use "Great Patriotic War"; in Ukraine it is "the Second World War". Use "країни Балтії", not "Прибалтика".
- Holodomor is recognised in Ukraine as a genocide. Treat it with gravity.
- Avoid praising Russian culture or drawing Ukrainian-Russian cultural parallels.
- Soviet and Nazi symbols are banned (decommunization laws); "Z" and "V" Russian military symbols are illegal to promote and deeply offensive.
- Domestic politics, leaders and corruption are contentious, especially when raised by outsiders.
- Stepan Bandera is viewed very differently in Ukraine than in Poland or Russia; take no position in content for mixed Ukrainian-Polish audiences.
- LGBT topics: attitudes are improving, especially among younger urban people, but still mixed.
- Personal data: the 2010 data protection law still applied at last check, with a GDPR-style replacement in progress; check current status and apply GDPR-level consent.
- Disability language: "людина з інвалідністю", not "інвалід"; relevant with many wounded veterans.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Blue and yellow are national colors; the tryzub is the coat of arms. Ads may not use state symbols disrespectfully.
- Sunflowers, vyshyvanka and wheat fields are positive national imagery; avoid kitsch.
- Even-numbered flowers are for funerals; give odd numbers. The "OK" hand sign and the "fig" gesture can be rude.
- Imagery of destruction, the military or the wounded must not be used for commercial purposes.
- Ukrainian media often write "росія" in lowercase as a stance; foreign brands should use standard "Росія" unless the client's style says otherwise.

## Variation within the culture

- Western Ukraine (Lviv) is predominantly Ukrainian-speaking and more traditional; "пане/пані" has long been common there.
- Kyiv, Dnipro, Kharkiv and Odesa were historically more Russian-speaking; many people have switched to Ukrainian since 2022. The choice is personal, and spoken language is not a proxy for political views.
- Large internally displaced and diaspora populations (Poland, Germany, Czechia, UK, Canada, US). Diaspora content may need sensitivity to the host country.
- IT and startups are informal and English-friendly; state bodies, banks and traditional industry stay formal.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style, with risky wording):

> Our killer new feature will blow up your sales in Kiev and the rest of the CIS market!

**After** (tailored for Ukrainian readers):

> The new feature helps sales teams in Kyiv and across Ukraine process orders 25% faster. We've tested it with three Ukrainian retailers over the last six months.

**Why:** Violent metaphors evoke the war, "Kiev" and "CIS" signal a Russian-centric frame, and hype reads as insincere. Ukrainian readers respond to concrete, locally proven results.
