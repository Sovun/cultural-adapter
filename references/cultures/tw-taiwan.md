# Taiwan

## In brief

Taiwanese professional readers expect polite, title-based address, a short and clearly organised email, and negatives that arrive cushioned rather than stated flatly. Text must be written in Traditional characters with Taiwan Mandarin vocabulary: Simplified Chinese, machine-converted mainland copy or a Hong Kong version all read as foreign. Dates and deadlines are taken seriously, and official documents use the Minguo (ROC) year. Taiwan's name and status are sensitive: never label Taiwan as part of China or reuse mainland-facing copy. Hiring, advertising and data rules are strict and enforced with real fines.

## Voice and tone

- Concise, courteous and practical. Native email guides favour three short paragraphs, few adjectives and no rambling backstory; a phone call replaces a long email.
- Write zh-TW natively, never convert from Simplified. Taiwan forms readers expect: 影片 (not 視頻, video), 品質 (not 質量, quality), 訊息/資訊 (not 信息), 軟體 (not 軟件), 水準 (not 水平), 回饋 (not 反饋), 預設 (not 默認), 早安 (not 早上好). Mainland internet slang and forced-intimacy terms (家人們, 小姐姐) irritate many readers.
- Hong Kong Traditional Chinese is a different variant with Cantonese vocabulary; do not ship one version for both.
- Product and UI copy: 您 for singular "you", 你們 for plural; semi-formal and friendly. Do not translate every "your" as 您的 or "you may want to" as 您可能想要; use 建議您 for advice.
- Full-width Chinese punctuation in running text (，。：「」); mixing in half-width commas looks careless.

## Addressing people

- Chinese names put the family name first (陳怡君 = Chen Yi-chun). Romanisation often follows older systems (Chang, Hsu, Lin) rather than mainland pinyin; copy the person's own spelling and never "correct" it.
- Many professionals use an English given name at work (Susan Huang) and their Chinese name in Chinese text. Do not use a bare Chinese given name: it is for family and close friends.
- Default in Chinese: surname + title + 您好 (王經理您好, 林小姐您好, 陳教授您好). Titles such as 經理, 協理, 主任, 組長, 專員 work as names. A colleague's full name + 您好 can read as cold or rude.
- Groups: 各位好, 各位同仁 (internal), 大家好. Name or thank the most senior person first.
- English from Taiwan often opens "Dear all" or "Dears"; these are local habits, not rudeness. In English to Taiwanese readers, "Dear Mr Chen" suits clients and seniors; "Hi Susan" once on first-name terms.
- Closings: a thanks line such as 感謝您撥冗閱讀 (thank you for your time) or 期待您的回覆 (I look forward to your reply), then the name. Classical blessings such as 敬祝 商祺 (wishing your business prosperity) still appear in formal mail, but many modern emails skip them. A bare 以上 (that's all) can feel curt to clients.
- Signature: Chinese and English name, title, company, phone with extension, email; a LINE ID is acceptable for ongoing business.

## Structuring a message

1. Specific subject line: topic, project, date (e.g. 【報價確認】A 專案 10/25 前回覆).
2. Title-based greeting and one courtesy line.
3. First paragraph: why you are writing and what it means for the reader.
4. Details in short paragraphs or bullets; separate topics under bold subheadings. Name and describe each attachment, with a date or version in the file name.
5. Close with thanks, the requested action and a reply date, plus an offer to answer questions (若有任何問題，歡迎隨時與我聯繫).

- After meetings send a recap of decisions and owners, framed as routine, not distrust.
- Do not demand a decision on the spot; approvals often go through seniors after the meeting.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with a reason, regret and an alternative ("這次恐怕較難配合，或許我們可以…" - this time it may be hard to accommodate; perhaps we could...). Avoid flat "不行" or "不可能" to outsiders.
- Never criticise in a group thread or widen the CC list on a complaint. Describe the issue, not the person, and follow up written criticism by phone or in person.
- Read soft signals: "我們再研究看看" (we'll look into it further), slow replies or silence on a point may mean no or "needs internal discussion". Use open questions that let the reader say no without losing face.
- Ultimatums and early hard deadlines in a negotiation damage trust.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Price weighs heavily, then quality and after-sales service; volume discounts and short-term payback persuade more than lifetime-cost arguments.
- All marketing, labels and instructions must be in Traditional Chinese; imported goods need Chinese labels at least as complete as in the origin country.
- Consumer channels: LINE (official accounts, coupons, stickers), Facebook, Instagram, YouTube; Google for search, so research keywords in zh-TW. Copy and layout must be mobile-first.
- Local wordplay, including homophone puns that mix Mandarin and Taiwanese Hokkien, is popular in ads; it rarely survives translation. Obvious paid posts on forums are distrusted.
- Ads: no false or misleading claims about price, quality or origin, and no disparaging competitors (Fair Trade Act); advertised content becomes a minimum the seller must deliver (Consumer Protection Act); online and distance sales carry a 7-day no-reason return. Food may not claim medical effects.
- Data and email marketing: state who collects data, why and how it is used; stop marketing on objection, and the first marketing contact must explain how to opt out (Personal Data Protection Act).
- CV: a one-page 履歷 plus a separate 自傳 (narrative self-introduction, about 800-1,000 characters). A professional head-and-shoulders photo is customary on local job platforms; multinationals may not want one. Quantified results beat adjectives like 細心; state the target role and an expected salary.
- Job ads: no conditions on age, sex, marital status, appearance, height, birthplace, zodiac sign, blood type or other protected grounds (Employment Service Act; Gender Equality in Employment Act). Youth-coded wording such as 熱血青年 has been fined as age discrimination. Posts paying under NT$40,000 a month must show the pay range.

## Humor and emphasis

- Keep humour out of first contacts and formal mail; sarcasm and irony misfire.
- Emphasis in email: bold or one restrained colour, not capitals, several "!!" or emoji. With close contacts, ～ or ^^ is fine.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Taiwan's name and status:** never write "Taiwan, Province of China", 中國台灣 or "China (Taiwan)", and never list Taiwan under China in country pickers or maps. Such labels draw organised complaints and official protests. List 台灣 / Taiwan on its own and use 國家/地區 (country/region) for location fields.
- **Mainland-facing copy:** forms required for mainland audiences (e.g. "Taiwan, China") offend Taiwanese readers. Keep Taiwan and mainland content fully separate.
- **Identity and cross-strait politics:** labels such as "Chinese" versus "Taiwanese", and 中國 versus 大陸 for the PRC, are read as political statements. In commercial text use 台灣 and 中國 plainly, call people Taiwanese (台灣人) unless they self-describe otherwise, and take no side on unification or independence. Blue and green used as political signals can read as partisan (KMT and DPP camps).
- **Respectful terms:** 移工 not 外勞 for migrant workers; 身心障礙人士 not 殘障人士; avoid 大陸妹 entirely.
- **Health data:** health, medical and criminal records are special categories; do not ask for them in forms or job applications without a legal basis.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice; check the current text of each law.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Many readers avoid 4 (sounds like "death") in prices, product counts and floor numbers in promotions.
- Clocks, scissors, umbrellas and white chrysanthemums carry death or separation associations; keep them out of gift promotions.
- Traditional owners may avoid Ghost Month (seventh lunar month, around August) for launches, moves and property sales.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | 2026年10月18日 (long); 2026/10/18 (short); weekday: 2026/10/18（日）. Official documents, invoices and tax forms use the Minguo year: Gregorian minus 1911, so 2026 = 民國115年 (115/10/18). |
| Time | 12-hour with the period word first: 上午9:30, 下午3:00; 24-hour (15:00) common in schedules and transport. |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89. In prose, large figures group by 萬 (10^4) and 億 (10^8): 1.2 million = 120萬. |
| Currency | NT$1,200 or 新台幣1,200元; $1,200 locally; TWD internationally. Prices usually without decimals; laws write 新臺幣. |
| Week starts | Sunday in calendars; weekdays 星期一…星期日, short 週一. |
| Phone | +886 2 1234 5678 (Taipei, area code 02 domestically); mobile 0912-345-678, internationally +886 912 345 678. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Languages:** written business text is Mandarin in Traditional characters. Taiwanese Hokkien is widely spoken, especially in the south and among older people, and appears in ads and puns; Hakka and Indigenous languages matter in some regions and in public-sector content. Audio and video may need Hokkien.
- **Generations:** younger professionals use English names, "Hi + name" and LINE stickers with managers, and pick up some mainland terms from short-video apps; older readers prefer Chinese names, full titles and object more strongly to mainland vocabulary. Some older businesspeople also read Japanese.
- **Sectors:** tech, startups and foreign firms are more relaxed; manufacturing, finance, family-owned firms and government keep formal titles and slower, senior-led decisions.
- **Regions:** Taipei is most international; Taichung, Tainan, Kaohsiung and rural areas lean more traditional and more Hokkien-speaking.
- **Identity:** views on identity and cross-strait ties split by generation and party, so the same China-related phrasing can please one reader and offend another.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt, task-only English style):

> Hi Kevin, we can't hit the 15 Nov delivery. The supplier messed up again. New date is 29 Nov. Let me know if that's a problem!!

**After** (for a Taiwanese client contact, in Chinese with gloss):

> 林經理您好：
>
> 感謝您一直以來的支持。關於 A 專案的出貨時程，因上游料件到貨延遲，原訂 11 月 15 日的交期恐怕無法如期完成，造成您的不便，我們深感抱歉。
>
> 目前我們已安排加班趕工，最新預計交期為 11 月 29 日，並會每週五回報進度。若此時程對貴公司安排有影響，我們也可先分批出貨，煩請於 10 月 25 日前告知您的意見。
>
> 若有任何問題，歡迎隨時與我聯繫。感謝您的體諒。
>
> 王小明 敬上

*(Gloss: "Hello Manager Lin, thank you for your continued support. Regarding the Project A shipment, a delay in upstream parts means the original 15 November delivery date unfortunately cannot be met; we sincerely apologise for the inconvenience. We have scheduled overtime; the new estimated delivery is 29 November, with progress updates every Friday. If this affects your plans, we can also ship in partial lots; please let us know by 25 October. Feel free to contact me with any questions. Thank you for your understanding. Wang Xiaoming")*

**Why:** The delay is still stated plainly in the first paragraph, but the blame on the supplier becomes a neutral cause, the writer apologises and owns the fix, and the reader gets an exact new date, a reporting rhythm, an alternative and a reply deadline. Title + surname, full-width punctuation and a calm close replace the casual "Hi" and "!!".
