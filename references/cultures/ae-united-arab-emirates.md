# United Arab Emirates

## In brief

The UAE has two readerships. Emirati citizens, roughly a tenth of residents, run government and semi-government bodies and expect Arabic or courteous formal English, correct titles and no public criticism. The expatriate majority (South Asian, Arab, Filipino, Western) works in plain international English and moves faster. Both write inside strict law: media content standards, an online insult and false-news regime, a protected national flag and Arabic as the language of government. Do not reuse Saudi or Qatari copy unchanged: the UAE works Monday to Friday, runs on UTC+4 and licenses alcohol more widely. Ambitious "world's first" framing is part of the national style, but every claim must be true.

## Voice and tone

- Respectful, polished and calm. Start formal and relax only when the reader's replies do; "Hi" or "Hey" to a first contact reads as careless.
- Formal Arabic is Modern Standard Arabic. Emirati dialect fits social posts and ads aimed at nationals, not letters or contracts.
- Arabic should be written or transcreated by native writers. Literal translation repeats "your" on every noun and sounds stiff; use plural or both-gender forms for generic people in job ads and UI.
- English is often a lingua franca between second-language readers: short sentences, no idioms, slang or jargon.
- Official messaging stresses tolerance, national vision and achievement; echo it lightly, not as flattery.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name, "bin" or "bint" (son or daughter of) plus the father's name, then the family name, often with "Al" (Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum). Use title plus given name ("Dr Ahmed", "Mr Khalid"); the family name alone can sound odd.
- "Sheikh" / "Sheikha" marks members of ruling families, not any senior man. Rulers and crown princes are "His Highness" (سمو). Ministers and senior officials are "His/Her Excellency" (سعادة, or معالي for ministers); check the right form before writing.
- English: "Dear Mr Ahmed", "Dear Dr Fatima"; first names are normal among expats after contact. When writing to several people, put the most senior first.
- Arabic letter: "السيد/ أحمد ... المحترم" (Mr Ahmed, respected), then "السلام عليكم ورحمة الله وبركاته" (peace and God's mercy be upon you) and "تحية طيبة وبعد" (kind greetings, and then...).
- Closings: "تقبلوا مني فائق الاحترام والتقدير" (please accept my highest respect and appreciation) for formal letters; "مع خالص التحية" (with sincere greetings) for everyday mail. In English, "Kind regards".
- Signatures: full name, title and company; adding your name in Arabic helps.
- Do not ask about a counterpart's wife or daughters; "I hope your family is well" is fine.

## Structuring a message

1. Greeting and one courtesy line (wellbeing, thanks for a meeting).
2. Purpose and request, kept brief for busy senior readers; a clear subject line.
3. Facts and figures, with detail in attachments.
4. A next step framed as an invitation, and good wishes.

- Cold email should aim for a meeting or call, not a close. Follow up by phone or WhatsApp; send a recap after meetings.
- Never push with deadline pressure or "ASAP"; hard selling reads as disrespect.
- Linking a proposal to national strategies (diversification, AI, net zero) can show relevance.
- Mail to ministries, municipalities and semi-government bodies goes in Arabic or bilingual; Arabic is their official language of correspondence and documents filed with them need an Arabic version.
- Schedule around prayer times (avoid Friday midday), Ramadan and the Monday to Friday week.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Never criticise or correct someone in front of others: no critical reply-all, no copying seniors to shame a colleague.
- Offer alternatives and questions instead of a flat no ("Could we also look at...?"); let senior people keep face.
- Silence or a vague answer can mean hesitation; follow up gently.
- Online insult and defamation are crimes under the cybercrime law (Federal Decree-Law 34/2021), and publishing harmful information can be an offence even when it is true. A factual, measured review of your own experience is generally fine; insults, exaggeration and "name and shame" posts are not.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Family, hospitality, premium quality, innovation and ambition resonate. Superlatives work only if they are accurate; consumer law punishes misleading ads, and unverified health claims have been prosecuted.
- **Media content standards** (Federal Decree-Law 55/2023) apply to all ads and content, paid or not: respect for religion, the state, rulers, symbols, national unity, foreign relations, public morals and privacy. Ads must be distinguishable from editorial. Fines run high; enforcement dates and minimum fines vary between reports, so check current status. The regulator has been called the UAE Media Council and, more recently, the National Media Authority: check current status.
- Individuals posting promotional content (influencers, affiliates, even unpaid) need an Advertiser Permit.
- **Flag and emblems:** the flag may not be used in advertising, on products, food or costumes, or with logos added (Federal Decree-Law 13/2019). Dubai and federal emblems need permission. AI images of national symbols or public figures need prior approval. Do not dress non-Emirati models in kandura or abaya.
- Imagery: no immodest dress (cover shoulders and knees), public affection, pork or gambling; alcohol only in clearly licensed contexts (it is banned in Sharjah); no LGBT themes in public campaigns.
- Product information and prices should be in Arabic first, then other languages. Mass campaigns usually run in parallel Arabic and English, sometimes South Asian languages too.
- Ramadan: generosity, family, iftar and reflection; avoid daytime food and drink imagery. Greetings ("Ramadan Kareem", "Eid Mubarak") and National Day (2 December) messages are expected in B2B relationships; Commemoration Day (30 November) is solemn.
- Marketing email and SMS need consent and a working opt-out (Consumer Protection Law, PDPL).
- **Job ads:** no nationality or gender preference (Federal Decree-Law 33/2021), but "UAE nationals only" roles are lawful. Candidates may not be charged recruitment fees, and title and pay must match the offer. Do not cite Emiratisation benefits in ads without ministry permission.
- **Emiratisation:** at last check, private firms with 50+ employees must raise the Emirati share of skilled jobs by 2 points a year (half by 30 June, half by 31 December), with a fine per missed position; Nafis is the national jobs platform. Rules for smaller firms: check current status.
- CVs: one to two pages, factual measurable results; nationality, visa status and notice period near the top. A photo is optional unless the ad asks; multinationals often prefer none.

## Humor and emphasis

- Leave humour out of first contacts; keep it light later. No jokes about religion, rulers, the state, national symbols, families, women or other nationalities (strife between communities is a banned content category).
- Emphasise with structure and bold, not capitals or exclamation marks. Swearing or insulting gestures in messages can count as online insult.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **State and religion:** criticism or insult of the rulers, the government, national symbols, Islam or other religions is illegal and enforced online.
- **False news:** spreading false or unofficial information that harms public order, the economy or the currency is a crime. Quote official statistics.
- **Regional politics:** Israel-Palestine, Iran, Yemen and Gulf rivalries are risky; stay neutral.
- **Morals:** same-sex relations and cross-dressing are illegal; public affection can lead to arrest.
- **Privacy:** get consent before publishing photos or details of identifiable people, especially women and families.
- **Charity:** unlicensed fundraising, including online appeals, is illegal.
- **Geography:** the UAE says "Arabian Gulf". Abu Musa and the Greater and Lesser Tunbs are disputed with Iran; do not map them as Iranian.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- National colours red, green, white and black; show the flag correctly and never torn, faded or altered.
- Right hand for giving, receiving and eating; no shoe soles or pointing fingers in imagery. Dogs are a poor choice for mainstream imagery.
- Falcons, Arabic coffee, dates, the desert and heritage architecture are positive symbols.
- Tolerance is an official value: respectful greetings for other faiths' festivals are fine. No proselytising and no decorative use of Quranic text.

## Variation within the culture

- **Dubai:** cosmopolitan, commercial, fast-moving and most liberal in lifestyle.
- **Abu Dhabi:** the capital, with many government and state-linked investment bodies, so more formal protocol.
- **Sharjah and the northern emirates** (Ajman, Umm Al Quwain, Ras Al Khaimah, Fujairah): more conservative; Sharjah bans alcohol and expects stricter dress.
- **Government vs private:** government and semi-government readers expect Arabic and protocol; free-zone multinationals expect efficient English. DIFC and ADGM have their own data and employment rules.
- **Emirati jobseekers** are a distinct, regulated audience reached through Nafis. Emirati women are most national graduates and much of the public-sector workforce; do not assume male readers.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The reader is identified: Emirati or government (Arabic or bilingual, formal) versus expat business (plain international English).
- [ ] Titles are right: Sheikh/Sheikha only for ruling families, Highness, Excellency, Dr, Eng.; title plus given name.
- [ ] One courtesy line precedes business; no hard sell, ultimatum or "ASAP".
- [ ] Criticism and refusals are private and framed as options; no public naming and shaming online.
- [ ] No criticism or jokes about rulers, government, symbols, religion or other nationalities; no regional politics.
- [ ] No flag on products or ads, no emblem without permission, no AI images of leaders or symbols, no kandura on non-Emiratis.
- [ ] Imagery is modest; no public affection, LGBT themes or gambling; alcohol only where licensed and never for Sharjah.
- [ ] Claims, statistics and health benefits are verifiable; ads are labelled; influencers hold an Advertiser Permit.
- [ ] Arabic is native-written MSA, RTL-checked; government mail and product information are in Arabic.
- [ ] Job ads have no nationality or gender preference and no candidate fees; Emiratisation wording is permitted.
- [ ] Photos of identifiable people and marketing messages have consent.
- [ ] Dates day-first, AED, UTC+4, Monday to Friday week; Ramadan, Eid and National Day respected.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (Western nightlife promo for a UAE-wide audience):

> Ladies' night! Free drinks, bikini pool party, kiss at midnight for good luck.

**After** (tailored for a UAE audience):

> Weekend sunset sessions at the pool: live music, signature mocktails and a family brunch on Saturday. Book your table now.

**Why:** Public affection can lead to arrest, and free-alcohol and bikini framing breaches public-morals standards for a broad audience (alcohol is banned outright in Sharjah). Family leisure on the Saturday-Sunday weekend works for Emirati and expat readers alike.
