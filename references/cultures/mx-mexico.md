# Mexico

## In brief

Mexican business communication is warm, courteous, relationship-driven and hierarchical. A blunt "no" is considered impolite, so refusals are wrapped ("let me see what I can do"). Professional titles (Licenciado, Ingeniero) are part of correct address. Mexicans are proud of their culture and history and sensitive to stereotypes about drugs, violence, migration and laziness. Phone calls and face-to-face contact often matter more than email; written follow-ups confirm what was agreed.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous, warm and personal. Formal politeness markers ("con gusto", "le agradecería mucho") are common.
- Use Mexican Spanish (es-MX), not Spain Spanish (es-ES): "computadora" not "ordenador", "celular" not "móvil", "ustedes" not "vosotros". Neutral Latin American Spanish is acceptable for pan-regional content, but Spain-specific forms stand out.
- Diminutives (-ito/-ita) soften requests and sound friendly: "un momentito", "un favorcito".
- Avoid vulgarity; some words (estúpido, güey, references to someone's mother) are much stronger than English equivalents.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s), father's surname, mother's surname (Héctor González López). Use the first surname alone in short form (Sr. González), never the second alone.
- **Professional titles** are used in writing and speech: Licenciado/Licenciada (Lic.) for university graduates in law, business and many fields; Ingeniero/a (Ing.); Arquitecto/a (Arq.); Contador/a Público (C.P.); Doctor/a (Dr./Dra.); Maestro/a (Mtro./Mtra.) for master's degree holders. Using the right title is a mark of respect.
- Email salutation: "Estimado Lic. González:" / "Estimada Ing. Ramírez:" (a colon after the salutation is standard in Spanish letters). Less formal: "Hola, Mariana:" or "Buen día, Mariana,".
- "Don/Doña" + first name for older, respected people.
- Sign-offs: "Saludos cordiales," "Quedo a sus órdenes," (I remain at your service; very common), "Atentamente," (formal).
- Always use "usted" with authorities, elders, clients and seniors unless invited to use "tú".

## Structuring a message

- Courteous greeting and a personal line ("Espero que se encuentre muy bien").
- Context and relationship, then the request, framed as a favor or collaboration.
- Explain how the proposal benefits the organization, the community and the person.
- Close with availability and warmth; offer a call.
- Summarize phone and meeting agreements by email.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Never criticize anyone publicly or in earshot of others. Deliver bad news privately and gently.
- Refusals: "Déjeme revisarlo", "Veré qué puedo hacer", "Lo vemos más adelante". When writing for Mexican readers, soften refusals and offer alternatives.
- Subordinates may not tell superiors that a deadline is unrealistic; ask open questions and check progress.
- Passion and emotion in discussion indicate commitment, not unprofessionalism.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Family, celebration, national pride, faith, humor and personal benefit are strong motivators.
- PROFECO (consumer protection agency) enforces truthful advertising; prices must include taxes (IVA). Food and beverages carry mandatory black octagon warning labels (NOM-051) and restrictions on marketing to children.
- Show pride in Mexican culture authentically; avoid clichés (sombreros, cacti, piñatas, tequila) in corporate content, and never use the Day of the Dead superficially.
- Use "estadounidense" rather than "americano" for US people in Spanish; Mexicans are also Americans in the continental sense.
- CV: one to two pages; photo is common, along with age in some sectors, although anti-discrimination law prohibits discriminatory hiring. Degrees and titles feature prominently.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor (albur wordplay, teasing) is part of social life; avoid albur and double entendre in business writing as it can be crude.
- Dark humor about death is culturally specific (Día de Muertos) and should not be imitated by outsiders.
- Exclamation marks use the opening ¡ and closing !; same for questions (¿?). Omitting the opening mark looks careless.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Stereotypes**: do not associate Mexicans with drug trafficking, violence, laziness or illegal migration. Use "undocumented migrants", not "illegals".
- The Virgin of Guadalupe is sacred to many; never treat her irreverently.
- Do not imply Mexico is lawless or that bribery is normal.
- US-Mexico relations (border wall, deportations, tariffs, the 1846 to 1848 war and lost territories) are sensitive.
- Class and skin color: avoid imagery that reinforces whiteness as aspirational.
- Indigenous peoples (over 60 languages): avoid using indigenous dress or symbols as costume; cultural appropriation of designs has led to official complaints against fashion brands.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Marigolds (cempasúchil) are associated with Day of the Dead and the deceased; yellow flowers can signal death in some contexts, red flowers can cast spells in folk belief.
- Green, white and red national colors and the eagle on a cactus are patriotic symbols; the national flag and coat of arms have legal protections against commercial misuse.
- The "OK" and other gestures differ regionally; a "come here" gesture is palm down.

## Variation within the culture

- Mexico City (CDMX) is cosmopolitan and liberal; Monterrey is industrial, business-driven and more direct; Guadalajara is more traditional; southern states (Oaxaca, Chiapas, Yucatán) have strong indigenous cultures.
- Northern border regions are culturally closer to the US.
- Large multinationals and maquiladoras mix US and Mexican norms.
- Younger urban professionals use "tú" and first names more readily.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (direct US-style):

> Hi Carlos, we can't accept your price. Send a new quote by tomorrow.

**After** (tailored for a Mexican reader, in Spanish with gloss):

> Estimado Lic. Herrera:
>
> Le agradezco mucho la cotización que nos envió. La revisamos con mucho interés; sin embargo, por ahora el monto está un poco por encima del presupuesto que tenemos asignado para este proyecto. ¿Sería posible revisar juntos algunas opciones para ajustarla? Con gusto le llamo mañana para comentarlo.
>
> Quedo a sus órdenes.

*(Gloss: "Dear Mr Herrera (Lic.): Thank you very much for the quote. We reviewed it with great interest; however, for now the amount is somewhat above our budget for this project. Could we review some options together to adjust it? I would be happy to call you tomorrow to discuss. I remain at your service.")*

**Why:** The title, warm tone, softened refusal ("un poco por encima") and offer to talk preserve the relationship while still communicating the price problem.
