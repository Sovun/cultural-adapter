# South Africa

## In brief

South African business runs in English, but for most readers English is a second or third language, so plain, concrete wording beats idiom and corporate jargon. The default tone is clear and friendly with a courteous opener: start with a title and surname, then follow the reader's lead to first names. Pin down dates, owners and terms in writing, because timing words and polite agreement are often looser than they look. Race, apartheid history, land and transformation are present in everyday public life; use current terms precisely, never joke about them, and show the country's diversity rather than one group. Consumer law, the advertising code and direct-marketing consent rules are detailed and enforced.

## Voice and tone

- Plain English, short sentences, everyday verbs. Many readers think in isiZulu, isiXhosa, Afrikaans, Sepedi or another language first; idioms, sports slang and puns can block meaning. Consumer notices and agreements must by law be understandable to an ordinary reader with average literacy.
- British spelling (colour, organisation, programme, licence as a noun). American spelling and US vocabulary read as imported.
- Warm but businesslike. Pressure tactics, flashy hype and "act now" closes tend to backfire; patient, win-win framing works.
- Do not imitate local slang or accents ("Howzit", "lekker", "eish"). Locals use them with friends and in ads; from an outsider they sound forced or mocking.
- Decode local English: "must" can be a soft "should" ("Must I send it?"), "just now" means later, "robot" is a traffic light, "bakkie" a pickup.

## Addressing people

- Name order is given name, then surname. Open with "Dear Mr Naidoo," or "Dear Dr Mokoena," for first contact; "Good morning Thabo," is a common, friendly opener once you have spoken. Titles take no full stop; keep Dr and Prof.
- Switch to first names when the reader signs with theirs or uses yours. Starting slightly too formal is safer than too familiar, especially with older readers and public bodies.
- Greet people individually in meetings and calls; in group email, avoid "guys" and use "Hi all" or "Dear colleagues".
- Afrikaans: "Geagte mnr. Botha" (formal letter), "Beste Annelie" (normal email); close with "Vriendelike groete" (kind regards). Formal "u" versus informal "jy" depends on age and closeness; many readers now find blanket "u" stiff, and app copy uses "jy". Older Afrikaans speakers in small towns may be called "Oom" (uncle) or "Tannie" (aunt) in speech; do not use these in business writing.
- Sign-offs: "Kind regards," (safe), "Regards," (brisk), "Thanks," (everyday).

## Structuring a message

1. A specific subject line; for public-sector applications, include the post reference number.
2. Salutation and one courtesy line.
3. Purpose and request early, in plain words.
4. Detail in short paragraphs or bullets; numbers and practical outcomes rather than vision.
5. Next step with owner, exact date and time (SAST), then thanks and sign-off.

- After a meeting, send a short written summary of decisions and deadlines within a day or two.
- Move sensitive or complex matters to a call or meeting and confirm the outcome by email. A warm introduction through a respected contact helps a cold approach.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagree with the idea, not the person: "I see the logic; another option could be...". Keep criticism private; a correction in a group thread embarrasses the whole team.
- Frame feedback forward: what to change next time, with a clear goal and date, or as a question ("How could we tighten this section?").
- A refusal gets a reason and, where possible, an alternative. Hard bargaining from an inflated opening and ultimatums damage trust.
- In traditional organisations and the public sector, juniors seldom contradict a senior in writing; ask for views directly rather than reading silence as consent.
- Watch for tone that could sound condescending, especially toward women, younger colleagues or second-language speakers; such remarks are noticed and can escalate.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Evidence over adjectives: local clients, figures, guarantees, professional registrations. Satirical, topical humour is a strong local ad tradition; the target must be the powerful, never a race, language group or gender.
- Advertising code: every factual claim needs documentary proof before the ad runs; opinion-style puffery and obvious exaggeration are allowed. Comparisons must be fair, like-for-like and not disparaging. "Free" only if nothing but delivery is paid; "new" for at most 12 months; a price reduction may run for at most three months. Copying another ad's concept, including a foreign one, is restricted. Testimonials must be genuine and dated; paid and influencer posts need a clear label such as #AD.
- Consumer Protection Act: no misleading claims or bait prices; quoted prices include VAT (15%) and unavoidable costs; "you'll be signed up unless you decline" offers are unlawful; competitions need full entry terms and closing date.
- Direct marketing (POPIA): email, SMS, calls and DMs to non-customers need prior opt-in consent, and you may ask only once. Existing customers may get offers for similar products if they could opt out at collection and in every message. Each message names the sender and gives an opt-out. Buying lead lists does not remove these duties.
- CV: usually two pages (three for senior or government roles), opening profile, reverse chronological, quantified achievements, languages with level, driver's licence code, NQF levels and professional registrations, two or three referees with contact details. Leave out photo, race, religion and marital status unless asked; add the ID number only if the ad or form requests it.
- Job ads: Employment Equity law lets employers state a preference for suitably qualified candidates from designated groups (black people, meaning African, Coloured and Indian, plus women and people with disabilities) in line with their employment equity plan. This is lawful here, unlike in most countries. Excluding everyone else outright ("only black applicants") is not; courts have struck such exclusions down. Sector targets apply to larger employers since 2025. Treat this as a pointer and check the employer's EE plan and current rules.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour and banter are welcome once a relationship exists; with strangers keep it light.
- Never joke about race, apartheid, crime, poverty, accents or other African countries.
- Emphasis through bold or a clear sentence, not capitals or "!!".

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Race terms:** official statistics use Black African, Coloured, White and Indian/Asian. "Coloured" is an official, widely self-used, capitalised term in South Africa; do not swap in a foreign equivalent, though some people reject the label. Use race only when relevant (e.g. EE reporting). Racial slurs have led to criminal convictions and dismissals.
- **Apartheid and its symbols:** do not use apartheid imagery, nostalgia or the pre-1994 flag; a court has found its gratuitous display can be hate speech. Hate speech and discriminatory ads are prohibited.
- **Land, transformation, B-BBEE, crime, party politics, xenophobia:** take no side and do not lecture. Show real B-BBEE commitment rather than a tick-box line; avoid fear-based safety messaging.
- **Languages:** 12 official languages: English, Afrikaans, isiNdebele, isiXhosa, isiZulu, Sepedi, Sesotho, Setswana, siSwati, Tshivenda, Xitsonga and South African Sign Language (added 2023). Government bodies communicate in at least two.
- Ads must not stereotype by gender, exploit children or show cruelty to animals; showing a living person needs their permission. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Cast across race, age, language and economic settings for national campaigns; one-group casting misrepresents the market.
- Avoid "safari Africa" imagery, people as exotic decoration and traditional dress as costume. Show cities, offices and townships realistically.
- Pointing at someone or an inward-palm V-sign reads as rude; some readers see left-hand giving as impolite.
- The braai (barbecue) is a broadly shared social image; wine is a common gift but not for Muslim readers (Cape Malay and Indian Muslim communities).
- Key dates: Freedom Day 27 April, Youth Day 16 June (1976 student uprising), Heritage Day 24 September, Day of Reconciliation 16 December. Christmas falls in summer.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | CLDR short: 2026/03/07; medium 07 Mar 2026; long 07 March 2026. Day-first 07/03/2026 is common. Spell out the month in anything important; never US month-first. |
| Time | 24-hour (14:30) in writing; 2:30 pm in informal use. SAST (UTC+2), no daylight saving. |
| Numbers | CLDR and official style: 1 234 567,89. Business, banking and retail often use a decimal point: 1 234 567.89. Use a space for thousands, one decimal style per document, and follow the client's house style. |
| Currency | R1 234,50 (CLDR) or R1 234.50; no space after R. ZAR in international text. VAT 15% included in consumer prices. |
| Percent | 15% (no space). |
| Week starts | CLDR: Sunday. Many business calendars start Monday; give dates, not "week 12". |
| Phone | +27 11 234 5678 (landline); 082 123 4567 (mobile); 10 digits nationally, starting with 0. |
| Address | 27 Main Road / Rondebosch / Cape Town / 7700 (street, suburb, city, 4-digit postcode). |
| Other | Metric (km, litres, fuel in km per litre). Business slows mid-December to mid-January and around Easter. Driver's licences by code (Code B, EB). |

## Variation within the culture

- **Language communities:** isiZulu (about a quarter of homes) and isiXhosa are the largest home languages, then Afrikaans, Sepedi and English. Most people speak several. Consumer campaigns gain from correct local-language taglines or greetings ("Sawubona" isiZulu, "Molo" isiXhosa, "Dumela" Sesotho/Setswana, "Goeie môre" Afrikaans); machine-translated African-language copy is risky, so use native translators.
- **Black African communities:** respect for age and seniority, consultation and community framing (ubuntu, "a person is a person through other people") often matter more, especially in traditional and rural settings. Ubuntu as an outsider's slogan can feel appropriative.
- **Afrikaans speakers:** Afrikaans is a home language for many Coloured as well as white South Africans; do not equate the language with one group. Use correct "u"/"jy" register.
- **Coloured and Indian communities:** concentrated in the Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal respectively; Indian South African business is often described as relationship-oriented. Include them in "diverse" casting rather than a Black/white binary.
- **Urban and rural:** Johannesburg is fast and results-driven, Cape Town somewhat more relaxed; small towns and rural areas keep more formal address and elder respect.
- **Generations and sectors:** younger, urban and tech readers are informal and quick to first names; government, law and older readers expect titles and formal process (forms, certified copies, reference numbers).

## Review checklist

- [ ] Plain English for second-language readers: no idioms, puns or slang; British spelling.
- [ ] First contact uses title and surname; register follows the reader's lead afterwards.
- [ ] One courtesy line, then purpose; next step has owner, exact date and SAST time.
- [ ] Commitments written out; no "soon", "now now" or "sometime next week".
- [ ] Criticism targets the work, stays private and offers a way forward.
- [ ] No pressure closes, fake deadlines or inflated opening offers.
- [ ] Factual claims are provable; "free", "new", "up to" and discounts follow the ad code; influencer posts labelled.
- [ ] Consumer prices in rand, including VAT and unavoidable costs.
- [ ] Marketing email, SMS or calls go only to opted-in contacts or eligible customers, with sender and opt-out.
- [ ] Race terms are current and used only when relevant; "Coloured" capitalised and not replaced.
- [ ] No apartheid imagery, old flag, racial jokes, crime fear or "safari Africa" clichés.
- [ ] National imagery reflects diverse races, languages and settings.
- [ ] Job ads express EE preference, never outright exclusion; no age, gender or appearance wording.
- [ ] CVs: about two pages, referees with contacts, no photo or race unless requested.
- [ ] Dates use a spelled-out month or day-first; one decimal style throughout.
- [ ] Campaigns and deadlines avoid mid-December to mid-January.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (fast US-style sales follow-up):

> Hey Sipho! Our AI-powered platform is the #1 solution for retailers worldwide. Lock in 30% off by tomorrow or miss out. Let's jump on a call ASAP!!

**After** (tailored for a South African retail operations head after one call):

> Dear Mr Ndlovu,
>
> Thank you for your time on Tuesday; I trust the stocktake went well. As promised, I attach a one-page summary of how our system cut stock-outs by 18% for a grocery chain with 40 stores in Gauteng.
>
> The 30% introductory discount we discussed is valid until Friday 27 November 2026, so there is time to involve your team. Would a 30-minute call on Thursday 12 November at 10:00 SAST suit you?
>
> Kind regards,
> Lerato

**Why:** Title and surname fit a first written contact with a senior reader, and the warm opener refers back to the call. A local, measurable result replaces the unprovable "#1" claim, which the ad code would require proof for. The fake one-day deadline becomes a real date with room to consult, and the call request names an exact day, time and time zone.
