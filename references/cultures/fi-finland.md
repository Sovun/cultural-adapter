# Finland

## In brief

Finnish readers want short, honest, matter-of-fact text and take words literally: a vague "let's talk sometime" may get a reply asking for a date. Hype, flattery and forced cheerfulness sound fake; facts and kept promises build trust. "Sinä" and first names are the business default, and the clock is the firmest in the Nordics. Of the Nordic readers Finns are the most reserved: warmth is sparse, positives often go unsaid and a cool first reply is not displeasure. Finland is bilingual (Finnish and Swedish), Nordic rather than Scandinavian, and its marketing, email-consent and recruitment rules are strict and specific.

## Voice and tone

- Matter-of-fact but human, as from one person to another; avoid both stiff officialese and chummy marketing talk. Energy, insurance, banking and public bodies are expected to sound more formal than a small local shop.
- Silence, short replies and a slow answer are not rudeness. Finns work through a question before replying; do not chase.
- Finnish carries politeness in restraint and the conditional mood ("Voisitko...?", could you...?) rather than in a word for "please". Finns' English can read blunt; no rudeness is meant.
- Spelling and grammar signal trustworthiness. English translated word for word sounds stiff in Finnish; use short everyday words.
- On sensitive topics (debt, illness, eviction) direct "sinä" can feel too personal; a passive or general phrasing is gentler.

## Addressing people

- Given name then surname. First names are normal from the first email, including with senior managers, and do not imply friendship.
- Finnish openings: "Hei Mikko," (neutral standard), "Hyvä Mikko Virtanen," (formal letter), "Hyvä asiakas" (customer letter), "Hei kaikki" (group). "Arvoisa" is ceremonial.
- Closings: "Ystävällisin terveisin," (kind regards), "Terveisin," (regards), or a wish like "Hyvää päivän jatkoa!".
- "Sinä" is the default in business, marketing and software. "Te" fits authorities, serious sectors and official matters at first contact; in ads it feels calculated. Many older people themselves prefer "sinä". Never mix the two in one text; passive or zero-person phrasing avoids the choice. Pronouns are not capitalised.
- Swedish-speaking Finns: "Hej Anna," and "Med vänlig hälsning,". "Hei/Hej" works in both languages without guessing the reader's mother tongue.
- English: "Hi Mikko," is fine; "Dear Mr Virtanen" only for a formal first letter to an authority or academic.
- Use gender-neutral job words ("puheenjohtaja", not "puhemies"; "myyjä", not "myyntimies").

## Structuring a message

- Keep a short letter frame (greeting, business, closing, name) but drop warm-up and padding. Purpose in the first sentence; short paragraphs.
- Concrete facts, numbers, owners and dates. Follow-ups list decisions, owners and deadlines and must match what was agreed verbally.
- Silence in a meeting is not agreement: confirm in writing.
- Promise only what you can deliver; if a plan changes, say so early with the new date.
- After hours, send an email rather than call, and do not expect a reply until the next working day.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Feedback is honest, factual and about the task. A plain "no" with a reason works; heavy cushioning reads as evasive.
- Positives often go unsaid, so Finnish feedback can look purely negative to outsiders. Minor mistakes are often not raised at all, and criticism goes privately, not in a group email.
- Instructions in customer text: no "!" for emphasis, no capitals; "Huomaa, että..." (please note that) instead of "Älä...!". Overusing the softener "-han" ("Muistathan...") sounds patronising.
- Ask for clarification without blame: "Anteeksi, en ymmärtänyt, täsmentäisitkö?".
- Rejection letters stay courteous and encourage reapplying; a short job-related reason is better than a set phrase alone.
- When adapting Finnish text for indirect or relationship-first cultures, add the warmth and explicit praise the original leaves out.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Quality, reliability, function, honesty and nature persuade. Realistic offers and plain facts beat vision statements and inflated openers.
- **Claims:** under the Consumer Protection Act and Competition and Consumer Authority (KKV) guidance, measurable claims must be provable, the more absolute the stronger the proof; subjective puffery ("herkullisin") is allowed. Comparisons need a representative group and must disclose material differences. "Ympäristöystävällinen" needs a full life-cycle basis.
- **Ad labelling:** the commercial purpose and advertiser must be clear in all marketing. Paid or gifted influencer posts start with "Mainos" or "Kaupallinen yhteistyö: [company]"; a hashtag alone is not enough.
- **Discounts:** show the lowest price of the previous 30 days next to a reduced price, visibly, not behind a link.
- **Children:** no direct urging to buy and no exploiting inexperience.
- CVs: one to two pages, reverse chronological, two or three quantified results per role, no superlatives or skill bars. Photo optional; leave it out for anonymous recruitment. Hobbies and positions of trust are fine; list references only with their consent.
- Cover letter a little under one page, written for that job. Calling the contact person in the ad before applying is normal.
- Job ads: name real tasks, requirements and salary (most job seekers expect a figure). Clichés like "hyvä tyyppi" or "nuorekas tiimi" read as empty.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour is not a workplace default. Self-irony can land; forced jokes, wordplay and staged enthusiasm alienate. Keep playfulness away from invoices, outages and complaints.
- "!" in moderation: fine in a greeting, a closing wish or an ad headline, not stacked and not on instructions. No capitals for emphasis; emoji belong to chat, not business mail.
- Jokes about Finnish stereotypes (silence, sauna) are safer left to Finns.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Email and SMS marketing:** consumers must opt in first (Act on Electronic Communications Services, s. 200); existing customers may get offers for similar own products with an easy, free opt-out. B2B email is allowed unless the organisation has refused, with an opt-out in every message (s. 202). Marketing must be recognisable as marketing on arrival.
- **Recruitment:** the Non-Discrimination Act bans requiring protected characteristics in job ads without a lawful reason, and the Equality Act bans ads for one sex only. Describe job demands ("Finnish at C1 level", "lifting 10 kg"), not traits ("young", "native speaker", "fully healthy"). Do not ask for health details.
- **Pay transparency:** the EU directive's Finnish bill was still before Parliament at last check, aimed at 2027; check current status before writing pay clauses or job ads.
- **Marketing ethics:** ads must not discriminate by sex, age, origin, language, disability or similar grounds, or violate dignity (Consumer Protection Act ch. 2 s. 2). Stereotyped gender roles draw complaints.
- **Language:** Finnish and Swedish are national languages. Authorities in bilingual municipalities must serve in both, and English-only names for public units have been ruled unlawful. Private firms are freer, but consumers expect Finnish (and Swedish where relevant).
- **History and identity:** Finland is Nordic, not Scandinavian, and was never part of the Soviet Union. Russia, the Winter War and the Continuation War are serious topics, more so since 2022; Finland joined NATO in 2023. Avoid flippant takes.
- **Sámi:** do not use Sámi dress or symbols as costume or decoration in Lapland tourism copy.
- Compulsory school Swedish is a live domestic debate; stay neutral. Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Nature, lakes, forest, the summer cottage (mökki) and sauna are authentic positive imagery; treat sauna with respect, not as a joke. Keep Santa Claus to Lapland tourism.
- Show diverse people in non-stereotyped roles. Flashy wealth and status display clash with Finnish taste.
- Blue and white are the flag colours; use the flag respectfully.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | 7.3.2026 (no leading zeros). Long: 7. maaliskuuta 2026. Months and weekdays lowercase. ISO only in systems. |
| Time | 24-hour with a full stop: 9.30, 14.30. No am/pm. |
| Numbers | 1 234 567,89 (non-breaking space, decimal comma); true minus sign; "milj.", "mrd.". |
| Currency | 1 234,50 € (symbol after, with a space); in running text "39 euroa". EUR codes for banking only. |
| Percent | 25 % (space before the sign). |
| Week starts | Monday; ISO week numbers widely used ("viikko 42", "vk 42"). |
| Phone | puh. 050 123 4567; +358 50 123 4567, Helsinki landline +358 9 123 4567. Spaces, no hyphens. |
| Punctuation | ”…” at both ends; no em dash (spaced en dash); sentence-case headings. CSV list separator is the semicolon. |
| Calendar | July is the main holiday month; avoid launches around Midsummer (juhannus) and Christmas. Metric units. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Swedish speakers:** about 5% of Finns, on the west and south coasts and in Åland (officially Swedish-speaking). Finland-Swedish keeps polite "ni" a little more than Sweden-Swedish.
- **Generations:** older readers are more used to formal address; younger urban readers expect "sinä" and a lighter tone.
- **Sectors:** tech and startups in Helsinki, Espoo, Tampere and Oulu are informal and often work in English; public bodies must write plain, clear Finnish and Swedish.
- **Lapland:** home to the Sámi, with Sámi languages used in public services in the north.
- **Neighbours:** Finnish expectations are not Swedish ones. Finns accept blunter verdicts and a firmer clock than Swedes, and expect less consultation framing.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The point comes first; text is short, factual and keeps a brief greeting and closing.
- [ ] Hype, superlatives and empty compliments are removed; measurable claims can be proved.
- [ ] Promises and times are exact; no vague "sometime" or "whenever".
- [ ] One address form throughout ("sinä" by default); no "Moikka" or emoji from a large firm.
- [ ] Refusals and instructions carry a reason; no "!" or capitals on instructions.
- [ ] Consumer email or SMS marketing has prior opt-in and an easy opt-out.
- [ ] Ads and paid posts are labelled ("Mainos", "Kaupallinen yhteistyö"); discounts show the 30-day lowest price.
- [ ] Job ads list job demands and salary, not age, gender, health or "native speaker".
- [ ] Consumer content is in Finnish (and Swedish where relevant).
- [ ] No confusion with Sweden or Russia; no "former Soviet" or "Scandinavian" framing.
- [ ] Formats: 7.3.2026, 14.30, 1 234,50 €, 25 %, ”quotes”, no em dash.
- [ ] Deadlines and launches avoid July, Midsummer and Christmas.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style enthusiastic follow-up):

> Hi Mikko!! Just checking in to see how you're doing and whether you've had a chance to look at our amazing proposal? We'd love to hop on a quick call whenever works for you!

**After** (for a Finnish reader):

> Hi Mikko, did you have time to review our proposal? If useful, we can go through the pricing in a 20-minute call on Thursday at 10.00.

**Why:** Finnish readers want brevity and specifics. Stacked exclamation marks, "amazing" and an open-ended "whenever" read as insincere; a concrete time and a short purpose read as respect.
