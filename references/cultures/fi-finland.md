# Finland

## In brief

Finnish readers value honesty, brevity and reliability. Words are meant literally: say only what you mean, and do not promise "maybe later" if there is no later. Small talk, flattery and hype are unnecessary and can seem insincere. Society is highly egalitarian, so titles and status play a small role; first names are normal. Punctuality is strict. Finland is bilingual (Finnish and Swedish) and Finns are neither Swedes nor Russians: national identity is a point of pride.

## Voice and tone

- Brief, factual, calm, honest. Silence and brevity are not rudeness.
- Avoid filler courtesy and hype; Finnish readers prefer understatement.
- Finnish has no grammatical gender and no single word for "please"; Finns writing in English may sound blunt or mix up "he" and "she". Do not read rudeness into it.
- Most Finns read English very well; consumer content should still be in Finnish (and Swedish for Swedish-speaking areas and official contexts).

## Addressing people

- Names: given name + surname. Use first names in most business contact after the first exchange.
- Finnish email: "Hei Mikko," (standard, neutral) or "Hyvä Mikko Virtanen," (more formal). Closing: "Ystävällisin terveisin," (kind regards), "Terveisin," (regards).
- Formal "te" (plural you) is used mainly with older people and in customer service; most business and marketing uses "sinä".
- Swedish-speaking Finns: "Hej Anna," and "Med vänliga hälsningar,".
- English: "Hi Mikko," is fine; "Dear Mr Virtanen" for very first formal contact.

## Structuring a message

- Purpose in the first sentence; short paragraphs.
- Concrete facts, numbers and clear next steps.
- No need for warm-up or long closings.
- Keep promises exactly; if a plan changes, inform early.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Feedback is honest and factual. A simple "no" with a reason is acceptable.
- Praise is rare; a modest "hyvä" (good) is meaningful.
- Avoid emotional or dramatic language in disputes.
- When adapting Finnish source text for relationship-oriented or indirect cultures, add warmth and softeners.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Quality, reliability, functionality, sustainability, nature, design and honesty persuade. Overstated claims damage trust.
- Finnish Consumer Protection Act and EU rules prohibit misleading advertising; the Consumer Ombudsman actively enforces against unfounded environmental and comparative claims.
- Public bodies must serve both Finnish and Swedish speakers in bilingual municipalities.
- Gender equality is a strong social norm; stereotyped gender roles in ads attract criticism.
- CVs: one to two pages, factual, photo optional; modest tone with concrete results.

## Humor and emphasis

- Dry, deadpan, self-deprecating humor; often understated irony.
- Exclamation marks are rare in professional text.
- Humor about Finnish stereotypes (silence, sauna, heavy metal) is fine among Finns but can seem patronizing from outsiders.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Finns are not Swedes or Russians; do not describe Finland as part of "Scandinavia" in formal contexts (it is Nordic, not Scandinavian, strictly speaking) or as "former Soviet" (it never was).
- Relations with Russia, the Winter War (1939 to 1940) and the Continuation War are serious historical topics; since 2022, Russia-related content is highly sensitive.
- NATO membership (since 2023) is broadly supported; avoid flippant takes.
- Language politics (compulsory Swedish in schools) is a domestic debate.
- Sámi rights and Lapland imagery: avoid using Sámi dress or symbols as costume in tourism marketing.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Blue and white national colors; the flag must be used respectfully.
- Nature, lakes, forests, sauna and design are authentic positive imagery; avoid overusing Santa Claus clichés outside Lapland tourism.
- Personal space is large; imagery of strangers in close physical contact looks unnatural.

## Variation within the culture

- Helsinki and the south coast are more international and bilingual; Ostrobothnia and Åland are strongly Swedish-speaking (Åland is officially monolingual Swedish).
- Eastern and northern Finland are more rural and traditional; Lapland has the Sámi minority.
- Startups and tech (Helsinki, Espoo, Oulu) are informal and English-friendly.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The point comes first; text is short and factual.
- [ ] Hype, superlatives and empty compliments are removed.
- [ ] Promises and timelines are exact and realistic; no vague "maybe later".
- [ ] Consumer content is in Finnish (and Swedish where relevant).
- [ ] No confusion of Finland with Sweden or Russia; no "former Soviet" framing.
- [ ] No stereotyped gender roles in imagery.
- [ ] Dates, numbers and time use Finnish formats (7.3.2026, 14.30).

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style enthusiastic follow-up):

> Hi Mikko!! Just checking in to see how you're doing and whether you've had a chance to look at our amazing proposal? We'd love to hop on a quick call whenever works for you!

**After** (tailored for a Finnish reader):

> Hi Mikko, did you have time to review our proposal? If useful, we can go through the pricing in a 20-minute call on Thursday at 10.00.

**Why:** Finnish readers prefer brevity and specificity. Exclamation marks, "amazing" and vague "whenever" read as insincere or unprofessional.
