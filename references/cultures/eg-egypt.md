# Egypt

## In brief

Egyptian readers expect warmth, respect for rank and correct titles: "Dr.", "Eng." or "Ustaz" before a first name matters more than polish. Refusals and criticism are soft and private, and silence often means no, so confirm every agreement in writing with dates and names. Formal and official text is in Modern Standard Arabic; consumer copy often speaks Egyptian colloquial, and multinationals work in English. The society is large and varied: a big Coptic Christian minority, a wide gap between Cairo's international sector and conservative provincial readers. Lifestyle rules are looser than in the Gulf, but laws on Arabic in ads, honest pricing, "family values" online and vulgar content are actively enforced.

## Voice and tone

- Warm, courteous and respectful. Religious formulas such as "inshallah" (God willing) and "alhamdulillah" (thanks be to God) are everyday language for many Muslim and Christian Egyptians alike; you need not add them, but do not strip them from a native text.
- Choose the register on purpose: MSA (fusha) for contracts, official letters, formal email and institutional content; Egyptian colloquial (ammiya) for social posts, chat-style marketing and much advertising. Heavy dialect in an institutional text can draw criticism.
- Egyptian Arabic is not Gulf or Levantine Arabic. Use Egyptian month names (يناير، فبراير) and vocabulary; Saudi-flavoured or Levantine copy is noticed at once.
- Machine-like Arabic is the most common quality problem. Rewrite rather than translate: plain imperatives (افتح, not قم بفتح), attached pronouns (ملفاتي, not الملفات الخاصة بي), natural equivalents for English idioms.
- English is standard in multinationals, banks, tech and tourism; Arabic is expected by government and most local firms. Consumer-facing material needs Arabic in any case.

## Addressing people

- Names: given name, father's name, then grandfather's or family name (Ahmed Mahmoud Hassan). The last element is not always a family name in the Western sense; copy the person's own signature.
- Titles carry weight and are generously used: دكتور (doktor, also for pharmacists and other health professionals), مهندس or colloquial باشمهندس (bashmohandes, engineer), مستشار (judges, prosecutors, senior lawyers), أستاذ/أستاذة (ostaz/ostaza, respectful Mr/Ms). Use the title the person uses; leaving it out reads cold.
- In English: "Dear Dr. Ahmed", "Dear Eng. Mona". Plain "Mr" + surname is safe without a known title; "Mr" alone is too plain for a minister.
- Arabic formal email: "الأستاذ/ أحمد حسن المحترم" or "الدكتورة/ منى سالم المحترمة", then "تحية طيبة وبعد،" (warm greetings, and then). Senior officials take سعادة, ministers معالي, religious scholars فضيلة.
- Closings: "مع خالص التحية" (with sincere regards), "تحياتي" (regards) for routine mail, "وتفضلوا بقبول فائق الاحترام والتقدير" (please accept our highest respect) in official letters.
- Spoken courtesy forms: hadretak/hadretik (polite "you", literally "your presence"). Colloquial "beh", "pasha" and "rayyes" belong to speech, never to business writing.
- Use feminine titles for women (أستاذة، مديرة) and inclusive group terms (الزملاء، فريق العمل) rather than masculine plurals.

## Structuring a message

1. A clear subject line.
2. Title salutation and greeting formula, then one line about the person ("I hope you and the family are well"). Ask about the family in general, never about a wife or daughters.
3. A short self-introduction in a first contact, then the purpose in concise paragraphs.
4. Concrete next steps: date, place, named contact. Mention attachments.
5. Warm closing, an offer to call or meet, signature with full name, title and company (bilingual helps).

- Reconfirm meetings in writing a few days before and again the day before; follow email with a call if replies stall.
- Ramadan, Eid, Coptic Christmas (7 January) and a contact's promotion are occasions for a short greeting.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Never criticise someone with others copied. Feedback goes privately, framed around the work and balanced with appreciation.
- Refuse with thanks, a reason and an alternative: "We value the offer; at this stage the budget does not allow it, but we could look at phase one."
- Decode soft signals: warm "inshallah", "we'll see" or silence are not commitments. Ask a specific, easy-to-answer follow-up.
- Upward disagreement arrives indirectly or not at all; invite input privately rather than asking juniors to object in a group thread.
- No ultimatums or high-pressure closing; support positions with research. Expect bargaining, and expect terms to be revisited after signing.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Strong themes: family and friends together, Ramadan generosity, nostalgia, music, football, Egyptian history and achievement. Ramadan is the peak ad season; keep brand cues clear and repeated, as emotional ads are often misattributed.
- Consumer protection law: ads, including digital ones, must be in Arabic (other languages may be added); prices must include all taxes and fees; claims about results, awards, certificates, discounts and stock must be true; contests must be notified to the consumer protection agency in advance; buyers have a 14-day return right; property ads need a building permit; ads, invoices and checkout pages must show the seller's identity and registration.
- Electronic marketing needs prior consent, a clear sender, a "direct marketing" label and an easy opt-out under the data protection law.
- B2B: credibility comes from senior, titled people on the team, references and known contacts, not superlatives.
- CVs: one page for graduates, up to two for experienced staff, reverse chronological, quantified results without exaggeration. A photo is common; date of birth, nationality and marital status often appear; male applicants often state military-service status. Keep an Arabic version for local firms and government, English for multinationals.
- Job ads: since September 2025 the labour law bans hiring discrimination on religion, gender, origin, colour, language, disability, social level and political, union or geographic affiliation. Drop "males only", religion and home-governorate filters, which older local ads used. Legitimate ads never ask candidates for fees.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour is valued and can appear early; warm, clean, situational jokes help in relationship messages.
- Avoid political humour, jokes about religion, and old jokes about Upper Egyptians, Sudanese people or class.
- Innuendo, crude dialogue or mocking a profession in ads has led to broadcast bans and syndicate complaints; suggestive influencer content has led to prosecutions.
- Emphasis in Arabic comes through repetition and eloquent phrasing; trim it for Western readers rather than "correcting" it.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Religion:** Islam is the state religion; Christians have their own personal-status rules. Never mock or compare religions or pit Muslims against Christians; insulting religion is prosecuted, including online posts. Do not assume every reader is Muslim.
- **Online speech:** the cybercrime law punishes violating "family values", and is used against content creators; media law covers false news and treats accounts with 5,000+ followers as media outlets.
- **Politics and the state:** avoid political commentary, criticism of state institutions, and the Israel-Palestine question in business content.
- **Media regulator:** can halt ads judged vulgar or against public values and taste.
- **Data:** religion, health and political views are sensitive data; collecting them needs a real reason and explicit consent. All children's data counts as sensitive.
- Legal items are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Show giving, eating and receiving with the right hand; avoid soles of feet pointed at people.
- Keep imagery modest; mixed-gender scenes are fine, physical intimacy between unrelated people is not. Ancient and modern Egyptian achievements, Egyptian cotton and football are welcome themes.
- Do not assume alcohol or pork for a general audience, and do not dress models in traditional Egyptian costume as decoration.

## Variation within the culture

- **Cairo and Alexandria vs the provinces:** international firms, English and faster schedules in the big cities; Upper Egypt (Saʿid) and the Delta are more conservative and expect Arabic.
- **Coptic Christians** (roughly one in ten) have their own holidays, notably Christmas on 7 January; "season's greetings" on 25 December misses them.
- **Education and age:** international-school graduates mix English and Arabizi (Arabic in Latin letters, "Franco") online; government-school graduates prefer colloquial in Arabic script. Arabizi suits youth social copy only.
- **Sectors:** titles are most intense in medicine, law, engineering, academia and government. Multinational managers hold deadlines more firmly and write more directly.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Professional title (Dr., Eng., Ustaz) + first name in the salutation; no colloquial "pasha" or "beh" in writing.
- [ ] Register fits the text: MSA for formal and official, colloquial only for consumer or social copy; Egyptian, not Gulf or Levantine, vocabulary and month names.
- [ ] Arabic reads natively: no قم بـ, no الخاص بك chains, no literal idioms.
- [ ] A greeting and personal line precede the request; family enquiries stay general.
- [ ] Refusals carry thanks, a reason and an alternative; no criticism with others copied.
- [ ] Next steps name a date, owner and place; nothing relies on "inshallah" or silence as agreement.
- [ ] No ultimatums or pressure tactics; claims are backed by data or references.
- [ ] Consumer ads and landing pages include Arabic; prices include all taxes and fees; seller identity is shown.
- [ ] Discounts, awards, results and stock claims are literally true; contests are notified in advance; returns allow 14 days.
- [ ] Marketing email or SMS has consent, sender identity and an opt-out.
- [ ] Job ads have no gender, religion, origin or governorate filters and ask no fees.
- [ ] No jokes on religion, politics or regional groups; no innuendo or crude slang.
- [ ] Content does not assume all readers are Muslim; Coptic Christmas is on 7 January.
- [ ] Imagery is modest; right hand used; RTL layout and one digit system checked.
- [ ] Deadlines avoid Ramadan and Eid and allow for holidays moved at short notice.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt project-manager style):

> Mohamed, the technical report is late again. Send it by Thursday or I will escalate to your director.

**After** (to an Egyptian engineer at a partner firm, in MSA with gloss):

> المهندس محمد المحترم،
>
> تحية طيبة وبعد،
>
> أتمنى أن تكون بخير. شكرًا لك ولفريقك على الجهد الكبير في المشروع. أود متابعة موعد التقرير الفني، فنحن نحتاج إلى عرضه على الإدارة يوم الأحد ١٥ نوفمبر. هل يمكن أن يصلنا يوم الخميس ١٢ نوفمبر؟ وإذا كانت هناك أي صعوبة، يسعدني أن نتحدث هاتفيًا غدًا لنرى كيف نساعد.
>
> مع خالص التحية،

*(Gloss: "Respected Eng. Mohamed, warm greetings. I hope you are well. Thank you and your team for the great effort on the project. I would like to follow up on the technical report, as we need to present it to management on Sunday 15 November. Could it reach us on Thursday 12 November? If there is any difficulty, I would be glad to talk by phone tomorrow to see how we can help. With sincere regards,")*

**Why:** The title and set formulas show respect, and thanks come before the request. The threat to escalate, which would cost face, becomes a business reason and a dated question. Offering a call gives room to raise problems privately, so a hidden "no" surfaces early instead of on the deadline.
