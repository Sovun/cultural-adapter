# Singapore

## In brief

Singapore reads business text in standard English: clear, well structured, correctly spelled, with a short courtesy line and the point near the top. Under that efficient surface sit Asian expectations about rank, age and face, so refusals and criticism are cushioned and never made in front of others. The readership is multiracial (Chinese, Malay, Indian, Eurasian and many foreign residents), and naming, food, festivals and imagery must not assume one group. Race and religion are protected by law, and advertising, pricing, job ads and personal data are tightly regulated.

## Voice and tone

- Standard English with British spelling (colour, organise, centre) is the safe default. Consistency matters more than the choice.
- Plain words, short sentences, active voice; corporate filler ("circle back", "touch base") is mocked locally. Proofread: typos read as carelessness, and a long national campaign has tied good English to competence.
- Singlish (particles like "lah", "leh", words like "shiok" or "paiseh") is a marker of local belonging in speech and chat. Outsiders imitating it in business text sound fake or patronising.
- Regional office English such as "please revert", "do the needful" or "pls advice" is common but looks wrong in formal writing: use "please reply", "please let me know".
- Prefer requests with a reason over commands: "Could you send the file by Friday so we can file on Monday?" rather than "Pls expedite."

## Addressing people

- Default: title plus the correct name (Mr, Ms, Dr, usually without a full stop). Use "Ms" unless the reader uses "Mrs". When unsure, mirror the signature or ask "How shall I address you?"
- **Chinese names:** family name first. Lim Wei Ming is Mr Lim. Many add an English given name in front (Grace Tan Hui Ling): "Ms Tan", and "Ms Grace" is a normal, friendly local form. Writing the surname in capitals in lists avoids errors.
- **Malay names:** usually no family name. "Bin" (son of) or "binti/binte" (daughter of) joins the personal name to the father's name. Hafiz bin Rahman is Mr Hafiz, never Mr Rahman. Some drop "bin", so the second name still is not a surname. Many readers appreciate "Encik" (Mr), "Puan" (Mrs) or "Cik" (Ms) with the given name.
- **Indian names:** many are patronymic too, with "s/o" or "d/o" or the father's initial (R. Karthik). Address by the personal name: Karthik s/o Ramasamy is Mr Karthik. Short forms only when invited. "Singh" and "Kaur" are religious names for Sikhs, not family names.
- These rules beat the "Mr + last word" habit, and beat any guide that says otherwise. Married women in all main communities often keep their own names; do not assume "Mrs + husband's surname".
- Juniors may write "Sir" or "Madam" to seniors; peers move to first names once invited. Sign-offs: "Best regards", "Thanks and regards", "Thank you", plus a full signature with name, role and contact details.

## Structuring a message

1. A subject line naming the topic and, if needed, the deadline; salutation and one courtesy line.
2. Purpose and ask in the first paragraph.
3. Key details in bullets; long background as an attachment.
4. The action needed, with an owner and a date; a courteous close ("Thanks for your time") and signature.

- Put the request as a question and save the thanks for after: "Would you be able to help with this?" then "Thank you". "Thanks in advance" can sound presumptuous.
- Copy only people who must act or know; adding a counterpart's boss to apply pressure costs them face.
- Keep "urgent" for real emergencies and say what "ASAP" means ("by 5 pm today"). Do not push for an instant decision; many firms need senior or head-office sign-off.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Refuse with a reason and an alternative: "We can't meet the 12th, but we could deliver the first part by then." Decode softeners: "It may be difficult", "We'll try our best", "Let me check and get back to you" are often a no. "Can" may be a reflex. Confirm with a specific question: "Can you confirm delivery on 14 Nov?"
- Silence or no objection is not agreement. Ask open questions and invite input privately, especially from juniors after a senior has spoken.
- Criticism goes one-to-one, never in a group thread. Describe the issue, not the person.
- Many locals, especially younger staff in international firms, want specific, direct task feedback. Directness about the work is fine; bluntness about the person, or in public, is not.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with value for money, quality, reliability and reputation. Data, case studies and named clients outweigh adjectives.
- Factual claims, comparisons and superlatives must be provable if challenged; comparisons must be fair and not run down competitors. Testimonials must be genuine and current.
- Prices: headline prices include GST (9% at last check) and unavoidable fees; no extras added at checkout, no fake "was" prices, "free" must be really free, add-ons not pre-ticked, offer periods stated.
- Family, community and practical benefit work well. Fear-of-missing-out promotions ("limited stock", queues) work, but "kiasu" (fear of losing out) is insider humour: a foreign brand calling customers kiasu sounds mocking.
- Festive campaigns should rotate across Chinese New Year, Hari Raya, Deepavali and Christmas; casting should show the mix of communities.
- CV: 1-2 pages early career (2-3 senior), reverse chronological, quantified results, a languages section. Leave out NRIC number, full address, age or birth date, race, religion and marital status. A photo is still seen, mostly in public-sector and entry-level roles, but add one only if asked. Foreign candidates may state work-pass status.
- Job ads follow fair-employment guidelines: criteria tied to skills and experience; no age ("below 35", "young team"), gender ("strong guys"), race ("Chinese preferred"), religion, marital or family status. "All races are welcome" is fine. Language needs a job reason ("Mandarin, to handle our China accounts"), never "native speaker". No preference for foreigners. The Workplace Fairness Act has passed and is expected to take effect around end-2027; it is not yet in force.

## Humor and emphasis

- Light, warm humour is fine in relationship messages. Jokes about race, religion, accents, food taboos or a person's mistakes are out.
- Ads must not make rudeness, bullying or disrespect for elders look funny, and commercial copy should not satirise national policy. Emphasise with bold or structure, not capitals or "!!".

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Race and religion:** a racial harmony law in force since September 2026 lets the government stop content that harms racial harmony quickly; it sits beside the religious harmony law and Penal Code offences. Content that mocks or stereotypes any race or religion is a legal risk, not just a reputational one.
- **Advertising code:** ads must respect the national shared values (nation and community, family, consensus, racial and religious harmony), may not exploit superstition or unjustified fear, and may not show the President.
- **National symbols:** businesses may use the flag commercially without approval only from 1 July to 30 September, and respectfully; altered flag or crest designs are banned; the anthem needs approval.
- **Personal data:** do not ask for NRIC or passport numbers in sign-ups, contests or applications unless a law requires it; never show a full NRIC in an ad. Check the Do Not Call registry before marketing calls or SMS; unsolicited commercial email and SMS need an "<ADV>" label and a working unsubscribe.
- **Politics, gifts, food:** avoid commenting on government policy or comparing Singapore unfavourably with neighbours; keep gifts modest (strong anti-corruption norms); event and food copy should cover halal, no-beef and vegetarian needs.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Many Chinese readers avoid the number 4 (it sounds like "death"). Red and gold are festive; white is linked with mourning.
- Clocks, sharp objects and white or yellow flowers are poor gift images for Chinese readers; leather is unsuitable for Hindu contexts; avoid pork or alcohol in imagery aimed at Muslim readers.
- Show giving and receiving with the right hand or both hands; avoid pointing fingers, shoe soles and touching heads. Mixed-gender handshakes may jar with some Malay and Indian readers.
- Public holidays come from every community plus National Day (9 August); lunar dates move each year.

## Variation within the culture

- **Chinese Singaporeans** (the majority) bring surname-first names, lunar festivals and number and colour symbolism; Mandarin and other Chinese languages appear in family and community settings. "Gong xi fa cai" is the Chinese New Year greeting.
- **Malay Singaporeans** are mostly Muslim: patronymic names, halal food, Hari Raya Puasa and Haji. "Selamat Hari Raya" is the festive greeting; Malay is the national language and used in the anthem.
- **Indian Singaporeans** (largely Tamil, also other groups) may use patronymic names; many Hindus avoid beef. "Happy Deepavali" is the usual greeting.
- **Eurasians** and other smaller communities belong in the multiracial picture; "everyone" imagery and greetings should not leave them out.
- **Foreign residents** make up a large share of the workforce, so nationality says little about a reader's style. **Generation:** younger readers are comfortable with first names, Singlish among friends and direct feedback; older readers expect titles, deference to age and more cushioning.
- **Sector:** government, banking, law and family-owned firms are more formal and hierarchical; tech, start-ups and Western multinationals are flatter and faster. Government content uses plain English and addresses readers as "you".
- **Consumer copy:** a touch of well-judged Singlish or local settings (hawker centres, housing estates) can signal authenticity when written by locals; B2B text stays standard.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Standard English, British spelling, consistent; no Singlish, "revert", "do the needful" or texting style in professional text.
- [ ] Greeting, one courtesy line, courteous close and full signature are present.
- [ ] Purpose and ask appear in the first paragraph; details in bullets; the action has an owner and a date.
- [ ] Names follow each community's rule: Chinese surname first; Malay and patronymic Indian names addressed by the personal name, never the father's name.
- [ ] Married women are not assumed to use their husband's surname.
- [ ] Refusals give a reason and an alternative; criticism is private and about the issue.
- [ ] Soft replies ("we'll try", "can", silence) are followed by a confirming question with a date.
- [ ] No capitals, "!!", or overused "urgent"; no copying bosses to apply pressure.
- [ ] Claims and comparisons are provable; no unsupported "No. 1" or "best".
- [ ] Prices include GST and all unavoidable fees; offers state their period.
- [ ] Imagery and festivals reflect several communities; nothing mocks a race, religion or food practice.
- [ ] No altered flag, and no commercial flag use outside 1 July to 30 September.
- [ ] Forms and CVs do not collect NRIC, age, race, religion, marital status or photos; marketing email has an unsubscribe.
- [ ] Job ads have no age, gender, race, nationality or "native speaker" requirements.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (blunt, informal style):

> Hey Ahmad, the deck you sent is not good enough. Redo slides 4-7 and send it back ASAP, the client meeting is coming up!!

**After** (for Mr Ismail bin Ahmad, a client-side manager):

> Dear Mr Ismail,
>
> Thank you for sending the draft deck. It gives us a strong base for Thursday's meeting.
>
> To make sure the cost figures match the latest quote, could we revise slides 4 to 7 together? I have added my comments in the attached file.
>
> Would you be able to send the updated version by 5 pm on Tuesday, 3 November? That gives us a day to review before the meeting. I am happy to call if that is easier.
>
> Best regards,
> Rachel Lim
> Account Manager

**Why:** The Malay name is used correctly (Mr Ismail, not "Ahmad", which is his father's name), and the tone moves from casual to courteous. The criticism becomes a shared task with a reason, the vague "ASAP" becomes a specific time, and the shouting punctuation goes. The request still appears in the second paragraph, as Singapore readers expect.
