# China (mainland)

## In brief

Chinese business communication is relationship-driven (guanxi), hierarchical and face-conscious. Messages are indirect when they carry bad news, and rejections are signalled rather than stated. Company size, history and reputation matter, so presenting your organization's credentials with facts is expected rather than boastful. Mainland China has strict legal rules on advertising language, endorsements, job ads, maps, personal data and political content, and these override any stylistic preference.

## Voice and tone

- Respectful, warm and relationship-aware. Courtesy formulas are expected in formal letters but over-frequent "please/thank you" between close partners can feel distant.
- Requests read better when they say why they are reasonable. Bare pressure ("请尽快回复", "please reply ASAP") should be cushioned.
- When writing in English for Chinese readers: simple declarative sentences, no idioms, no sarcasm, explicit dates. Jokes and wordplay rarely survive translation.
- Chinese copy should be restructured, not translated word for word: shorter sentences, specific measure words, active voice in positive copy (被 often sounds negative). Taglines and brand names need transcreation with characters of positive meaning.
- Register depends on text type: 您 in business letters to clients, seniors and strangers; 你 or no pronoun in app and product copy.
- Daily business runs on WeChat; short acknowledgements such as 收到 (received) and 好的 (OK) are polite there. Email is kept for formal documents, proposals, contracts and international firms, and an email written like a chat message looks careless. Consumer content runs mainly on WeChat, Weibo, Douyin and Xiaohongshu, with a lively, visual, emoji-rich tone.
- Use Simplified characters for mainland China. Traditional characters are for Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan; sending them to a mainland audience looks careless.

## Addressing people

- Name order: family name first (王伟 = WANG Wei). Women usually keep their own family name after marriage. Many professionals use an English given name internationally ("David Wang"); use the form they present. Given names alone are for family and close friends.
- Family name plus title: 王先生 (Mr Wang), 李女士 (Ms Li), 张经理 (Manager Zhang), 刘总 (Executive Liu, for general managers and directors), 陈教授 (Professor Chen). 女士 is the safe form for women; 小姐 is less formal and can be misread.
- Formal salutation: 尊敬的王总： or 尊敬的李女士：. Unknown recipient: 致相关负责人. Formal closing: 此致 敬礼 (on two lines), then name; 敬上 after the name is also used. In email: 祝好 or 顺祝商祺 (wishing business prosperity).
- Signature: full name (Chinese and pinyin or English), title, company, direct phone and email.
- In English: "Dear Mr Wang" or "Dear Director Wang".

## Structuring a message

- Specific subject line and one main topic per email. Open with a courteous greeting and acknowledgement of the relationship, previous contact or a recent achievement.
- Introduce your company with facts: history, size, key clients, achievements. This is expected, not boastful.
- Present the proposal with mutual benefit framing (互利共赢, win-win).
- List what you need and by when, politely (烦请于X月X日前确认, kindly confirm by...). Leave room for negotiation; avoid ultimatums.
- Close with good wishes and an invitation to continue (期待您的回复, looking forward to your reply).
- Decisions are rarely made in the meeting; they follow internal review and offline talks (calls, WeChat). Allow time, and confirm what was agreed in writing.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Do not reject a proposal or person outright, especially in front of others, including group chats and wide CC lists. Rejecting an idea can be felt as rejecting the person.
- Use indirect forms: "This may require further study", "We may need to consider the timing", and propose alternatives. If you must say no, wrap it in thanks, an objective constraint (budget, policy, schedule) and an alternative.
- Criticism in private, introduced gradually, balanced with acknowledgement of effort and aimed at the issue, not the person.
- Decoding incoming messages: praise followed by "but" (想法不错，但…), "possible in principle, but" (原则上可以，但…), "it has to go through the process" (还要走流程) or "I'll check with my boss" (和领导商量一下) often mean no or not now.
- Nodding and "yes" (是, 好) often mean "I understand", not agreement.
- Silence, or laughter and a light tone, can mask discomfort or disagreement.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- **Advertising Law of the PRC** (Art. 9) bans absolute terms: 最 (most/best), 第一 (number one), 国家级 (national-level), 顶级 (top-level), 唯一 (only), 首选 (first choice) and anything of similar meaning. Fines run from RMB 200,000 to 1,000,000 at last check, and licences can be revoked. SAMR's 2023 guideline allows some cases (aspirations such as "we strive for the best service", comparison with your own earlier products, official awards, facts with a stated period and region) if provable. Never treated as minor in medical, health-food, investment or education ads. English superlatives in Chinese-market ads must be rewritten too.
- Statistics, survey results and quotes in ads must be true and cite source and scope. No disparaging competitors; ads must be recognizable as ads.
- Ads must not damage national dignity, use the national flag, anthem, emblem or military symbols, show state bodies' or officials' names or images, or include content considered vulgar, superstitious or discriminatory.
- Sector rules: endorsers must have used the product, none under 10, none at all for medical or health food; education ads may not promise passes or admission; investment ads need risk warnings and no promised returns.
- Maps must show China's official borders (Taiwan, South China Sea islands, Diaoyu Dao, disputed Himalayan borders) and any published map needs an official review number (审图号). "Problem maps" lead to seizures and fines; avoid decorative maps.
- Strong persuasion levers: family benefit, prosperity, status and face (prestige products), national pride (国潮 guochao, "national trend" brands), festivals (Spring Festival, Mid-Autumn, Singles' Day 11.11). Generic red-gold-dragon decoration now reads as shallow; work with local creators.
- Third-party endorsement, KOL/influencer reviews and social proof are powerful. Under the 2023 Internet Advertising Measures, paid "seeding" posts (种草) with a purchase link must be labelled 广告.
- CV: one page is usual (two for senior roles). Photo, age, hometown and political status are optional, more common for local firms and state-owned employers. State the target role (求职意向), give quantified results, often a short self-evaluation (自我评价). Multinationals accept Western formats.
- Job ads: no gender restrictions or preferences (限男性, 男性优先), no questions on marriage or childbearing plans, no ethnic, racial or religious discrimination, no household-registration (户籍) conditions. Uncorrected postings are fined (RMB 10,000-50,000 at last check).

## Humor and emphasis

- Idioms and homophone wordplay appear in advertising, but characters that are fine alone can combine into an unwanted meaning; have native speakers check. Western-style sarcasm and irony are risky.
- Humor fits only after rapport exists. Never use humor touching politics, leaders, ethnic minorities or national history.
- Exclamation marks and festive language are acceptable in consumer marketing; restrained in formal B2B and government communication. In email, highlight only critical items such as deadlines.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Taiwan, Hong Kong, Tibet and Xinjiang: content suggesting independence or separate statehood is illegal in China and triggers boycotts. International brands have faced major backlash for listing Taiwan or Hong Kong as countries in dropdowns or on maps, or quoting figures Beijing treats as separatist. For mainland audiences, use "Taiwan, China", "Hong Kong, China" or "Hong Kong SAR". These forms will offend Taiwanese audiences: keep regional content separate.
- Avoid criticism of the Communist Party, leaders, government policy and the military. Avoid references to 4 June 1989, Falun Gong and dissidents. Jokes comparing leaders to cartoon characters are censored and offensive to many. Religion is regulated; proselytizing content is not permitted.
- Japan and WWII (Nanjing massacre), the Rising Sun flag and Japanese imperial symbols are deeply offensive.
- Territorial disputes (South China Sea, India border) are emotive. Use official names of states and ethnic groups and neutral words (外国人, not 老外).
- **PIPL** (Personal Information Protection Law): consent must be informed and explicit, with a notice naming the processor, purpose, data types and retention. Sensitive data (health, biometrics, location, finances, children under 14) and transfers abroad need separate consent. Personalized marketing must offer a non-personalized option or easy opt-out. Marketing messages need prior consent and an opt-out.
- Expensive gifts or lavish offers can read as bribery under anti-corruption rules.
- Do not lump China together with Japan or Korea culturally. Criticism or mockery from foreign brands draws far harsher reactions than self-criticism by Chinese people.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- **8** (bā, sounds like prosperity) is lucky; **6** (smooth) and **9** (long-lasting) are positive. **4** (sì, sounds like death) is unlucky; avoid in prices, floors and gift sets.
- **Red** = luck, celebration. **Gold** = wealth. **White** and **black** are funeral colors; white envelopes and white flowers for gifts are inappropriate.
- Never write a person's name in red ink (associated with death notices or severing ties).
- A **green hat** implies a man's wife is unfaithful; avoid green hats on men in imagery.
- Gift taboos (relevant for promotions and gifting campaigns): clocks (送钟 = attending a funeral), umbrellas and pears (separation), knives and scissors (cutting ties), handkerchiefs and straw sandals.
- Money gifts go in red envelopes (红包), also widely digital. Swastika (卍) in Buddhist context is not Nazi symbolism.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | 2026年3月7日; short form 2026/3/7 or 2026-03-07 (hyphens only for full dates). Four-digit years. In English text spell the month out. |
| Time | 24-hour clock in business (14:30); 下午2:30 in casual text. One time zone nationwide (UTC+8). |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89, but Chinese uses 万 (10,000) and 亿 (100 million): 3.5 million = 350万, 1.2 billion = 12亿. Repeat units in ranges (9亿~16亿). |
| Currency | ¥1,234.50 or 1,234.50元; CNY or RMB in international text. |
| Week starts | Monday. Spring Festival and the October Golden Week stop most business; plan deadlines around them. |
| Units | Metric; traditional units such as 斤 (jin, 500 g) and 亩 (mu) in some contexts. |
| Phone | +86 10 1234 5678; mobile +86 138 0000 0000 |
| Address | Largest to smallest: province, city, district, street, building, room, then name. Six-digit postcode. |
| Typography | Full-width punctuation (，。：“”). Chinese book title marks 《》. |

## Variation within the culture

- Tier-1 cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Guangzhou) are cosmopolitan; lower-tier cities and rural areas are more traditional.
- Southern China (Guangdong) has Cantonese language and business traditions; written copy stays in standard Mandarin. Shanghai is commercially sophisticated; Beijing is political and formal.
- Younger generations are digitally native, brand-savvy and responsive to "guochao" pride, but also quick to call out tokenistic or disrespectful foreign brands.
- State-owned enterprises are more hierarchical and formal, and value credentials and party membership more, than private tech companies.
- Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan have different scripts, legal systems, sensitivities and norms; do not reuse mainland copy unchanged.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No absolute or superlative claims (最, 第一, 唯一, "best", "No. 1") in Chinese-market advertising unless a SAMR exemption applies and it is provable.
- [ ] Statistics cite source and scope; paid KOL or seeding posts with purchase links are labelled 广告.
- [ ] Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau are never listed or shown as separate countries in mainland-facing content; any map has a review number.
- [ ] No political, religious or historical content that could be read as criticism of China; no flags, emblems or officials' images in ads.
- [ ] No Rising Sun flag or Japanese imperial imagery.
- [ ] Job ads have no gender, marriage, childbearing, ethnic or hukou conditions.
- [ ] Refusals and criticism are indirect and face-saving; alternatives are offered.
- [ ] Company credentials and partnership benefits are presented early, with facts.
- [ ] Simplified Chinese is used for the mainland; 您 in letters, 你 in product copy.
- [ ] Numbers avoid 4; colors avoid white/black funeral associations; no names in red.
- [ ] Large numbers are correctly converted to 万/亿; dates are year first or month spelled out.
- [ ] Data collection text is PIPL-compliant (separate consent where needed, opt-out of personalized marketing).

## Rewrite example

**Before** (Western cold pitch):

> We're the best CRM on the market. Sign up for a free trial now.

**After** (tailored for a Chinese B2B reader, in English):

> Dear Mr Zhang,
>
> I am writing on behalf of Nordline, a CRM provider founded in 2012 and now serving over 1,500 companies in 14 countries, including several leading manufacturers in Jiangsu and Guangdong. We have followed your company's growth in export markets with great interest and believe there may be good opportunities for mutually beneficial cooperation.
>
> If convenient, we would be honored to introduce our solution to you and your team, at a time that suits you.
>
> With best wishes for your business,

**Why:** "Best on the market" violates Chinese advertising law in Chinese-language ads and sounds empty. Establishing company credibility, acknowledging the reader's success and framing mutual benefit build face and relationship.
