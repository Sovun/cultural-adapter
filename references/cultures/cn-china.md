# China (mainland)

## In brief

Chinese business communication is relationship-driven (guanxi), hierarchical and face-conscious. Messages are indirect when they carry bad news, and rejections are signalled rather than stated. At the same time, company size, reputation and status matter, so presenting your organization's credentials is expected rather than boastful. Mainland China has strict legal rules on advertising language, maps and political content, and these override any stylistic preference.

## Voice and tone

- Respectful, warm and relationship-aware. Courtesy formulas are expected in formal letters but over-frequent "please/thank you" between close partners can feel distant.
- When writing in English for Chinese readers: simple declarative sentences, no idioms, no sarcasm, explicit dates.
- Mainland consumer content runs mainly on WeChat, Weibo, Douyin and Xiaohongshu; tone is lively, visual and emoji-rich. Email is used mainly with international partners.
- Use Simplified characters for mainland China. Traditional characters are for Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan. Mixing them, or sending Traditional to a mainland audience, looks careless.

## Addressing people

- Name order: family name first (王伟 = WANG Wei). Many professionals use an English given name internationally ("David Wang"). Use the form they present.
- Family name plus title: 王先生 (Mr Wang), 李女士 (Ms Li), 张经理 (Manager Zhang), 刘总 (Executive Liu, for general managers and above), 陈教授 (Professor Chen). Avoid 小姐 (xiaojie) for women in business; it can carry negative connotations in some contexts. 女士 is safe.
- Given names alone are for family and close friends.
- Formal letter salutation: 尊敬的王总： or 尊敬的李女士：. Formal letter closing: 此致 敬礼 (on two lines), then name and date. In email: 祝好 or 顺祝商祺 (wishing business prosperity).
- In English: "Dear Mr Wang" or "Dear Director Wang".

## Structuring a message

- Courteous greeting and acknowledgement of the relationship or previous contact.
- Introduce your company: history, size, key clients, achievements. This is expected, not boastful.
- Present the proposal with mutual benefit framing (互利共赢, win-win).
- Leave room for negotiation; avoid ultimatums.
- Close with good wishes and an invitation to continue the discussion.
- Important decisions are often taken offline (meals, calls, WeChat). Written follow-up confirms what was agreed.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Do not reject a proposal or person outright, especially in front of others. Rejecting an idea can be felt as rejecting the person.
- Use indirect forms: "This may require further study", "We may need to consider the timing", and propose alternatives.
- Criticism in private, introduced gradually, balanced with acknowledgement of effort.
- Nodding and "yes" (是, 好) often mean "I understand", not agreement.
- Silence and laughter can mask discomfort or disagreement.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- **Advertising Law of the PRC** prohibits absolute terms: 最 (most/best), 第一 (number one), 国家级 (national-level), 顶级 (top-level), 极致 (ultimate) and similar. Fines are common. English superlatives in Chinese-market ads must be rewritten too.
- Ads must not damage national dignity, use the national flag, anthem or emblem, or include content considered vulgar, superstitious or discriminatory.
- Maps must show China's official borders (including Taiwan and South China Sea claims); "problem maps" lead to bans and fines. Map content needs specialist review.
- Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL) governs data collection; consent language is required.
- Strong persuasion levers: family benefit, prosperity, status and face (prestige products), national pride (国潮 guochao, "national trend" brands), festivals (Spring Festival, Mid-Autumn, Singles' Day 11.11).
- Third-party endorsement, KOL/influencer reviews and social proof are powerful.
- CV: often includes photo, date of birth and contact details; one to two pages; education and employer prestige weigh heavily. Multinationals accept Western formats.

## Humor and emphasis

- Wordplay and puns (homophones) are common in advertising. Western-style sarcasm and irony are risky.
- Never use humor touching politics, leaders, ethnic minorities or national history.
- Exclamation marks and festive language are acceptable in consumer marketing; restrained in formal B2B and government communication.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Taiwan, Hong Kong, Tibet and Xinjiang: content suggesting independence or separate statehood is illegal in China and triggers boycotts. International brands have faced major backlash for listing Taiwan or Hong Kong as countries in dropdowns or on maps. For mainland audiences, use "Taiwan, China", "Hong Kong, China" or "Hong Kong SAR". Note that these forms will offend Taiwanese audiences: keep regional content separate.
- Avoid criticism of the Communist Party, leaders, government policy and the military. Avoid references to 4 June 1989, Falun Gong and dissidents. Jokes comparing leaders to cartoon characters are censored and offensive to many.
- Japan and WWII (Nanjing massacre), the Rising Sun flag and Japanese imperial symbols are deeply offensive.
- Territorial disputes (South China Sea, India border) are emotive.
- Religion is regulated; proselytizing content is not permitted.
- Do not lump China together with Japan or Korea culturally.
- Foreigners criticizing China is received far worse than self-criticism by Chinese people.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- **8** (bā, sounds like prosperity) is lucky; **6** (smooth) and **9** (long-lasting) are positive. **4** (sì, sounds like death) is unlucky; avoid in prices, floors and gift sets.
- **Red** = luck, celebration. **Gold** = wealth. **White** and **black** are funeral colors; white envelopes and white flowers for gifts are inappropriate.
- Never write a person's name in red ink (associated with death notices or severing ties).
- A **green hat** implies a man's wife is unfaithful; avoid green hats on men in imagery.
- Gift taboos (relevant for promotions and gifting campaigns): clocks (送钟 = attending a funeral), umbrellas and pears (separation), knives and scissors (cutting ties), shoes in some regions.
- Money gifts go in red envelopes (红包), also widely digital.
- Swastika (卍) in Buddhist context is not Nazi symbolism.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | YYYY年M月D日 (2026年3月7日) or 2026-03-07 / 2026/3/7. Never DD/MM ambiguity; spell month names in English text. |
| Time | 24-hour clock in business (14:30); 下午2:30 in casual text. One time zone nationwide (UTC+8). |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89, but Chinese uses 万 (10,000) and 亿 (100 million) units: 1.2亿 = 120 million. Convert carefully. |
| Currency | ¥1,234.50 or 1,234.50元; CNY or RMB in international text. |
| Week starts | Monday. |
| Units | Metric; traditional units such as 斤 (jin, 500 g) and 亩 (mu) in some contexts. |
| Phone | +86 10 1234 5678; mobile +86 138 0000 0000 |
| Address | Largest to smallest: province, city, district, street, building, room, then name. |
| Typography | Full-width punctuation (，。：“”). Chinese book title marks 《》. |

## Variation within the culture

- Tier-1 cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Guangzhou) are cosmopolitan; lower-tier cities and rural areas are more traditional.
- Southern China (Guangdong) has Cantonese language and business traditions; Shanghai is commercially sophisticated; Beijing is political and formal.
- Younger generations are digitally native, brand-savvy and responsive to "guochao" pride, but also sensitive to perceived disrespect by foreign brands.
- State-owned enterprises are more hierarchical and formal than private tech companies.
- Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan have different scripts, legal systems, sensitivities and norms; do not reuse mainland copy unchanged.

## Review checklist

- [ ] No absolute or superlative claims (最, 第一, "best", "No. 1") in Chinese-market advertising.
- [ ] Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau are never listed or shown as separate countries in mainland-facing content; maps reviewed.
- [ ] No political, religious or historical content that could be read as criticism of China.
- [ ] No Rising Sun flag or Japanese imperial imagery.
- [ ] Refusals and criticism are indirect and face-saving; alternatives are offered.
- [ ] Company credentials and partnership benefits are presented early.
- [ ] Simplified Chinese is used for the mainland.
- [ ] Numbers avoid 4; colors avoid white/black funeral associations; no names in red.
- [ ] Large numbers are correctly converted to 万/亿.
- [ ] Dates are unambiguous (year first or month spelled out).
- [ ] Data collection text is PIPL-compliant.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (Western cold pitch):

> We're the best CRM on the market. Sign up for a free trial now.

**After** (tailored for a Chinese B2B reader, in English):

> Dear Mr Zhang,
>
> I am writing on behalf of Nordline, a CRM provider founded in 2012 and now serving over 1,500 companies in 14 countries, including several leading manufacturers in Jiangsu and Guangdong. We have followed your company's growth in export markets with great interest and believe there may be good opportunities for mutually beneficial cooperation.
>
> If convenient, we would be honored to introduce our solution to you and your team, at a time that suits you.
>
> With best wishes for your business,

**Why:** "Best on the market" violates Chinese advertising law in Chinese-language ads and sounds empty. Establishing company credibility, acknowledging the reader's success and framing mutual benefit build face and relationship.
