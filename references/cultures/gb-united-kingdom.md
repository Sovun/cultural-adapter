# United Kingdom

## In brief

British readers prize understatement, politeness and a dry, self-aware voice. Requests and criticism are wrapped in softeners, and what is said is often milder than what is meant. Hype, boasting and heavy enthusiasm erode credibility fast; evidence and modest confidence work better. The UK is four nations, and treating "British" and "English" as the same is a classic mistake.

## Voice and tone

- Understated, measured, lightly humorous. Confidence is shown through calm, not volume.
- Politeness markers are heavy: "please", "thank you", "sorry" (often meaning "excuse me"), "if you don't mind".
- Plain English is valued in public-sector writing (GOV.UK style is a good benchmark: short sentences, no jargon).
- Swearing is common in casual speech, but out of place in professional writing.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, middle name(s), surname.
- Titles in British style have **no full stop**: Mr, Mrs, Ms, Dr. "Ms" is the safe default for women.
- First contact or formal letter: "Dear Ms Patel,". Ongoing business: "Dear Priya," or "Hi Priya,". Mirror the form the other person uses in their sign-off.
- Knights and dames: "Sir Paul" or "Dame Judi", never "Sir McCartney".
- Sign-offs: "Kind regards," and "Best wishes," are standard. "Cheers," is friendly and informal. "Yours sincerely" (name known) and "Yours faithfully" (name unknown) are for formal letters.
- Terms of endearment ("love", "mate") are common in speech but never appropriate in business writing.

## Structuring a message

- Brief pleasantry ("I hope you're well"), then the purpose.
- Put the request in a cushioned form but make sure it is still identifiable: readers expect to "read between the lines" but also expect a clear action.
- Formal decisions are taken in stages. Spell out what information or approval is needed for the next step.
- Close with appreciation and a gentle call to action ("Do let me know if you have any questions").

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement is coded. Learn the decoder when reviewing British source text or tailoring for British readers:
  - "That's an interesting idea" often means "I don't agree".
  - "I'll bear it in mind" often means "I won't do it".
  - "Quite good" can mean mediocre; "not bad" can mean good.
  - "With the greatest respect" signals strong disagreement.
- When writing for British readers, cushion criticism: "One thing that might be worth revisiting is..." rather than "This is wrong".
- When writing to non-British readers from a British draft, make hidden criticism explicit; international readers may miss it entirely.
- Refusals: polite and often apologetic: "I'm afraid we won't be able to..."

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Evidence over adjectives. Facts, figures and independent proof beat superlatives.
- Self-deprecating or witty copy often outperforms earnest hype.
- Advertising claims are regulated by the Advertising Standards Authority (CAP Code): objective claims need evidence; "best" and comparative claims are checked.
- Job adverts must comply with the Equality Act 2010: avoid wording that implies age, sex, race, disability or other protected characteristics ("young team", "recent graduate", "native English speaker" unless genuinely required).
- CVs: typically two pages, a short profile, no photo, no date of birth or marital status. Achievement-focused but in a measured tone ("contributed to", "led" with numbers).
- LinkedIn and bios: confident but not boastful. "Proud to share" posts are tolerated but a little un-British.

## Humor and emphasis

- Dry wit, irony, sarcasm and understatement are everyday tools, including at work. A well-placed light remark builds rapport.
- Irony is risky in content that will be translated or read by non-native speakers.
- Teasing is a form of affection among colleagues, but avoid it in writing with people you don't know.
- Exclamation marks: sparingly, at most one per message.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- National identity: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland are referred to as countries. Do not call Scots or Welsh people "English". In Northern Ireland, "British" and "Irish" are both politically loaded; "Northern Irish" or simply the person's own term is safest.
- Brexit, Scottish independence, Irish reunification and the Troubles remain divisive.
- Empire and colonial history: views vary sharply; avoid nostalgic framing.
- The NHS enjoys near-universal affection. Avoid dismissive comparisons, especially from a US perspective.
- Class is noticed. Copy that sounds snobbish or that mocks regional accents lands badly.
- Salary, personal finances and religion are private topics in early contact.
- Satire of politicians and the royal family is normal among Britons, but riskier from outsiders.
- Disability and mental health: strong legal protections and social expectations. Never use them as jokes.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The V-sign with palm facing inward is an insult; with palm outward it means peace or victory.
- Union flag versus national flags (St George's cross, Saltire, Welsh dragon) carry different meanings; using the English flag for "UK" content irritates Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish readers.
- Poppies (red) are worn in late October to 11 November for Remembrance; they are a serious symbol, not decoration.
- Imagery should reflect the multi-ethnic population, especially for London-focused content.

## Variation within the culture

- Scotland, Northern Ireland and northern England often read as warmer and more direct than south-east England.
- Welsh is an official language in Wales; public bodies there must publish bilingual content.
- London is highly international; regional audiences may react poorly to London-centric assumptions.
- Younger and tech-sector professionals are more informal and more comfortable with US-style enthusiasm.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Superlatives and hype words are replaced with evidence or toned down.
- [ ] Requests are cushioned but still clearly identifiable as requests.
- [ ] Criticism is phrased as suggestion, not verdict.
- [ ] "English" is not used to mean "British" or "UK".
- [ ] Titles have no full stops (Mr, Dr) in British-style text.
- [ ] British spelling and DD/MM/YYYY or "7 March 2026" date format are used.
- [ ] Exclamation marks are at most one, and only in informal text.
- [ ] Job ads and HR texts avoid age-coded or other protected-characteristic wording.
- [ ] No dismissive remarks about the NHS, national identities or regional accents.
- [ ] If a British source text is adapted for other cultures, hidden criticism and understatement are made explicit.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style enthusiasm):

> We're super excited to announce our game-changing, best-in-class platform! You'll absolutely love it!!

**After** (tailored for UK readers):

> We've launched a new version of our platform. Early users cut their reporting time by about a third, and we think you'll find it useful too. Have a look and let us know what you think.

**Why:** British readers discount hype. A plain statement with a modest, evidence-backed claim is more credible and still warm.
