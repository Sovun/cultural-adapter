# United Kingdom

## In brief

British readers prize understatement, politeness and a dry, self-aware voice. Requests and criticism are wrapped in softeners, and what is said is often milder than what is meant. Hype, boasting and heavy enthusiasm erode credibility fast; evidence and modest confidence work better, and UK advertising and consumer law back this up with strict rules on claims, reviews and prices. The UK is four nations, and treating "British" and "English" as the same is a classic mistake.

## Voice and tone

- Understated, measured, lightly humorous. Confidence is shown through calm, not volume.
- Politeness markers are heavy: "please", "thank you", "sorry" (often a reflex, not an admission), "if you don't mind".
- Plain English is the public-sector norm (GOV.UK style: short sentences, everyday words such as "use" not "utilise"). Product copy uses contractions freely; official text avoids "can't" and "don't".
- Emphasis comes from front-loaded sentences, not bold, capitals or intensifiers. Swearing is out of place in professional writing.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, middle name(s), surname.
- Titles have **no full stop**: Mr, Mrs, Ms, Dr. "Ms" is the default for women unless they state otherwise. Do not carry over foreign honorifics (Herr, Madame).
- First contact or formal letter: "Dear Ms Patel,". Ongoing business: "Dear Priya," or "Hi Priya,". Match the formality of the other person's greeting and sign-off. Groups: "Hi everyone" or "Hi all", not "guys".
- Knights and dames: "Sir Paul" or "Dame Judi", never "Sir McCartney".
- Sign-offs: "Kind regards" is the safe standard; "Best wishes" is warmer; "Many thanks" after a request. "Cheers" is for people you know, and some readers find it curt. "Yours sincerely" (name known) and "Yours faithfully" (Dear Sir or Madam) are for formal letters. No sign-off at all reads as abrupt.
- Terms of endearment ("love", "mate") are common in speech but never appropriate in business writing.

## Structuring a message

- Brief pleasantry ("I hope you're well"), then the purpose. Front-load the key information in subject lines, headings and first sentences.
- Put the request in a cushioned form but make sure it is still identifiable: readers expect to "read between the lines" but also expect a clear action.
- Formal decisions are taken in stages. Propose a timeline and say what information or approval is needed for the next step; pressing for an immediate yes backfires.
- Close with appreciation and a gentle call to action ("Do let me know if you have any questions").

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement is coded. Typical signals when decoding British text (context decides, not every case):
  - "That's an interesting idea" often means "I don't agree".
  - "I'll bear it in mind", "that might be difficult" or "leave it with me" can mean "no".
  - "Not bad" usually means good; "quite good" is ambiguous, from faint praise to genuine praise.
  - "Not quite there yet" or "a few minor points" can mean substantial rework.
  - "With the greatest respect" usually precedes strong disagreement.
- An important point may be slipped in casually ("by the way..."), and self-blame ("I probably wasn't clear") is often a polite formula, not an admission.
- When writing for British readers, cushion criticism and aim it at the work: "One thing that might be worth revisiting is..." rather than "This is wrong".
- When adapting a British draft for non-British readers, make hidden criticism explicit; international readers may miss it entirely.
- Refusals: polite and often apologetic: "I'm afraid we won't be able to..."

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Evidence over adjectives. Facts, figures and independent proof beat superlatives. Self-deprecating or witty copy often works better than earnest hype; hard selling is disliked.
- Advertising standards (CAP Code, enforced by the ASA): objective claims need evidence on file before publishing. Unqualified "best", "#1" or "UK's leading" count as claims against every rival and need comparative proof. Comparisons must be like-for-like and not denigrate rivals. "Free" means nothing to pay beyond unavoidable delivery or response costs. Ads must not use harmful gender stereotypes, even as a joke.
- Consumer law (DMCC Act 2024, enforced by the CMA, which can fine up to 10% of global turnover): fake reviews, hidden incentivised reviews and cherry-picked review displays are banned (since April 2025); false "limited time" or scarcity claims are banned; the headline price must include all mandatory fees, and consumer prices include VAT.
- Paid influencer and social content must be labelled up front as "Ad" or "#ad"; "sponsored", "gifted" or a brand tag alone is not enough.
- Job adverts must comply with the Equality Act 2010 (Great Britain; Northern Ireland has its own equality law): avoid wording that implies age, sex, race, disability or other protected characteristics ("young team", "recent graduate", "digital native", "native English speaker" unless genuinely required). Use gender-neutral titles (chair, salesperson).
- CVs: called a CV, typically two pages, a short personal profile, reverse-chronological experience, no photo, date of birth, marital status or nationality. Achievements stated factually with numbers, no inflated adjectives. Referees are not listed.
- LinkedIn and bios: confident but not boastful. "Proud to share" posts are tolerated but a little un-British.

## Humor and emphasis

- Dry wit, irony and understatement are everyday tools, including at work. A well-placed light remark builds rapport.
- Irony is risky in content that will be translated or read by non-native speakers.
- Teasing is a form of affection among colleagues, but avoid it in writing with people you don't know.
- Exclamation marks: sparingly, at most one per message.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- National identity: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland are referred to as countries. Do not call Scots or Welsh people "English". In Northern Ireland, people may identify as British, Irish, Northern Irish or several; use the person's own term and neutral place names.
- Brexit, Scottish independence, Irish reunification and the Troubles remain divisive. Empire and colonial history: avoid nostalgic framing.
- The NHS enjoys wide affection; avoid dismissive comparisons, especially from a US perspective.
- Class and accent are noticed. Copy that sounds snobbish or mocks regional accents lands badly. Salary, personal finances and religion are private early on.
- Satire of politicians and the royal family is normal among Britons, but riskier from outsiders.
- Inclusive wording: "disabled people" (not "the disabled" or "suffering from"); name specific ethnic groups rather than "BAME"; "women" and "men", not "females" and "males". Never joke about disability or mental health.
- Marketing email and texts (PECR, regulated by the ICO): individuals must opt in, except the soft opt-in for existing customers who bought similar products; every message needs the sender's identity and a working unsubscribe. Corporate B2B addresses are exempt, sole traders are not. Non-essential cookies need prior opt-in; the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 added exemptions such as some analytics cookies, so check current status.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- The V-sign with palm facing inward is an insult; with palm outward it means peace or victory.
- Union flag versus national flags (St George's cross, Saltire, Welsh dragon): using the English flag for "UK" content irritates Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish readers, and flags can read as nationalist or political.
- Poppies (red) are worn from late October to 11 November for Remembrance; they are a serious symbol, not decoration.
- Imagery should reflect the four nations and a multi-ethnic population, without stereotypes; sexualised imagery unrelated to the product breaches ad rules.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | DD/MM/YYYY (07/03/2026). Long form: 7 March 2026 (no comma, no "th"). Deadlines: "on or before 7 March". |
| Time | 24-hour in schedules (14:30); 12-hour in prose (2:30pm or 2.30pm), "midday"/"midnight". Add "UK time" for international readers. |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89; 50% (no space). |
| Currency | £1,234.50, £75 for whole pounds (no ".00"); "GBP" in international documents. Consumer prices include VAT. |
| Week starts | Monday. |
| Units | Metric officially, but miles and mph on roads, pints for beer, and height and weight often in feet/inches and stone. |
| Spelling | British: colour, organise (-ize only in some house styles), centre, programme, licence (noun) / license (verb), travelling. |
| Style | Sentence case headings; no Oxford comma; "Monday to Friday" (not "through"); "call us on"; "No." not "#". |
| Phone | +44 20 7946 0000 (nationally 020 7946 0000). |
| Postcode | Alphanumeric, e.g. SW1A 1AA, on its own line. |

## Variation within the culture

- Scotland, Northern Ireland and northern England are often described as warmer and more direct than south-east England; treat this as a loose tendency.
- Welsh is an official language in Wales; public bodies there must publish bilingual content, with Welsh first (or on the left) and equal prominence. Slogans need original Welsh copy, not literal translation.
- London is highly international; regional audiences may react poorly to London-centric assumptions.
- Younger and tech-sector professionals are more informal and more comfortable with US-style enthusiasm.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Superlatives and hype words are replaced with evidence or toned down; objective claims are provable.
- [ ] Requests are cushioned but still clearly identifiable as requests.
- [ ] Criticism is phrased as suggestion, not verdict.
- [ ] "English" is not used to mean "British" or "UK".
- [ ] Titles have no full stops (Mr, Dr); sentence case headings, no Oxford comma.
- [ ] British spelling and DD/MM/YYYY or "7 March 2026" date format are used.
- [ ] Exclamation marks are at most one, and only in informal text.
- [ ] Consumer prices include VAT and all mandatory fees; "free" and urgency claims are genuine; no fake or selective reviews.
- [ ] Paid social content is labelled "Ad" or "#ad"; marketing email has consent and an unsubscribe.
- [ ] Job ads and HR texts avoid age-coded or other protected-characteristic wording.
- [ ] No dismissive remarks about the NHS, national identities or regional accents.
- [ ] If a British source text is adapted for other cultures, hidden criticism and understatement are made explicit.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (US-style enthusiasm):

> We're super excited to announce our game-changing, best-in-class platform! You'll absolutely love it!!

**After** (tailored for UK readers):

> We've launched a new version of our platform. Early users cut their reporting time by about a third, and we think you'll find it useful too. Have a look and let us know what you think.

**Why:** British readers discount hype, and "best-in-class" would need comparative proof under UK ad rules. A plain statement with a modest, evidence-backed claim is more credible and still warm.
