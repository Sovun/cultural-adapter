# Italy

## In brief

Italian business readers expect a courteous frame around every message: a proper salutation with the right title, the formal "Lei", one warm line, and a set closing formula. Without them, even correct content can come across as careless or rude. Inside that frame, modern Italian guidance asks for plain, short sentences rather than dense bureaucratic prose. Trust is personal, so writing often confirms what was agreed by phone or in person, and pressure tactics backfire. Consumer copy uses "tu" and must be available in Italian; advertising claims must be provable, and job ads may not filter by sex, age or family status.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous, clear, measured. Modern Italian style favours everyday verbs ("vedere" over "fare riferimento a", "per" over "affinché") and short sentences; long chains of subordinate clauses read as "burocratese".
- Do not overcorrect into old-style deference. "Egregissimo", "Esimio", "Pregiatissimo" or a capital on every courtesy word now sound stiff or even ironic.
- Polish counts: typos, wrong titles or machine-like translation hurt the sender's image ("fare bella figura" matters). Rewrite English idioms for intent. Write first business contacts in Italian; many firms take an English pitch less seriously.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, surname. "Andrea", "Luca", "Nicola" and "Simone" are male names; check before writing "Gentile Sig.ra".
- Titles are used: Dott./Dott.ssa for any university graduate, Ing. (engineer), Avv. (lawyer), Prof./Prof.ssa. Use the title in the reader's signature; leaving it out can read as a slight.
- Salutation adjectives: "Gentile" is the safe choice for anyone ("Gentile Dott.ssa Bianchi,"); "Egregio/Egregia" is more formal or institutional. "Spett.le" (spettabile) is for companies and offices only, never a person. "Caro/Cara", "Ciao" and "Salve" are too familiar for a first contact; "Buongiorno Ing. Rossi," works in ongoing threads.
- The salutation ends with a comma; the body starts on a new line in lowercase.
- "Lei" for one reader, "Voi" for a company or group ("restiamo in attesa di un Vostro riscontro"). Capitalised forms (La ringrazio, Sua) are permitted but optional; pick one style and keep it.
- Address is often asymmetric: a senior may write "tu" and still expect "Lei" back. Mirror only when invited.
- Use "Sig.ra" (signora) for all women; "signorina" sounds dated. For feminine job titles (avvocata, sindaca), use the form the person uses herself.
- Sign-offs: "Cordiali saluti" (standard), "Distinti saluti" (more distant), "Un cordiale saluto" (warmer), "A presto" (established contacts). A typical pre-close: "In attesa di un Suo cortese riscontro, La ringrazio e Le porgo cordiali saluti."

## Structuring a message

1. "Oggetto" (subject) of five or six words naming the topic.
2. Salutation with title, then one courtesy line ("La ringrazio per l'incontro di ieri").
3. Reason for writing ("Le scrivo in merito a...").
4. Details in short paragraphs or a bullet list; attachments marked "All.".
5. A clear request or next step with a date, an offer to call, thanks, closing formula.

- Formal letters carry place and date ("Milano, 12 marzo 2026"), references ("Vs. rif.", "Ns. rif.") and the partita IVA in the footer.
- Settle important points by phone or meeting, then send a short written recap; agendas are loose and points get reopened.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Lively disagreement about ideas is normal and not hostile. A sharply worded reply often cools quickly; do not read it as the end of the relationship.
- Refusals and requests to outsiders use the conditional ("Vorrei chiederLe se fosse possibile...") and give a reason. The decision itself should still be unmistakable.
- Criticise privately; never correct someone with colleagues in copy. Explain why a rule applies rather than just citing it.
- Avoid ultimatums, "last chance" offers and pushing for a fast answer; a new relationship moves at the reader's speed. Skip negative remarks about Italy or its politics.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade through the person and references: a shared contact, known clients, long presence in the market, after-sales support. Quality, craftsmanship, design and origin are strong themes.
- Consumer information must be available at least in Italian, with Italian at least as visible as any other language. Consumer prices are final prices including VAT (IVA).
- Self-regulation and consumer law require provable claims; exaggeration is allowed only when obviously non-literal. Comparative ads must compare relevant, verifiable features without discrediting the rival. Green claims need specific evidence. Fake urgency or scarcity counts as an unfair practice.
- Influencer posts need an upfront label such as "Pubblicità", "Sponsorizzato da [brand]" or "Prodotto inviato da [brand]"; a buried "#ad" is not enough. Ads must not urge children to buy.
- CV: one or two pages, reverse chronological, sober layout. Photo, date of birth and nationality are common but not required; leave out marital status. Give the Italian degree name and final grade (110/110) and CEFR language levels. Many recruiters still expect a closing data-processing line, though privacy law no longer requires it. Europass suits public competitions but looks generic elsewhere.
- Job ads: no sex requirement (write both forms or "(m/f)"), no reference to marital status, pregnancy or family, no age limits ("max 30 anni"), no questions on religion, politics, union membership or health. Since June 2026 pay-transparency rules require the starting pay or range in the ad or before the interview and ban asking about previous pay; check the current details.

## Humor and emphasis

- Irony and teasing are common in conversation but belong to established relationships, not to a first written contact.
- Emphasise with italics or bold, not quotation marks or capitals.
- Stereotypes (mafia, mandolins, "mamma mia", spaghetti jokes) irritate readers.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Religion:** the state is secular, most people identify as Catholic, few practise weekly. Ads that mock Catholic rites have been pulled by the self-regulator; the advertising code protects religious convictions generally.
- **Politics, Fascism, World War II:** keep out of business text. Praising Fascism and displaying fascist symbols can be criminal offences.
- **Regions and migration:** North-South stereotypes are touchy; caricature neither side. Migration is politically charged; do not assume the reader's view.
- **Dignity:** advertising must not demean or sexualise people or rely on domestic gender roles. Write "persona con disabilità", not "handicappato" or "diversamente abile".
- **Defamation:** publishing an attack on a person or company on social media is an aggravated offence; keep complaints and reviews factual.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- 17 is the unlucky number, and Friday the 17th the unlucky date; avoid it in promotions ("17 reasons").
- Chrysanthemums are funeral flowers. Flowers are given in odd numbers; even numbers are associated with mourning.
- Purple is unlucky in theatre contexts; black means mourning. Hand gestures carry specific meanings; check gesture imagery with a native reader.
- National day is 2 June; 4 October (St Francis) is a national holiday from 2026. Cities also close for their patron saint.

## Variation within the culture

- **North and South:** business in Milan, Turin, the North-East and Emilia tends to move faster, run closer to the clock and accept a quicker route to the point. In the South and much of the centre, expect more relationship-building, more phone contact and more flexible timing; the respectful singular "Voi" still survives with older people. The gap is narrowing and many firms are national or international, so treat this as a tendency and never as a stereotype in the text itself.
- **Language minorities:** German is co-official in South Tyrol (Alto Adige/Südtirol) and French in Valle d'Aosta; consumer material there often needs a bilingual version. Slovene, Friulian, Ladin and Sardinian are recognised regional languages.
- **Sectors:** banks, insurers, law, public administration and family-owned SMEs keep "Lei", titles and formal letters; tech, startups, retail and youth brands use "tu". Decisions in family firms often rest with one owner and take time. Younger professionals write shorter, more digital messages; older readers expect formal address and value a call.
- **Calendar:** August (around Ferragosto, 15 August) and late December are near-closed periods.
- **Swiss Italian (Ticino):** shares the language but uses Swiss formats (CHF, apostrophe thousands separator); do not reuse it-IT prices or legal copy unchanged.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The email has an "Oggetto", a salutation with title, and a closing formula; none of them is missing.
- [ ] "Gentile" or "Egregio/a" for people, "Spett.le" only for companies; no "Caro", "Ciao" or "Salve" in a first contact.
- [ ] The reader's title (Dott., Ing., Avv., Prof.) is used and spelled consistently.
- [ ] "Lei" (or "Voi" for a company) throughout B2B text; "tu" only in consumer copy or when invited. Capitalisation of courtesy pronouns is consistent.
- [ ] One courtesy line, then the reason for writing; the request has a date and an offer to talk.
- [ ] Refusals give a reason and are unambiguous; criticism is private, no colleagues in copy.
- [ ] No ultimatums, "last chance" wording, fake urgency or fake scarcity.
- [ ] Sentences are short and plain; no bureaucratic chains and no archaic super-deference.
- [ ] Claims are backed by evidence; comparisons are verifiable and do not discredit competitors.
- [ ] Consumer copy is in Italian, prices include IVA and read "1.234,56 €".
- [ ] Influencer content carries an upfront "Pubblicità" or "Sponsorizzato da" label.
- [ ] Job ads have no sex, age, family or appearance requirements, address both sexes, and state pay or a pay range.
- [ ] No mafia, pizza or North-South clichés; no jokes about Catholic rites, Fascism or migration.
- [ ] Dates DD/MM/YYYY, lowercase months and weekdays, sentence-case headings; no 17 in promotions; no deadlines in August.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (terse international style):

> Hi Marco, we need the signed contract back by Friday, otherwise the project start slips. Thanks.

**After** (to a new client contact, an engineer, after a first meeting, in Italian with gloss):

> Oggetto: Contratto di fornitura - firma entro venerdì 23 ottobre
>
> Gentile Ing. Ferri,
>
> La ringrazio ancora per l'incontro di martedì a Bologna. Come concordato, Le invio in allegato il contratto con le modifiche al punto 4.
>
> Per avviare il progetto lunedì 2 novembre, avremmo bisogno della copia firmata entro venerdì 23 ottobre. Se ci fossero ancora aspetti da chiarire, sarei lieto di sentirLa per telefono in settimana.
>
> In attesa di un Suo cortese riscontro, Le porgo cordiali saluti.

*(Gloss: "Subject: Supply contract - signature by Friday 23 October. Dear Mr Ferri (Engineer), thank you again for Tuesday's meeting in Bologna. As agreed, I attach the contract with the changes to clause 4. To start the project on Monday 2 November, we would need the signed copy by Friday 23 October. If anything still needs clarifying, I would be glad to speak by phone this week. Awaiting your kind reply, kind regards.")*

**Why:** The deadline stays firm and dated, but it now comes with a reason instead of a threat. The title "Ing.", "Lei", the courtesy line and the set closing make it read as a proper business letter, and the offer of a call keeps the relationship personal. With a long-standing contact who already writes "tu", the same message could open with "Ciao Marco" and lose the formal pre-close.
