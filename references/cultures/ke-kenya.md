# Kenya

## In brief

Kenyan business is written in English, polite and fairly formal: titles and surnames by default, a greeting and a wellbeing line before the point, and refusals or criticism wrapped with care. Kiswahili is the national language and appears in mass-market and public communication; consumer ads freely mix English, Kiswahili and the Nairobi youth slang Sheng. Readers value calm, courteous wording, so anger, pressure and hype read badly. Do not reuse a Nigerian or generic "African" template: Kenyan written style is more cushioned and less exuberant, and Nairobi business meetings and deadlines are taken seriously. Ethnicity and politics are legally sensitive, and data-protection rules on direct marketing are strict.

## Voice and tone

- Courteous, friendly and composed. "Please", "thank you" and "kindly" (often used in Kenyan formal requests) are expected; leaving them out is noticed.
- British spelling and usage: "programme", "organisation", "colour", "cheque".
- Proofread carefully: grammar errors in professional text, and especially in CVs and cover letters, are judged.
- Keep frustration out of writing, even about delays. Patient, polite persistence works better than urgency or blame.
- Formal business, B2B and government text stays in standard English. Kiswahili lines or a Kiswahili version help in mass-market and community-facing copy.

## Addressing people

- Title plus surname: Mr, Mrs, Ms, Dr, Prof. Keep academic and professional titles; dropping "Dr" or "Prof." can offend. "Hon." is used for MPs and senior public officials; "Eng." also appears as a title.
- Let the Kenyan side move to first names. Many people carry an English or biblical given name plus African names; the surname usually comes last. Keep particles such as "wa", "arap" or "ole" as part of the name.
- "Dear Sir/Madam" is still common in formal letters to an unknown reader; a named person is better.
- In group emails, name or greet the most senior person first.
- In speech and warm local marketing, "Mzee" (respected elder), "Bwana" (mister) and "Mama" (respectful address to a woman, often older) signal respect; do not use them in formal English letters.
- Sign-offs: "Kind regards", "Best regards", "Yours faithfully" (unknown reader) or "Yours sincerely" (named reader). In Kiswahili: "Wasalaam" (regards), "Wako" (yours), "Asante" (thank you).

## Structuring a message

1. Salutation with title, then a wellbeing line ("I hope you are well"; in Kiswahili "Natumaini uko salama na unaendelea vizuri", I hope you are safe and doing well).
2. Purpose in the first or second paragraph ("I am writing to..."; in Kiswahili "Sababu ya kuandika barua hii ni...", the reason for this letter is...).
3. Reasons and background in short paragraphs; invite comments rather than presenting a closed decision.
4. A clear next step with a date, and an offer to call or meet.
5. Thanks and good wishes before the sign-off.

- Expect several rounds before a decision; senior people and other stakeholders are consulted. Do not push for a commitment in the first exchange.
- Important or sensitive matters go by phone or in person, then get confirmed in writing.
- In Kiswahili letters, "Baada ya salamu" (after greetings) moves from the courtesy part to business.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Give criticism privately, about the issue, with a positive frame: "Could we look again at the figures in section 3?" rather than "Section 3 is wrong."
- Never correct someone in a group thread or widen the CC list on a complaint.
- With seniors, phrase disagreement as a question or an alternative, and acknowledge their experience first.
- Read soft agreement carefully: "no problem", "we shall see" or silence may hide doubt. Ask open questions and confirm dates and amounts in writing.
- Refuse with a reason and, where possible, an alternative. Unsolicited critique or "solutions" may be taken badly; offer them as options.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with references, local presence, known partners, credentials and value for money. Community and family benefit, and social or environmental projects, fit corporate copy well.
- Consumer ads mix English and Kiswahili; Sheng signals youth and urban style and is used by telecoms, banks and insurers. Sheng changes fast and differs by neighbourhood: use it only with a local writer, and never in B2B, government or copy for older or rural readers.
- Consumer law bans misleading claims, including fake "was/now" discounts, unrealistic delivery or availability promises, invented endorsements and exaggerated referral rewards. Exploiting a reader's limited literacy or language is an unfair practice, so mass-market terms should be plain and, where needed, in Kiswahili.
- Broadcast ads must be honest and decent, must not open with "breaking news" or news-style framing, must not attack competitors unfairly and must not pressure children. Tobacco ads are banned on air; gambling ads run only late at night with the licence number shown. Alcohol ads have been treated as adult content.
- Direct marketing needs consent, a named sender and a free, visible opt-out in every message ("Reply STOP to opt out", an unsubscribe link). Sensitive data such as ethnicity, religion, health or marital status may never be used for marketing, and children may not be profiled.
- Payment copy usually covers M-Pesa: give the Paybill number and account format, or the Till number, using the standard wording "Lipa na M-PESA". Never ask for a PIN in any message.
- CV: English, reverse chronological, one to two pages, results quantified. Current recruiters advise leaving out photo, national ID number, marital status, religion and date of birth unless the advert asks; some banks still expect more. Never list an unfinished degree or professional exam as completed.
- Job ads must not exclude on race, sex, ethnic or social origin, religion, language, disability, pregnancy, HIV status, age, marital status or similar grounds; upper age limits have been struck down by the courts. Public bodies must reflect ethnic diversity, and many ads invite women and persons with disabilities to apply.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humour is common in conversation and youth ads, but keep it out of first contact and messages to seniors.
- No jokes about ethnic groups, religion or Kenya's problems; from an outsider they read as insults.
- Emphasis with bold, not capitals or doubled punctuation.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Ethnicity:** Kenya has over 40 communities. Hate speech or words that stir ethnic contempt are criminal offences under the cohesion law, and publishers can be liable too, so moderate user comments. Do not name communities in copy, imply a product suits one group, or comment on ethnic voting patterns.
- **Politics and elections:** the next general election is due in August 2027. Keep brands clear of party colours, slogans and political allusions; paid political content has its own disclosure rules.
- **Religion:** most Kenyans are Christian; Muslims are concentrated on the coast and in the north-east. Do not trivialise faith; respect Ramadan and modesty norms in coastal and north-eastern campaigns.
- **Image of the country:** avoid corruption generalisations, "poverty" framing, and any hint of buying influence or "connections". State compliance policies neutrally.
- **Identity:** many Kenyan Somalis are citizens; do not conflate them with refugees.
- **LGBTQ:** same-sex acts are criminalised under the Penal Code, and further restrictions have been proposed; check current status before any related content.
- **Personal data:** photos of identifiable people need consent for commercial use, and forms should not collect sensitive data without a lawful basis.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Show items given or received with the right hand or both hands. Avoid images of pointing at people.
- Business imagery: neat formal dress; modest clothing, especially for coastal and Muslim audiences.
- Safari, wildlife, Maasai and "Hakuna Matata" imagery reads as a tourist cliché to many Kenyan readers; show modern Kenya (Nairobi offices, mobile money, local brands) unless tourism is the topic.
- Show a mix of people rather than one community.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | DD/MM/YYYY (18/10/2026); long 18 October 2026. Never month first. |
| Time | Locale default 24-hour (14:30); 12-hour "2:30 pm" is common in everyday text. EAT, UTC+3. Swahili time counts from about 6:00, so "saa moja" is 7 o'clock: give clock digits. |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89 |
| Currency | Kenyan shilling, KES. "KES 1,500" in formal or international text; "Ksh 1,500" (also "KSh") in consumer copy. Cents rarely shown. |
| Week starts | Locale data: Sunday; the working week is Monday to Friday, and business calendars often start Monday. |
| Phone | +254 712 123456 (local 0712 123456; 0712 345 678 also seen). Mobile numbers have 9 digits after the 0 and start with 7 or 1. |
| Address | P.O. Box 1234 - NAKURU / 20100 / NAKURU / KENYA. Five-digit postcode; 00100 is Nairobi GPO. One-line "P.O. Box 12345-00100, Nairobi" is common on letterheads. |
| Other | British spelling. Metric. Working hours about 8:00-17:00. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Nairobi vs elsewhere:** Nairobi's private sector is fast and punctual; Mombasa, the coast, small towns and rural areas run slower and expect more traditional deference.
- **Languages:** most people speak a mother tongue, Kiswahili and English, and language choice signals education and setting. English for business, Kiswahili for national mass communication, Sheng for urban youth.
- **Sectors:** banks, law, government and NGOs keep formal titles and traditional formats; tech and startups use first names, lean CVs and faster replies. The public sector is more top-down.
- **Generations:** younger urban readers are more direct and less focused on ethnicity; older readers expect titles and greetings.
- **Religion and region:** coastal and north-eastern areas are largely Muslim; elsewhere mostly Christian.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (terse, written with a direct Northern European default):

> Hi James, the invoice you sent has the wrong amount again. Fix it and resend today, otherwise payment will be delayed.

**After** (for a Kenyan supplier contact):

> Dear Mr Kamau,
>
> I hope you are well, and thank you for the quick delivery last week. Kindly have a look at invoice 4471: the total shows KES 184,000, while our order confirmation of 2 October was KES 148,000. Could your team please confirm the correct figure and share a revised invoice by Friday 23 October? That way we can include it in this month's payment run. I am happy to call if it is easier to go through it together.
>
> Kind regards,

**Why:** The title, wellbeing line and thanks set a respectful tone, and "Kindly" with a question softens the request. The problem is stated as a fact about the document, not the person, and "again" is dropped. Exact amounts, the order date and a deadline with a reason keep it clear, and the offer to call leaves room to sort it out without losing face.
