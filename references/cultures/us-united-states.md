# United States

## In brief

American professional readers expect the point early, a confident and upbeat voice, and claims backed by numbers. Self-promotion is normal and modesty can read as weakness. The main traps are not tone but content: politics, race, religion, guns and other polarized subjects can derail any text, and US English conventions (dates, spelling, units) differ from other English variants.

## Voice and tone

- Optimistic, solution-focused and confident. Problems are usually framed together with a proposed fix.
- Plain English is valued in business and government writing. Short sentences, active voice and concrete verbs.
- Enthusiasm is expected in marketing, recruiting and internal announcements, but heavy hype in B2B or technical writing reduces trust.
- Swearing is out of place in professional text even when the culture is casual.

## Addressing people

- Name order: given name, optional middle name, family name. Middle names rarely appear in address.
- Default salutation: "Hi Jordan," for most business email; "Dear Dr. Lee," for a more formal email. Formal letters and cover letters use a colon ("Dear Dr. Lee:") and close with "Sincerely,".
- Use "Ms." for women unless you know the person prefers "Mrs." or "Miss". Avoid gendered salutations when gender is unknown: use the full name ("Dear Jordan Lee") or a role ("Dear Hiring Team").
- Respect stated pronouns in email signatures.
- "Sir" and "ma'am" are polite in speech in the South and the military but rare in writing.
- Sign-offs: "Best," "Thanks," "Best regards,". "Cheers" reads as British or Australian.
- Hispanic names may carry two surnames (father's then mother's). Use the full form the person uses; the first surname alone is the common short form.

## Structuring a message

- Subject line states purpose and, when relevant, the action and date: "Approval needed by Fri Mar 6: Q2 budget".
- Opening line: the ask or the key point. Background follows.
- Use bullets and bold for scannability. Busy readers skim.
- Close with explicit next steps: who does what by when.
- Keep email short. A long email is often answered with "can we hop on a call?".

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Disagreement on ideas is acceptable and not taken personally, but keep it about the issue, not the person.
- Feedback commonly wraps one criticism in several positives. Pure criticism with no acknowledgement of strengths can feel harsh.
- Refusals are clear but softened with appreciation and an alternative: "Thanks for thinking of us. We can't take this on this quarter, but we could revisit in July."
- Decode American positivity: "great" and "awesome" carry less weight than their face value, and a point wrapped in praise or marked "a bit" or "maybe" is often the real message.
- Politeness markers that foreigners take literally: "That's an interesting idea" can be a polite no; "we should get coffee sometime" is often not an invitation.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Benefit-led, second-person copy ("You'll save 3 hours a week") works well.
- Quantified claims, case studies and testimonials are expected. Superlatives ("best", "#1") need substantiation: the Federal Trade Commission treats unsupported objective claims as deceptive advertising.
- Comparative advertising naming competitors is legal and common if truthful.
- Endorsements and influencer posts must disclose any paid or material connection clearly ("Ad", "Sponsored"); vague tags such as "#sp" are not enough. Fake or bought reviews and testimonials are banned under federal consumer-protection rules.
- An unqualified "Made in USA" claim means all or virtually all of the product is made in the US.
- CVs/résumés: one to two pages, no photo, no date of birth, marital status, religion or nationality. Anti-discrimination rules mean recruiters often discard résumés with these details. Lead with achievements, use action verbs and metrics.
- Cover letters are direct about fit and motivation: why you, why this role, why now.

## Humor and emphasis

- Humor is direct and often exaggerated rather than ironic. Dry understatement and sarcasm are more likely to be misread than in the UK.
- Light humor helps in internal and social content. Avoid it in apologies, incident reports and anything involving money owed.
- Exclamation marks are tolerated in friendly business email (one is fine, several look juvenile).
- Sports metaphors (baseball and American football especially: "touch base", "ballpark", "hail mary") are common in US text but confuse international readers. Keep them only for a purely US audience.

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- Highly polarized: abortion, guns, immigration, race relations, policing, LGBTQ+ rights, religion in public life, the current administration and elections. Do not take sides in content that is not explicitly about these subjects.
- Race and ethnicity: use current, specific terms (Black, African American, Latino/Hispanic, Asian American, Native American). Avoid outdated or offensive terms such as "Oriental". Do not use or imitate AAVE if the writer is not part of that community.
- Criticism of the US by outsiders is received poorly even when Americans criticize their own country.
- Employment texts must avoid language implying preference by age, sex, race, color, religion, national origin, disability or pregnancy ("young and energetic team", "digital native", "recent graduates only", "native English speaker"; write "fluent English" only if the job needs it). An equal-opportunity statement at the end of job ads is customary and required for federal contractors.
- Pay transparency: there is no federal rule, but a growing number of states and cities (about 15 states plus DC at last check, including New York and California) require a good-faith salary range in job postings. Check the rules where the job is based.
- Accessibility (ADA): alt text, sufficient contrast, captions, labelled form fields, no meaning conveyed by color alone; web accessibility claims are a frequent lawsuit target. Plain language is required separately for federal agency writing.
- Commercial email (CAN-SPAM): accurate sender and subject line, identification as an ad, a physical postal address and a working opt-out honored within 10 business days. The US uses opt-out, not opt-in, for most marketing email.
- Health, financial and environmental claims attract regulatory scrutiny (FTC, FDA, SEC).

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- National symbols (flag, eagle) are used freely in marketing but treat the flag respectfully. Flags and patriotic cues can imply US manufacture, which then has to be true.
- Confederate flags and imagery are highly divisive. Swastikas and other hate symbols are taboo; the "OK" hand gesture has been appropriated by some extremist groups, so avoid it in political contexts.
- The middle finger gesture is offensive. "Thumbs up" is positive.
- Imagery is expected to reflect diversity in age, ethnicity, ability and body type. An all-white or all-male stock photo set is noticed.
- Number 13 is mildly unlucky (some buildings skip the 13th floor).

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | MM/DD/YYYY (3/7/2026). Long form: March 7, 2026. Spell out the month in international text to avoid ambiguity. |
| Time | 12-hour clock with AM/PM (2:30 PM). Always give a time zone (ET, CT, MT, PT) for cross-region scheduling. |
| Numbers | 1,234,567.89 |
| Currency | $1,234.50; use "USD" or "US$" in international text. |
| Week starts | Sunday in calendars. |
| Units | Imperial (miles, feet, pounds, Fahrenheit, US gallons). Add metric in parentheses for international audiences. |
| Spelling | US spelling: color, organize, center, license (noun and verb). |
| Phone | +1 (555) 123-4567 |
| Address | Street number before street name; state abbreviation and ZIP code on the last line. |

## Variation within the culture

- The Northeast is fast and blunt; the South is the most formal (titles, "sir" and "ma'am") and its politeness warmer and more indirect; the West Coast is the most casual; the Midwest is friendly and conflict-averse.
- Tech, startups and media are the most casual; finance, law, government and healthcare more formal.
- About 13% of people speak Spanish at home. For consumer content, Spanish versions or bilingual copy may be expected, especially in California, Texas, Florida and New York.
- Political identity strongly shapes reception of value-laden messaging (sustainability, diversity, patriotism).

## Review checklist

- [ ] The ask or conclusion appears in the subject line or first two sentences.
- [ ] Next steps have an owner and a specific date with time zone.
- [ ] Achievements carry measurable outcomes; résumé bullets start with action verbs, bios and cover letters use first person.
- [ ] Superlative or objective claims ("fastest", "clinically proven") are substantiated or softened.
- [ ] No content takes a side on polarized issues unless that is the purpose of the text.
- [ ] Race, ethnicity and identity terms are current and specific.
- [ ] Job ads and HR text contain no age, gender, family-status or national-origin preferences (including "native speaker"), and include a salary range where state or city law requires it.
- [ ] Paid endorsements are disclosed clearly; marketing email has a postal address and a working unsubscribe.
- [ ] CVs contain no photo, date of birth, marital status or religion.
- [ ] Dates are unambiguous for the reader (month name preferred in international text; MM/DD/YYYY for US-only text).
- [ ] US spelling and units are used, or metric is added for global readers.
- [ ] Criticism of the US or Americans is not made casually by an outsider.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (written with a modest, context-first style):

> Our team has been working on this area for some time and we have gained some experience. We would be grateful if you might consider our proposal, which we believe could possibly be of some interest to your company.

**After** (tailored for US readers):

> We can cut your onboarding time by 30%. We did it for three SaaS companies last year, including Acme, which went from 14 to 9 days. Can we set up a 20-minute call next week to walk you through the approach?

**Why:** US readers expect the benefit first, quantified proof and a concrete next step. Excessive hedging reads as low confidence.
