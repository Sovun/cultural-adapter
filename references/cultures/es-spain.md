# Spain

## In brief

Spanish professional readers expect a warm, personal tone and plain, concise writing. Trust grows through personal contact, so an email usually prepares or confirms a call or meeting rather than closing a deal alone. Register relaxes fast: "usted" opens a cold contact, "tú" often follows within a reply or two, and consumer copy uses "tú" by default. Spain is not Latin America: vocabulary, the plural "vosotros", number formats and a somewhat more direct style all differ from Mexican or neutral Spanish. Regional identities and languages (Catalonia, the Basque Country, Galicia) are sensitive, and advertising law is strict on stereotypes and price display.

## Voice and tone

- Friendly, clear, conversational. Outside legal and official text, short sentences and everyday verbs ("pedir" rather than "solicitar") read better than bureaucratic phrasing.
- Use Spain Spanish (es-ES): "ordenador", "móvil", "vale", "vosotros". Latin American forms ("computadora", "celular", informal "ustedes") stand out at once.
- Word-for-word translation from English reads stiff. Rewrite idioms and watch false friends: "excitado" is sexual; use "ilusionado" or "con ganas".
- No emoji, all capitals or doubled "!!" in professional email; use bold to highlight.
- Plain requests ("envíame el informe") are normal among colleagues; with a new client, soften ("¿Sería posible...?", "Le agradecería...").

## Addressing people

- Names: given name(s), then two surnames, usually the father's first and the mother's second (Laura Martín Ruiz). Use the first surname (Sra. Martín), never the second alone. Compound given names (María José) are used in full.
- Formal: "Estimado Sr. García:" / "Estimada Laura:". A colon follows the salutation; a comma is an anglicism, though common in casual mail, so do not "correct" a native writer.
- Neutral: "Hola, Laura:" is acceptable once there is any contact. Warm: "Querido/a Javier:". Unknown reader: "A quien corresponda:" (impersonal; find a name if you can).
- "Señora" for all adult women; "señorita" sounds dated. "Don/Doña" + first name survives in protocol, legal text and with elderly customers. "Licenciado" is not a form of address in Spain.
- Sign-offs: "Atentamente" (formal), "Un cordial saludo" / "Saludos cordiales" (standard), "Un saludo" (everyday), "Un abrazo" (established). Never "Besos" in business.
- Keep one register: mixing "le escribo" (usted) and "te envío" (tú) is a common error.

## Structuring a message

1. A short subject line naming the topic.
2. Salutation and one courtesy line ("Espero que todo vaya bien", "Gracias por su mensaje").
3. Purpose early ("Le escribo para..."), then reasons in short paragraphs; detail goes in attachments.
4. A next step with owner and date, plus an offer to talk ("Si le parece, lo comentamos por teléfono").
5. "Quedo a su disposición para cualquier duda" and the sign-off.

- Decisions happen by phone or in person, then get confirmed in writing. Headings use sentence case; months, days and nationality adjectives are lowercase (lunes, octubre, español).
- Employees have a legal right to digital disconnection; do not demand replies in the evening, at weekends or on holiday.

## Disagreeing, refusing, giving feedback

- Peers can disagree plainly once there is trust. Toward outsiders and seniors, give a reason and leave room to negotiate.
- Criticism is private: no corrections in group threads, no widening the CC list on a complaint.
- Describe the issue, not the person: "Aún no hemos recibido el archivo" rather than "No nos ha enviado el archivo".
- Decode soft signals: a first "sí" may mean "we will study it", and "es complicado" can be a no. Ask for a concrete next step.
- An ultimatum or a push for a fast answer usually hurts the deal.
- When adapting Mexican or Latin American text for Spain, trim deferential formulas; long cushioning can read as evasive.

## Persuasion, marketing and self-presentation

- Persuade with references, known clients, local presence, after-sales support, guarantees and clear prices. A credible, likeable sender matters.
- Sales and technical material should be in Spanish. Ads often use humour and emotion. Advertising law bans ads that degrade dignity, use a woman's body as decoration, or promote sexist, racist, body-image, homophobic or disability stereotypes, and restricts ads aimed at children.
- Comparative ads must compare like with like on verifiable features, without denigration. Consumer prices must include VAT ("29,90 € IVA incluido", not "+ IVA"); labels must be at least in Spanish.
- Promotional email needs prior consent (except similar products for existing customers) and an easy opt-out.
- CV: one to two pages, reverse chronological, tailored to the offer; CEFR levels for languages (B2, C1); a short tailored cover letter. A photo is common but optional and cannot be required; date of birth and marital status are optional and declining.
- Job ads must not exclude by age, sex, origin, disability, beliefs, sexual orientation or other protected grounds; health questions are banned. Avoid "menor de 35 años", "buena presencia", "nativo" (write "nivel C2"). Pay is quoted gross per year ("bruto anual"); EU salary-transparency rules are being transposed, so check their current status.
- Inclusive forms with "@", "x" or "e" are rejected by the language academy and split readers; collective nouns ("el personal", "la plantilla") are the least contested.

## Humor and emphasis

- Warm, situational humour helps in relationship messages; localise jokes from Latin America. Irony or teasing aimed at the reader does not belong in business text.
- Opening "¿" and "¡" are mandatory. Missing accents or "ñ" look careless and can change meaning (año/ano).

## Sensitive topics and legal red lines

- **Catalonia and the Basque Country:** independence remains divisive (the 2017 Catalan referendum was ruled illegal). Take no side, make no jokes, and do not call these regions countries or list them beside countries.
- **Regional languages:** Catalan (Valencian in Valencia), Basque and Galician are co-official in their regions. In Catalonia consumers may be served in Catalan or Spanish and receive essential product information in Catalan; firms have been fined over labels without Catalan.
- **Franco and the Civil War:** exalting the dictatorship in a way that humiliates victims is an administrative offence, and Francoist symbols are being removed. Keep such imagery and nostalgia out; heavy patriotic imagery can read as political.
- **ETA:** the Basque armed group dissolved in 2018 but its violence left deep wounds; never trivialise it.
- Bullfighting divides opinion; Gibraltar wording can offend either side. Jokes about laziness, "mañana" or siestas insult.
- Legal items here are pointers, not legal advice.

## Symbols, numbers, colors and imagery

- Chrysanthemums are funeral flowers. Tuesday the 13th, not Friday, is the traditional unlucky date.
- Flamenco, bulls and siesta imagery are clichés, mostly Andalusian; sombreros are Mexican, not Spanish.
- Regional flags (the Catalan "estelada" above all) signal political positions; do not mix national and regional flags casually.

## Formats and conventions

| Item | Convention |
|---|---|
| Date | DD/MM/YYYY (07/03/2026); short 7/3/26. Long: 7 de marzo de 2026 (lowercase month). |
| Time | 24-hour: 14:30 or 14:30 h. 12-hour forms are rare in writing. |
| Numbers | 1.234.567,89. Four-digit numbers are not grouped (2500), years never. The language academy prefers a thin space (12 500) in formal publishing. |
| Currency | 1.234,50 € (symbol after amount, with a space). |
| Percent | 25 % (space before the sign). |
| Week starts | Monday; ISO week numbers. |
| Phone | +34 912 34 56 78 (nine digits; 912 345 678 also seen). Mobiles start with 6 or 7. |
| Address | Calle Mayor, 12, 3.º B / 28013 Madrid. Five-digit postcode (first two digits = province) before the town. |
| Punctuation | ¿? and ¡! in pairs; colon after the salutation; « » standard, " " widely used. |
| Other | Metric. VAT 21 % general rate; the Canary Islands, Ceuta and Melilla differ. |

## Variation within the culture

- **Catalonia, Valencia, Balearics:** Catalan is a working business language, most of all in Catalonia. Consumer campaigns there often need a Catalan version; a line in Catalan reads as courtesy. All-Spain campaigns default to Spanish.
- **Basque Country and Navarre:** Basque is co-official and place names are often bilingual (Vitoria-Gasteiz, Donostia / San Sebastián); use the official regional form.
- **Galicia:** Galician is co-official and used by local government and media.
- **Canary Islands:** "ustedes" replaces "vosotros" in every register; the islands are one hour behind the mainland.
- **Sectors and age:** banking, law, insurance and public administration keep "usted"; tech, startups and multinationals in Madrid and Barcelona use "tú" and tighter schedules. Many older readers expect "usted" and Spanish-language materials.
- **Calendar:** split working days with lunch around 14:00-16:00 are common, with shorter summer hours. Replies slow in August, Holy Week, Christmas and "puente" bridge days.

## Rewrite example

**Before** (terse Northern European style):

> Your offer is too expensive. We need a revised price by Monday or we will go with another supplier.

**After** (for a Spanish contact after a first meeting, in Spanish with gloss):

> Hola, Javier:
>
> Gracias por la propuesta y por la reunión del martes. La hemos revisado con el equipo y, tal como está, el precio queda por encima de lo que podemos asumir este año. ¿Podríais revisarlo? Nos ayudaría mucho tener una nueva cifra antes del lunes 9, porque el comité decide esa semana. Si te parece, lo hablamos el jueves por teléfono.
>
> Un saludo,

*(Gloss: "Hi Javier, thank you for the proposal and for Tuesday's meeting. We reviewed it with the team and, as it stands, the price is above what we can take on this year. Could you (your company) revise it? A new figure before Monday the 9th would really help, because the committee decides that week. If it suits you, let's talk on Thursday by phone. Regards,")*

**Why:** The price problem stays clear, but the ultimatum becomes a reason and a dated request, and the call keeps the relationship going. "Tú" for Javier and "vosotros" for his company are natural in Spain after a first meeting; a Mexican version would add a title, "usted" and more cushioning.
